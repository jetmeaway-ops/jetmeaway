## What ATOL Actually Covers

ATOL stands for Air Travel Organisers' Licensing. It's managed by the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) — whose official [ATOL protection scheme pages](https://www.caa.co.uk/atol-protection/) set out exactly what is and isn't covered — and exists for one specific, narrow purpose: to guarantee that if a licensed UK travel organiser collapses financially, customers get their money back, or — if they're already abroad when it happens — get flown home and looked after until they can travel back.

**The safety net.** If an ATOL holder goes bust, the scheme (funded by the ATOL Protection Contribution, a small per-person levy — currently £2.50 — added to every protected booking) steps in to either refund customers who haven't yet travelled, or arrange and fund repatriation and reasonable additional costs for those stranded overseas. This is genuinely valuable protection, and it's one of the reasons the UK package holiday market has a stronger consumer-safety reputation than many comparable markets.

**The catch, and it's the whole point of this article: ATOL only applies when you buy a flight-inclusive holiday bundle — for example flight + hotel, or flight + car hire — from a single travel firm, in a single transaction, that meets the legal definition of a "package."** It is not a general insurance policy against anything going wrong with air travel. It doesn't cover delays. It doesn't cover a disappointing hotel. It doesn't cover you simply because you bought a plane ticket. It is, specifically and only, an insolvency-protection scheme for package holidays.

That narrowness is exactly where the confusion starts, because most UK travellers' mental model of "booking a holiday" was formed in an era when packages were the default way to travel abroad. In 2026, DIY booking — searching flights and hotels separately, often on different sites, sometimes with different providers for each night of a multi-stop trip — is how a large and growing share of UK travellers actually build their trips. The legal protection framework hasn't caught up with that shift in the public's understanding, even though the framework itself is actually quite clear once you see it laid out.

## Why Direct Bookings Fall Outside ATOL

When you cut out the middleman and build a "Do-It-Yourself" holiday — comparing prices, then booking each component directly and separately — the legal framework changes completely. Here's exactly why, component by component.

### 1. Booking a flight directly with an airline (the e-ticket rule)

If you use [JetMeAway Flights](/flights) to compare routes and then go straight to an airline like British Airways, easyJet, Ryanair or a long-haul carrier to buy a seat, **you are not ATOL protected on that flight — and that's true whether it's a budget carrier or a full-service national airline.** Under UK law, scheduled airlines selling their own seats directly are entirely exempt from the ATOL scheme. This isn't a loophole or an oversight; it's a deliberate design feature of the regulatory framework, because a scheduled airline selling you a seat directly is treated as the primary transport provider, not a travel organiser assembling a bundle.

Instead of ATOL protection, you're issued a direct e-ticket the moment you pay. That e-ticket is a legally binding, direct contract between you and the airline — no intermediary sits between you and the party that owes you a service. Because of this direct contractual relationship, **the airline itself carries direct legal and financial liability for your flight.** If they cancel, delay you significantly, or change your schedule materially, they're obligated under UK261 passenger rights law (the UK's retained version of the EU's EU261 regulation, applying to flights departing the UK, and to flights arriving in the UK operated by a UK or EU carrier) to refund you, rebook you, or in many cases pay you fixed compensation on top. You don't need ATOL here because the airline itself holds the liability — ATOL exists to cover situations where nobody would otherwise hold that liability, which isn't the case with a direct airline booking.

The one thing UK261 genuinely does not cover is the airline going bankrupt. If the airline itself ceases trading, UK261's operational protections (rebooking, compensation, care) become meaningless because there's no functioning airline left to enforce them against — that's the specific gap that Section 75 and SAFI travel insurance exist to fill, covered in detail further down this guide.

### 2. Booking a standalone hotel room

If you use [JetMeAway Hotels](/hotels) to secure your room directly with the hotel or through a hotel booking platform, **ATOL is entirely non-existent here — there was never a scheme to lose.** Accommodation-only transactions never carry ATOL certificates because there's no air travel component in the booking at all; ATOL is specifically an *air* travel organiser's licensing scheme. A five-night stay at a five-star resort, booked on its own, carries exactly the same (zero) ATOL protection as a one-night stay at a budget city hotel. The price or luxury tier of the room makes no difference to the legal position.

This surprises some travellers who assume "expensive equals protected" — it doesn't. What protects a standalone hotel booking is the hotel's own cancellation policy, your payment method (Section 75 if paid by UK credit card and the booking value sits between £100 and £30,000), and, if you've bought one, a travel insurance policy with the right add-ons.

### 3. Booking a flight and a hotel separately, even on the same day

This is where the myth causes the most real-world confusion. **Booking a flight on one site and a hotel on a completely separate site — even within the same hour, even for the exact same trip — does not create a package, and does not trigger ATOL, no matter how "combined" it feels from your side of the screen.** The legal test isn't about your intent or your mental trip-budget; it's about the structure of the transactions. Two separate contracts, with two separate providers, paid separately, with no shared checkout and no data-sharing arrangement between the two sites, are exactly that: two separate, unprotected-by-ATOL transactions.

Where it gets genuinely subtle is the "Linked Travel Arrangement" (LTA) category introduced by the Package Travel Regulations 2018 — covered in full in the next section — which can apply in a narrower set of circumstances even without a fully combined package. But the default, and the situation the vast majority of DIY UK travellers are actually in when they use comparison tools like JetMeAway, is straightforward standalone booking with no ATOL and no LTA protection either.

## The Package Travel Regulations 2018, Explained

Almost everything about when ATOL does and doesn't apply flows from one piece of underlying law: the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018 (the "PTRs"), which replaced the older 1992 Package Travel Regulations and updated UK consumer protection for the way people actually book trips today — including through comparison sites, apps, and multi-provider checkouts.

### What legally makes something a "package"

Under the PTRs, a **package** exists when a trader combines at least two different types of travel service — for example a flight and accommodation, or accommodation and car hire, or a flight and an excursion — for the same trip, and any one of the following is true:

- The services are sold or charged at an **inclusive or total price**.
- The contract is **concluded under a single name** — booked as one product, even if the price is itemised.
- The services are **advertised or sold as a "package"** or similar term.
- The services are **combined after the customer selects and pays**, for example through a linked booking process where you pick a flight, then are immediately offered a hotel to add to the same transaction, and the two are combined into a single contract at checkout.
- Personal data is **passed between traders** as part of a booking made through one point of sale, and the second service is booked within 24 hours of the first, in a way that goes beyond a simple linked arrangement.

If your booking meets this definition, the organiser must, by law, hold appropriate insolvency protection — for a flight-inclusive package, that's typically ATOL; for a non-flight package (for example a coach or rail-based holiday), it's usually an alternative bonding scheme like ABTA's or a trust-account arrangement.

### What does NOT make something a package

Critically for DIY UK travellers, the PTRs also make clear what falls **outside** the definition — and this is the exact territory standalone flight and hotel comparison searches sit in:

- Booking a flight directly with an airline, then separately and independently booking a hotel with a different, unconnected provider, with no combined checkout, no shared total price, and no data-passing arrangement between the two.
- Using a comparison site (like JetMeAway) to research and compare prices across providers, then completing each booking directly with the individual airline, hotel or OTA — the comparison step itself doesn't combine anything into a package; only a genuinely linked, combined checkout would.
- Booking two or more separate hotel stays for a multi-city trip, each with a different, independent hotel or OTA, with no shared transaction.
- Adding a genuinely optional extra (like travel insurance from a different provider) to a flight booking, where the insurance isn't bundled into a combined travel-service price with the flight itself.

### Linked Travel Arrangements (LTAs) — the middle category

The 2018 Regulations also created a lighter, intermediate category: the **Linked Travel Arrangement**. An LTA can exist when a trader facilitates you booking a second travel service (say, a hotel) *after* you've already booked a first one (say, a flight) with them, within 24 hours, through a separate booking process, but without combining them into one total price or one contract. LTAs get some financial protection — specifically for money you've paid to that particular trader — but it's narrower than full package protection, and the trader is legally required to tell you clearly, before you pay, that what you're buying is an LTA rather than a fully protected package.

This is a genuinely useful distinction to understand, because some booking flows that *feel* like a package (flight booked, then immediately prompted to "add a hotel and save") may in fact be structured as an LTA rather than a full ATOL package — meaning your protection is real but narrower than you might assume. Always check what the confirmation documents actually say, rather than assuming from the flow of the website.

### Where ATOL specifically fits into the PTR framework

ATOL is the CAA's implementation of the PTRs' insolvency-protection requirement, specifically for packages and some Linked Travel Arrangements that include a flight. In short: the PTRs are the law that decides *whether you have a package (or LTA) at all*. ATOL is *how the money is protected* if that package includes flights and the organiser is UK-established. It's worth remembering these are two different layers — a booking can fail the "is this a package" test entirely (most DIY flight+hotel combinations do), in which case the question of ATOL protection never even arises, because there was no package to protect in the first place.

## Chargeback vs Section 75 vs ATOL vs Travel Insurance

These four protections get conflated constantly, but they cover genuinely different situations, apply to different payment methods, and kick in at different points. Understanding the differences is the single most useful thing a DIY-booking UK traveller can do.

### ATOL

**What it covers:** the financial collapse of an ATOL-holding travel organiser you booked a genuine flight-inclusive package through. **What it does not cover:** standalone flights, standalone hotels, ordinary delays, service quality, or anything at all if your booking wasn't structured as a package in the first place. **How you claim:** through the CAA's dedicated process for the failed company, using your ATOL Certificate as proof of protection. **Cost to you:** the small ATOL Protection Contribution (currently £2.50 per person), already built into the package price.

### Section 75 (Consumer Credit Act 1974)

**What it covers:** purchases between £100 and £30,000 made on a UK credit card, where the retailer fails to deliver — including going out of business entirely. Because the law makes your card issuer *jointly liable* with the retailer, you claim directly from your card provider rather than chasing the collapsed company. **What it does not cover:** debit card payments, purchases under £100 or over £30,000, or situations where the transaction chain to the actual supplier is broken by certain payment intermediaries (this is a genuinely contested area — some card providers have resisted Section 75 claims where a booking agent sat between you and the airline or hotel, so paying the actual supplier directly, where possible, strengthens your position). **How you claim:** contact your credit card provider directly, provide your booking confirmation and evidence the company has ceased trading. **Cost to you:** nothing extra — it's a statutory right that comes with qualifying credit card purchases.

### Chargeback

**What it covers:** a broader but weaker net than Section 75 — available on both debit and credit cards, run voluntarily by the card networks (Visa, Mastercard) rather than mandated by law, generally used for goods or services not received, items significantly not as described, duplicate charges, or fraud. **What it does not cover:** it's discretionary — the card network and your bank decide the outcome, and there's no fixed legal entitlement the way there is with Section 75. **How you claim:** contact your bank or card provider, who submits the chargeback request to the card network on your behalf, typically within around 120 days of the transaction or the expected service date. **Cost to you:** nothing extra, but success isn't guaranteed the way Section 75's legal right is.

### Travel Insurance with SAFI / End Supplier Failure cover

**What it covers:** specifically, the financial failure of an airline, hotel or other travel supplier you booked independently — but only if your policy explicitly includes Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI) or End Supplier Failure cover. Standard travel insurance, bought without checking for this, typically does *not* cover supplier insolvency at all. **What it does not cover:** anything outside the specific perils listed in your policy wording — always read the actual document, not just the marketing summary. **How you claim:** through your insurer's claims process, with your booking confirmation, proof of payment and evidence of the supplier's failure. **Cost to you:** the price of the policy, usually a modest addition to a standard travel insurance premium — [compare travel insurance options through JetMeAway](/insurance) and specifically check for SAFI or End Supplier Failure wording before buying if you're travelling DIY.

### Putting the four together

For a DIY-booked trip, no single one of these four fully replicates ATOL's coverage on its own — but together, they come close. Pay by UK credit card wherever the transaction value allows (Section 75), keep chargeback as a fallback for debit-card or edge-case purchases, and buy travel insurance with SAFI/End Supplier Failure cover specifically checked for, not assumed. That combination is the honest, practical equivalent of "building your own ATOL" for a self-booked trip.

## How to Stay Protected When You Book Standalone Travel

Comparing and booking standalone options often secures better room rates, direct airline loyalty points, and more flexible seat selection than a rigid package allows. Since ATOL isn't backing you up on standalone bookings, put these protections in place deliberately, rather than assuming they exist by default.

**Pay via credit card, not debit, wherever the amount allows.** Under Section 75, your credit card provider becomes jointly liable for purchases between £100 and £30,000 if a travel provider goes completely bankrupt. This is the single highest-value, lowest-effort protection available to a DIY traveller, and it costs nothing beyond using the right card at checkout.

**Get SAFI or End Supplier Failure insurance.** When buying [travel insurance](/insurance) through JetMeAway, specifically check the policy includes Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI) or End Supplier Failure cover — don't assume a standard policy has it, because many don't. This is the protection layer that most directly substitutes for the ATOL gap on an independent airline or hotel going under.

**Check cancellation terms before you book.** Keep track of the cancellation windows for your standalone hotel bookings specifically, so you retain the option to cancel or shift dates if your flight is disrupted. A flexible-rate hotel costs more upfront but removes an entire category of risk if your travel plans change; a non-refundable rate is cheaper but locks you in regardless of what happens to the flight side of the trip.

**Keep every confirmation, invoice and payment record together.** Whether you're claiming through Section 75, chargeback, or a SAFI insurance policy, claims move faster and succeed more often with a complete paper trail — booking confirmation, payment receipt, and (if relevant) evidence the company has entered administration.

**Verify ATOL claims before you trust them.** If a smaller or unfamiliar operator markets itself as "ATOL protected," check it directly on the CAA's official ATOL check tool at atol.org rather than taking the marketing claim at face value. A genuine ATOL holder will have a verifiable ATOL number that checks out on the CAA's own database.

**Understand what you're actually buying at checkout.** If a site prompts you to "add a hotel and save" after booking a flight, read carefully whether that combination is being sold as a genuine package (full ATOL protection), a Linked Travel Arrangement (narrower protection), or simply two separate bookings dressed up in the same flow (no combined protection at all). The confirmation documents, not the marketing language on the page, tell you the truth.

## Real 2026 Scenarios

Working through specific situations makes the legal framework concrete. These scenarios reflect the kinds of bookings UK travellers commonly make through comparison tools like JetMeAway in 2026.

**Scenario 1 — City break, flight and hotel booked separately, three weeks apart.** A traveller books a £68 easyJet return to Lisbon in April, then books a hotel through a hotel comparison site in early May for the same dates. No ATOL protection exists on either booking — they were made weeks apart, on unconnected sites, with no shared checkout. If the flight is cancelled, easyJet must rebook or refund under UK261; the hotel booking is entirely unaffected and stands on its own terms. If easyJet somehow ceased trading before the trip (an extreme scenario, but the one this whole guide exists to explain), Section 75 (if paid by credit card) or SAFI travel insurance would be the traveller's only recourse — not ATOL.

**Scenario 2 — Flight booked on the airline's own site, hotel booked the same afternoon on a comparison site.** Same-day timing doesn't change the legal analysis. Provided the two transactions were genuinely separate — different providers, different contracts, no combined total price, no data-passing checkout — this remains two standalone bookings with no ATOL protection, exactly like Scenario 1 with a shorter gap between purchases.

**Scenario 3 — A genuine flight-inclusive package booked through an ATOL-licensed partner.** A traveller uses JetMeAway to compare options, then completes a combined flight-plus-hotel package through one of our ATOL-licensed partners (for example Expedia, ATOL 5788, or Trip.com, ATOL 11572) in a single transaction with a single total price. This meets the Package Travel Regulations' definition and carries ATOL protection — the traveller should receive an ATOL Certificate at the point of booking. If that specific partner's ATOL-holding entity collapsed before the trip, the CAA's ATOL scheme would step in.

**Scenario 4 — Multi-city trip with three separate hotel bookings and one flight.** A traveller flies into one city and books three separate hotels for three separate legs of the trip, each with a different hotel or OTA, plus the flight booked independently with the airline. None of these four bookings are combined into a package — each is its own contract. This is standalone booking at scale: more moving parts, but the same legal principle repeated four times. The traveller's protection comes entirely from payment method (Section 75/chargeback) and travel insurance, not from ATOL, on any of the four components.

**Scenario 5 — A "flight plus hotel" prompt at checkout that turns out to be a Linked Travel Arrangement.** A traveller books a flight on a booking site, and within the same session is prompted to "add a hotel" — the hotel is booked with a different underlying supplier but through the same site's checkout, within 24 hours of the flight purchase, with data passed between the two bookings. This may meet the legal definition of a Linked Travel Arrangement rather than a full package. The traveller should check their confirmation documents carefully: an LTA gives some insolvency protection specific to money paid to that trader, but it's narrower than full ATOL package protection, and the site is legally required to disclose which category applies before payment is taken.

**Scenario 6 — Independent airline collapse with a separately booked non-refundable hotel.** A traveller has booked a non-refundable hotel rate directly with a hotel, and separately, an independent long-haul carrier serving the same route ceases trading before the trip. The airline failure has no automatic effect on the hotel booking's status or protection — the hotel booking continues under its own terms regardless of what happens with the airline. The traveller's flight-side recourse is Section 75 or SAFI insurance; the hotel-side risk (a wasted non-refundable rate if the trip can no longer proceed as planned) is a separate consideration entirely, best mitigated at booking time by weighing a flexible-rate hotel against the savings of a non-refundable one when travel plans carry any uncertainty.

## Common Myths, Corrected

**Myth: "Any flight booked from the UK is automatically ATOL protected."** Corrected: false. Scheduled airlines selling their own seats directly are specifically exempt from ATOL. ATOL only attaches to flight-inclusive packages sold by an ATOL-licensed organiser.

**Myth: "If I book flight and hotel on the same site, it's automatically a package."** Corrected: false. What matters is whether the transaction is combined into one contract and one total price, not which website you happened to use for both searches. Many comparison sites, including JetMeAway, deliberately keep standalone flight and hotel searches separate so you retain flexibility — and that structure means no package is created.

**Myth: "A more expensive hotel or flight must come with more protection."** Corrected: false. Price has no bearing on ATOL protection. A £2,000 luxury hotel booked standalone has exactly the same (zero) ATOL protection as a £60 budget hotel booked standalone — protection depends on transaction structure, not cost.

**Myth: "Travel insurance automatically covers a travel company going bust."** Corrected: false, unless the specific policy includes SAFI or End Supplier Failure cover. Many standard policies exclude supplier insolvency entirely — check the wording, not the marketing headline.

**Myth: "ATOL covers flight delays and cancellations."** Corrected: false. ATOL is purely an insolvency-protection scheme. Ordinary delays and cancellations are governed by UK261 passenger rights law, which applies regardless of ATOL and regardless of whether you booked a package or a standalone flight.

**Myth: "Booking through a big-name comparison site gives you the same protection as booking a package."** Corrected: false. A comparison site's brand recognition has no bearing on whether ATOL applies — the legal trigger is the structure of the transaction you complete, not which company helped you compare prices beforehand.

**Myth: "If my package holiday changes airline, I lose my ATOL protection."** Corrected: false, provided the organiser issues an updated ATOL Certificate reflecting the change — the protection continues, just against the updated details. Keep the most recent certificate you're issued.
