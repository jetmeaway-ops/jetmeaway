## What to See and Do in Bangalore

Bangalore does not hit you with monuments the way Delhi or Agra do — it rewards you slowly. Start with the green: **Lalbagh Botanical Garden**, laid out in the eighteenth century under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, with its Victorian glasshouse and a 3,000-million-year-old rock outcrop, and **Cubbon Park**, 300 acres of shade in the middle of the city that closes to traffic on Sundays. Between them they are the reason Bangalore is called the Garden City.

For history, **Bangalore Palace** is a Tudor-revival mansion modelled loosely on Windsor Castle, still owned by the Mysore royal family and open for audio-guided tours. **Tipu Sultan's Summer Palace** near the City Market is a beautiful two-storey teak pavilion from the 1790s, small but genuinely atmospheric. The **Vidhana Soudha** — the enormous granite state legislature building beside Cubbon Park — is not open to casual visitors but is worth seeing from outside, especially when it is floodlit at the weekend. The **ISKCON temple** on a hill in Rajajinagar is one of the largest Krishna temples in the world and a striking piece of modern religious architecture.

For shopping, **Commercial Street** is the dense, bargaining-heavy option and **MG Road** and Brigade Road are the mainstream one. And then there is what Bangalore is genuinely famous for: eating and drinking. The city effectively invented Indian craft beer, and **Indiranagar** and **Koramangala** are where the breweries, independent restaurants and cafes cluster. Add a South Indian breakfast — idli, vada, a crisp dosa and a filter coffee — at one of the old Malleshwaram or Basavanagudi institutions and you have understood the city better than any monument would teach you. The **tech corridor** around Whitefield, Manyata and **Electronic City** is not a sight, but it explains why the rest exists.

Beyond the city, three trips are worth the traffic. **Mysore Palace** is around three hours south-west and is genuinely spectacular, especially illuminated. **Nandi Hills**, 60km north, is the famous pre-dawn drive for sunrise above the clouds — conveniently on the airport side of the city. And the **Coorg coffee plantations**, five to six hours south-west, are the classic two-or-three-night escape into cool hill air and plantation stays.

## Where to Stay in Bangalore

**MG Road and the central belt** — Richmond Road, Residency Road, St Mark's Road, Lavelle Road — is the best base for a first visit. It is the most walkable part of the city, close to Cubbon Park, the Vidhana Soudha, Bangalore Palace and Commercial Street, and it is on the Namma Metro. Nearly all the mid-range hotels in this guide and several of the luxury ones sit here.

**Indiranagar and Koramangala** are where you stay for food and nightlife. Indiranagar has the craft-beer bars, 100 Feet Road restaurants and a metro station; Koramangala is younger and denser with independent cafes but no metro yet. If your idea of a good evening is walking to dinner rather than sitting in a taxi, base yourself in one of these two.

**Whitefield and Electronic City** are business bases and nothing else. Whitefield covers the eastern ITPL corridor; Electronic City is the southern belt on Hosur Road. Manyata Tech Park in the north is the third cluster. Staying in the wrong one costs you hours a day in traffic — which brings us to the honest bit.

**Near the airport at Devanahalli** is a real option, but only for flight nights. Kempegowda International sits roughly 40km north of the city, and the transfer realistically takes one to one and a half hours, more at peak times or in rain. If you land late or fly out at dawn, an airport-area hotel is the sane choice. Otherwise stay in town and allow serious margin for the drive.

One last point in Bangalore's favour: at around **900 metres of elevation** on the Deccan Plateau, it has the mildest year-round climate of any big Indian city. October to February is the best window, March to May is warmer but still comfortable, and June to September brings monsoon showers — usually afternoon and evening rather than all day, and the city is at its greenest then.

## Bangalore Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Bangalore?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party "visa services" charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Bangalore?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards are accepted almost everywhere in Bangalore, which is India's most card-and-app friendly big city, but carry some cash for autorickshaws and small cafes.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Bangalore?**
Yes. British Airways flies direct from London Heathrow to Kempegowda International Airport (BLR) in roughly ten hours. Other airlines connect through the Gulf hubs — Emirates via Dubai, Qatar Airways via Doha, Etihad via Abu Dhabi — which is usually cheaper but adds several hours. BLR is also a major domestic hub, so onward flights to Goa, Kerala, Mumbai and Chennai are frequent and cheap.

**When is the best time to visit Bangalore?**
October to February. That is the driest, coolest stretch, with comfortable days and genuinely pleasant evenings. March to May is warmer but still far milder than Delhi or Chennai. June to September is the southwest monsoon, which in Bangalore mostly means afternoon and evening showers rather than all-day rain — it is very green then, and hotel rates are lower.

**Why is Bangalore's weather so much better than the rest of India?**
Altitude. Bangalore sits on the Deccan Plateau at around 900 metres, which knocks several degrees off the temperature year round and gives it the mildest climate of any big Indian city. It is why the British made it a garrison town, why it is nicknamed the Garden City, and why so many people move there. You will rarely need more than a light layer for the evenings.

**How bad is Bangalore's traffic, really?**
It is genuinely bad — this is the honest caveat of the city. Bangalore's road network never caught up with its tech boom, and journeys that look like fifteen minutes on a map routinely take an hour at peak times. Plan around it: pick a hotel in the area you will actually spend time in, use the Namma Metro where it goes, and never schedule anything tight after a cross-city journey.

**How far is Bangalore airport from the city?**
About 40 kilometres north, at Devanahalli — one of the longest airport transfers of any major Indian city. Allow between one and one and a half hours, and more at rush hour or in the rain. There is a dedicated Vayu Vajra airport bus network, plus Uber and Ola. If you have a very early flight, an airport-area hotel like SM Royal Stay near Bangalore International Airport is worth the night.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Bangalore?**
MG Road and the central Richmond Road / Residency Road belt. It is the most walkable part of the city, close to Cubbon Park, the Vidhana Soudha, Commercial Street shopping and Bangalore Palace, and it is on the Namma Metro. Most of the mid-range hotels in this guide sit here, which makes it the easiest and best-connected first-trip base.

**Where should I stay for food and nightlife?**
Indiranagar or Koramangala. Indiranagar's 100 Feet Road and 12th Main are the heart of Bangalore's craft-beer and restaurant scene, and Indiranagar is on the metro's Purple Line. Koramangala is younger, denser and full of independent cafes and startup crowds but has no metro station yet. Both put you within walking distance of dinner, which in this city matters more than proximity to sights.

**Where should I stay for business or the tech parks?**
Whitefield or Electronic City, depending on which employer you are visiting. Whitefield is east and covers ITPL and the Whitefield tech corridor; Electronic City is south on the Hosur Road and covers the big Infosys and Wipro campuses. Manyata Tech Park in the north is the third cluster. Staying in the right one saves you two hours of traffic a day — that is not an exaggeration in Bangalore.

**Is the Namma Metro useful for visitors?**
Yes, and it is improving fast. The Purple and Green Lines cross the city centre and cover MG Road, Cubbon Park, Vidhana Soudha, Majestic, Indiranagar and Whitefield, and it is clean, air-conditioned, cheap and signed in English. It is by far the fastest way to move at peak times because it skips the traffic entirely. It does not yet reach the airport or Koramangala, so pair it with app taxis.

**Should I use Uber and Ola in Bangalore?**
Yes — both work well, and Bangalore is one of the easiest Indian cities for them since app culture runs deep here. You get a fixed fare, a tracked route and no negotiation. Autorickshaws are everywhere and can be booked through the same apps, which avoids the meter argument entirely. Allow far more time than the app estimate suggests at rush hour.

**Can I drink the tap water in Bangalore?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only. Most hotels supply bottled water in the room or have a filtered dispenser. Avoid ice at street stalls until your stomach has adjusted. Bangalore's restaurant scene is excellent and generally very clean, and the South Indian breakfast — idli, dosa, vada with filter coffee — is one of the best reasons to visit.

**Is Bangalore a good first trip to India?**
It is one of the gentlest introductions. It is calmer and less overwhelming than Delhi, the weather is mild, English is widely spoken, the food is outstanding and app taxis and cards work everywhere. The trade-off is that it has fewer headline monuments than Delhi, Agra or Jaipur — Bangalore is a city you enjoy for its parks, food and pace rather than tick off.

**What is there to actually see in Bangalore?**
Lalbagh Botanical Garden and Cubbon Park are the two great green spaces and the reason for the Garden City nickname. Bangalore Palace is a Tudor-revival mansion you can tour, Tipu Sultan's Summer Palace is an eighteenth-century teak pavilion near the City Market, the Vidhana Soudha is the vast state legislature building, and the ISKCON temple on a hill in Rajajinagar is one of the largest Krishna temples in the world.

**Can I do a day trip to Mysore from Bangalore?**
Yes, and it is the classic one. Mysore Palace is around 150 kilometres south-west, roughly three hours by the expressway or by train, and it is spectacular — especially when it is illuminated. **What is Nandi Hills and is it worth the early start?**
Nandi Hills is a hill fort about 60 kilometres north of the city, famous for sunrise above a sea of cloud. You leave in the dark — typically around 4am — and on a clear morning it is genuinely worth it. It is conveniently on the airport side of the city, so it pairs well with an airport-area hotel or an afternoon flight.

**Is Coorg worth adding to a Bangalore trip?**
If you have two or three spare nights, yes. Coorg (Kodagu) is India's coffee heartland, around five to six hours south-west, with plantation stays, waterfalls and cool hill air. It is the standard weekend escape for Bangaloreans, which means it books up on Fridays. It suits a slower second week rather than a day trip.

**Why is Bangalore called Bengaluru?**
Bengaluru is the official Kannada name, adopted formally in 2014. Both are in everyday use — locals and airlines use each interchangeably, hotel names in this guide use both spellings, and the airport code is BLR. Nothing about your booking changes depending on which spelling you search for.

**Which side of the road do they drive on in India?**
The left, as in the UK — but Bangalore traffic is dense and lane discipline is looser than you will be used to. Very few visitors self-drive, and there is no need to: app taxis, autorickshaws and the metro cover everything. If you want a car for Mysore or Coorg, hiring one with a driver is normal, cheap and much less stressful.

**How many days do I need in Bangalore?**
Two full days covers the city itself: one for Lalbagh, Cubbon Park, the Vidhana Soudha and Bangalore Palace, and one for Tipu Sultan's Summer Palace, the ISKCON temple, Commercial Street and an evening in Indiranagar or Koramangala. Add a third for Mysore and a fourth for Nandi Hills at sunrise, and a longer tail if you want Coorg.

**Is Bangalore safe for tourists?**
It is generally regarded as one of India's easier big cities, and millions of visitors and residents move around it daily without incident. The realistic risks are traffic, petty theft in crowded markets and overcharging rather than anything more serious. Use app taxis rather than street cabs after dark, and read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go.

**Do Bangalore hotels charge extra fees?**
There is no resort fee, but Indian hotel rates carry GST, which varies by room-rate band, and some properties add a service charge or price breakfast separately. Budget rooms are the most likely to exclude breakfast. Always check what is included on the hotel's live page before comparing two similar-looking rates.

**Is breakfast usually included in Bangalore?**
At the luxury and mid-range tiers, usually yes, and often a very good South Indian and continental buffet. In the budget tier it is inconsistent — some include a simple breakfast, others charge a small supplement. Breakfast is cheap to add in Bangalore, so the room rate matters far more than the inclusion when you are comparing.

**Are there good vegetarian hotels and restaurants in Bangalore?**
Bangalore is one of the best cities in the world to eat vegetarian. Pure-veg restaurants are everywhere, South Indian breakfast is vegetarian by default, and some hotels in this guide — The Byke Signature Business, for example — are pure-veg properties by chain policy. Vegan is harder because of the dairy, but it is improving quickly in Indiranagar and Koramangala.
