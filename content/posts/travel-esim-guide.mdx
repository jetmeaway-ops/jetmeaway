## 1. What Is an eSIM, Actually?

An eSIM ("embedded SIM") is a small chip soldered directly onto your phone's motherboard, distinct from the removable plastic SIM card that's defined mobile phones since the 1990s. Instead of a physical card printed with a unique identifier and mailed or handed to you in a shop, an eSIM holds that same identifying information — the profile — purely in software. Your carrier (or, for travel purposes, your eSIM provider) sends that profile to you as a QR code. Scan it, and the phone downloads and installs the profile onto the embedded chip. You're on the network in under 90 seconds.

The crucial difference for travellers: because the profile lives in software, a single eSIM chip can hold **multiple carrier profiles at once**, and modern phones can run two active at the same time (plus, on many models, a physical SIM as a third line). That's what makes dual-SIM travel setups possible — your UK carrier and a destination-country data plan, both live on the same phone, switching automatically or manually depending on what you need at that moment.

In practical terms: you can land at Bangkok's Suvarnabhumi Airport, pull your phone out of airplane mode, and already be connected to a Thai network at Thai prices — no hunting for an AIS or TrueMove counter, no queuing behind twenty other flights' worth of tired passengers, no fumbling for a passport and a pin to eject a tray in the arrivals hall.

### Why physical SIMs are disappearing

Three forces are killing the plastic card, and none of them are reversing:

1. **Flagship phones are dropping the tray.** Every current iPhone from the XS generation supports eSIM, and from the iPhone 14 onward, US-market models ship with no physical tray at all — eSIM-only. That pattern is expanding into more countries and more manufacturers' flagship lines each year, and Samsung's high-end Galaxy S and Z Fold/Flip ranges are following the same trajectory market by market.
2. **5G speeds on eSIMs now match local SIMs.** A few years ago there was a genuine, measurable speed penalty on eSIM data versus a physical local SIM. That gap has closed. eSIM carriers ride the same physical 5G towers as local networks in most major cities — any perceived speed cap was a commercial choice by specific budget plans, not a technical limitation of the eSIM format itself.
3. **Coverage caught up to demand.** Airalo alone now covers 200-plus destinations; Yesim covers 180-plus with a particularly strong European footprint. Between JetMeAway's two eSIM partners, there's almost nowhere a UK traveller can go and find themselves with no eSIM option at all — the exceptions (heavily restricted markets like China and Russia) are covered later in this guide.

## 3. Setting Up Dual-SIM Properly: The Configuration That Actually Works

The single most valuable five minutes you'll spend before any trip is setting up dual-SIM correctly — not just installing the eSIM, but configuring which line does what.

**The target configuration:**

- **Physical UK SIM** → stays in the tray, handles **calls and SMS only**. This is what receives your banking 2FA codes, your family's calls, and any SMS-based verification you rely on.
- **Travel eSIM** → handles **all data**. Maps, messaging apps, social media, everything.
- **UK SIM data roaming** → switched **off**. This is the step people forget, and it's the one that causes a surprise £40 roaming charge landing on top of a perfectly good eSIM purchase.

### iPhone setup

1. Install the eSIM (see the activation flow below).
2. Go to **Settings > Cellular** (or **Mobile Data**).
3. Under your **UK SIM line**, tap it, then toggle **Data Roaming** off.
4. Under **Cellular Data**, set the default data line to your **new eSIM**.
5. Under **Default Voice Line**, confirm it's set to your **UK SIM** (this should be automatic, but check).
6. iMessage and FaceTime stay tied to your UK number regardless of which line is carrying data — no need to change anything there.

### Android setup

1. Install the eSIM.
2. Go to **Settings > Network & Internet > SIMs**.
3. Tap your **UK SIM**, and disable **Data roaming**.
4. Set **Mobile Data** to route through the **eSIM**.
5. Confirm **Calls** and **SMS** default to the **UK SIM**.

### The banking-code check

Before you fly, send yourself a test 2FA code from your bank's app (most apps have a "resend code" option) to confirm the UK SIM is still receiving SMS normally with roaming off. This takes thirty seconds and eliminates the single most common travel-eSIM anxiety: "will my banking app still work abroad?" It will, because SMS delivery to your UK number doesn't depend on data roaming being enabled — only on the SIM being physically present and registered on a network, which it will be even sitting passively in your phone abroad.

## 4. The Activation Flow, Start to Finish

Here's exactly what happens, in order, when you buy a travel eSIM through JetMeAway or directly with Airalo or Yesim.

1. **Buy the plan.** Pick your destination country (or region), pick a data size and validity window, and pay — no ID, no passport, just an email address and a card. This takes under two minutes on [JetMeAway's eSIM page](/esim).
2. **Receive the QR code.** The provider emails a QR code (and usually also shows it directly in their app) within a minute or two of purchase.
3. **Install before you fly.** Scan the QR code over your home WiFi — you don't need to wait until you land. The profile downloads and installs in about 60-90 seconds.
4. **The plan sits dormant.** Most plans don't start their validity countdown at installation — they start on **first connection to the destination network**. This is the detail almost nobody mentions and it matters: install a 7-day Thailand eSIM in your living room in Manchester a week before you fly, and the clock doesn't start ticking until your plane's wheels touch down in Bangkok and your phone finds an AIS or TrueMove signal.
5. **Land and toggle it on.** Switch off airplane mode, make sure the eSIM's data line is enabled (it usually activates automatically if you followed the dual-SIM setup above), and you're online within moments — no fumbling with a paperclip, no queue at a kiosk.
6. **Top up if needed.** If you run low, open the provider's app and buy more data on the same profile — no new QR code, no reinstall.

**One practical gotcha:** always double-check the validity-start rule for your specific plan before booking a trip with a gap in it — flying out, spending a week somewhere, flying home for a few days, then flying back out. Some plans count validity from first connection regardless of whether you're actually using data day to day, so a 14-day plan activated on day one of a fortnight that includes a five-day trip home might expire before your return leg. If your itinerary has a break in it, check the specific provider's validity terms rather than assuming.

## 6. Regional vs Global Plans: When to Bundle, When Not To

This is one of the most consequential decisions for anyone visiting more than one country on a single trip, and it's easy to get expensively wrong in either direction.

**Buy a regional or global bundle when:**
- Your trip crosses **three or more countries** in one region — inter-railing through five European cities, or island-hopping Thailand, Laos and Cambodia.
- You want **zero-friction continuity** — your data just keeps working as the train crosses a border, with no manual eSIM swap.
- You're on a **fast-moving itinerary** where you won't be in any single country long enough to justify a country-specific plan or a local SIM's setup friction.

**Buy a single-country plan when:**
- You're visiting **one destination only**, even for a long stay — a country-specific plan is reliably cheaper per GB than the regional equivalent, because you're not paying a coverage premium for countries you won't visit.
- Your trip is **two countries or fewer** — the maths rarely favours a regional bundle at that scale; two single-country plans usually beat one regional one.

**A concrete example:** a two-week inter-rail trip through France, Germany, Austria and Italy is a clear case for Airalo's Eurolink regional bundle (roughly 10GB covering 39 European countries under one profile) rather than buying and swapping four separate country eSIMs at each border. But a two-week trip that's entirely in Spain is better served by a Spain-specific Airalo or Yesim plan, which will beat the regional bundle on price for the same data allowance.

The rule of thumb: **regional bundles buy you convenience and continuity across borders; single-country plans buy you the cheapest possible price for a fixed destination.** Match the plan to the actual shape of your itinerary rather than defaulting to whichever sounds more comprehensive.

## 7. Top-Ups vs New Plans: What's the Difference and When Each Makes Sense

**A top-up** adds more data (and sometimes more validity days) to an **existing, already-installed eSIM profile**. No new QR code, no reinstall — you open the provider's app, buy more data, and it lands on the same profile within moments.

**A new plan** means installing an entirely fresh eSIM profile, complete with its own QR code and its own validity window, separate from anything you've already got running.

### When to top up

- You're still inside your current plan's validity window and simply ran out of data before the days expired.
- You're staying in the **same country** and want to extend both data and time without disrupting your existing connection.
- You want the least friction possible — no new QR code, no reconfiguring dual-SIM settings, nothing.

### When to buy a new plan instead

- You're moving to a **different country** not covered by your current plan.
- Your current plan's **validity window has expired**, even if you never technically used all the data — expired data doesn't roll over into a fresh purchase, it simply evaporates.
- You want a genuinely different plan shape — switching from a fixed-GB plan to an unlimited one, for example, usually means a new purchase rather than a top-up, since it's a different product tier.

**A cost trap worth flagging explicitly:** because unused data expires with the plan rather than rolling over, buying one large plan "to be safe" for a short trip is often worse value than buying a smaller plan and topping up if you actually need more. A 20GB/30-day plan for a 5-day trip wastes most of its own allowance; a 3GB/7-day plan with a one-tap top-up available if you run low is usually the more efficient purchase.

## 8. KYC Requirements: What eSIMs Skip That Local SIMs Don't

**Know Your Customer (KYC)** rules require mobile carriers to verify a customer's identity — typically a passport or national ID — before activating a SIM. Regulators impose this to curb SIM-card fraud and, in some countries, for security and surveillance reasons. It's one of the most underrated advantages of a travel eSIM: **buying an Airalo or Yesim eSIM requires no ID at all — just an email address and a payment card** — while buying a local physical SIM in the same country very often does.

Here's how that KYC burden varies by destination, so you know what you'd be dealing with if you went the local-SIM route instead:

## 9. Hotspot and Tethering: What Actually Works

If you plan to work from a laptop while travelling — checking email, joining video calls, uploading files — you'll be hotspotting off your eSIM, and this is where plan choice matters more than almost anywhere else in this guide.

**The general rule:** standard fixed-GB Airalo and Yesim plans allow tethering up to your plan's full data allowance, with no separate hotspot restriction. If you've bought 10GB, you can burn all 10GB through a hotspotted laptop exactly as you could through the phone itself.

**Where it gets restrictive: "unlimited" plans.** These almost always carry a **daily fair-use threshold**, after which speed throttles — sometimes down to a crawl that's unusable for video calls. Critically, some providers apply a **lower, separate threshold specifically for hotspot/tethered use** than for on-device browsing, because a laptop pulling video calls and cloud syncs burns data at a completely different rate than a phone doing maps and messaging. Airalo's Unlimited Lite tier, for example, throttles noticeably faster under heavy tethered use than the headline "unlimited" framing suggests; check the specific plan's fair-use policy rather than assuming unlimited means unrestricted.

**Practical guidance if you're working remotely on the road:**

- **Buy a large fixed-GB plan (20-50GB) rather than an unlimited one.** A capped plan with full, un-throttled speed for its entire duration is more reliable for video calls and uploads than an "unlimited" plan that quietly drops to 2G-equivalent speeds once you cross a daily threshold.
- **Disable background syncs before you tether.** Turn off automatic cloud photo/video backup (iCloud Photos, Google Photos) on both the phone providing the hotspot and any device using it — a single background video upload can burn through several GB without you noticing.
- **Check the fair-use policy's specific wording**, not just the headline "unlimited" claim, particularly the daily threshold in GB and whether tethered/hotspot use is called out separately from on-device use.

For genuinely heavy, sustained hotspot use over weeks or months — a full year abroad, for instance — a local SIM or a local broadband/mobile-broadband contract will consistently beat any travel eSIM on cost and reliability, for the same reason local contracts beat eSIMs on any long-stay comparison: providers price eSIMs for short-term convenience, not for month-after-month heavy data volume.

<a id="data-sizing"></a>

## 10. How Much Data Do You Actually Need?

Most travellers massively over-buy. Here's a realistic calibration based on actual usage patterns, not marketing-page upsell tiers:

- **1GB per week** — mostly on hotel WiFi, using data only for maps, messages and occasional light browsing on the go.
- **3GB per week** — actively using Google Maps for navigation, social media, and streaming music outside WiFi.
- **5GB+ per week** — regular video calls, some remote work, or streaming video on mobile data.
- **Unlimited** — digital nomads, content creators uploading regularly, or anyone treating their phone as their primary internet connection with no hotel WiFi fallback.

For most **two-week trips, 5-8GB total is genuinely plenty** for the large majority of travellers. Don't let a provider's sales funnel push you into a 20GB "pro" plan sized for a use case you don't have — the unused allowance simply expires with the plan.

**A rough per-activity reference**, useful for building your own estimate:

## 11. Privacy Shield: The Part No eSIM Company Puts in Their Marketing

Here's the part no eSIM company will tell you outright. When you roam on your home UK carrier, that carrier logs everywhere you go — every cell-tower handshake recorded and retained under UK data-retention rules for around 12 months. It's legal, routine, and increasingly relevant to everything from insurance risk profiling to government data requests.

Using a travel eSIM puts a genuine privacy layer between your home identity and your travel activity. The local carrier sees an anonymous eSIM profile — no ID was required to buy it, remember — rather than your home phone number tied to your UK identity. For most people this is simply a nice-to-have. For journalists, lawyers, executives, or anyone with a specific reason to keep their movements less traceable, it's a genuinely material difference, not a marketing angle.

## 12. Five Mistakes to Avoid

**Don't buy at the airport kiosk.** "Tourist SIM" counters at Heathrow, Gatwick and most major airports mark up considerably versus buying an eSIM in advance from your phone or laptop. Buy before you fly — there's no cost benefit to waiting.

**Don't disable your UK SIM line entirely.** You'll miss banking 2FA codes, family calls and any SMS-based verification tied to your UK number. Keep both lines active with the configuration in the [dual-SIM setup section](#dual-sim-setup) above.

**Don't buy the smallest plan without knowing your actual usage.** "1GB / 7 days" sounds like plenty until a morning of live navigation and a couple of Instagram Stories quietly empties it by lunchtime. Use the [data sizing table](#data-sizing) above rather than guessing.

**Don't forget to disable cloud photo backup over cellular.** A single background video upload from iCloud Photos or Google Photos can silently burn 2-3GB. iPhone: Settings > Photos > Cellular Data > off. Android: Google Photos app > Settings > Back up & sync > Mobile data usage > Wi-Fi only.

**Don't trust "unlimited" without reading the fair-use terms.** Genuinely unlimited, no-throttle plans are rare. Most "unlimited" tiers slow down considerably after a daily data threshold — often lower for tethered/hotspot use than for on-device browsing. Check the specific plan's fair-use policy, especially if you're planning to work from a laptop.
