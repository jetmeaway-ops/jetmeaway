## Where to Stay in Strasbourg: Choosing Your Base

Strasbourg is small and walkable, so the choice of base matters less than in a sprawling city — but it still shapes the trip. Here is the honest breakdown.

**The Grande Île** is the right answer for most visitors. It is flat, largely pedestrianised and beautiful, and staying on it means you walk out of the door into the best of the city. The trade is price, and, near the busiest squares, some evening noise; light sleepers should ask for a courtyard room.

**Petite France**, at the western tip of the Grande Île, is the prettiest quarter — half-timbered houses over the canals, the covered bridges, the Barrage Vauban — and a lovely place to be based, with the REGENT Petite France spa hotel and the Adagio aparthotel among the options. It is small and short on rooms, so if you cannot find one there, anywhere else on the island is minutes away.

**The station quarter**, just west of the Grande Île around Gare de Strasbourg, is the value sweet spot. It carries several of this guide's better-priced hotels and aparthotels — the Diana Dauphine, Origami, Victoria, Vendôme, K Hotel — it is a ten-minute walk or one tram stop from the cathedral, and it puts you next to the trains for Colmar, the wine route and Paris. It is a working city quarter rather than a postcard, but it is central, convenient and cheaper than the island.

**The Neustadt and the European district** lie north and north-east of the old town. The Neustadt is the grand German-imperial quarter of wide boulevards and Wilhelmine architecture around Place de la République — handsome, central and quieter than the island. Further out around the Orangerie park sit the **European Parliament, the Council of Europe and the Court of Human Rights**, a green, calm area that is the right base only if your trip is built around the institutions; for a leisure break it is a tram ride from the action, and the Hilton by the Wacken convention centre is the main hotel out here.

**The western and outer districts** — around the **Zénith** arena, **Lingolsheim** near the airport, and the **port and Deux-Rives** area towards the Rhine — are where the budget chains and aparthotels cluster, with rates from about £35 to £55. You trade a central address for a tram ride, but the CTS network is fast and cheap, so this is a genuine saving. Best for drivers, event-goers at the Zénith, and anyone whose priority is price over proximity.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Strasbourg

Start at the centre, because in Strasbourg the centre is the show. **Strasbourg Cathedral** — Cathédrale Notre-Dame — is one of the great Gothic buildings of Europe, its single soaring spire once the tallest structure in the Christian world, its rose window and carved façade extraordinary in close-up. Inside, the **astronomical clock** performs its procession of figures each day at 12:30, a Renaissance marvel of automata and astronomy that still draws a crowd; climb the platform for a view over the rooftops and, on a clear day, the Vosges and the Black Forest. The square around it, Place de la Cathédrale, holds the Maison Kammerzell, one of the finest medieval merchant houses in the city.

A few minutes west, **Petite France** is the postcard: the old tanners', millers' and fishermen's quarter, where half-timbered houses lean over the canals of the Ill and the **covered bridges** (Ponts Couverts) and the **Barrage Vauban** close the western end with a panoramic terrace over the water. It is touristy and it earns it — go early or late for the light and the quiet. Between here and the cathedral, the lanes of the Grande Île reward aimless wandering more than a checklist.

North-east, the **European district** is Strasbourg's other identity. The city is the official seat of the **European Parliament**, and the **Council of Europe** and the **European Court of Human Rights** sit nearby around the **Orangerie**, the city's oldest park, which has a little zoo, a boating lake and storks — the symbol of Alsace — nesting on the rooftops. You can visit the Parliament's public gallery outside session weeks; during a plenary the whole quarter, and the city's hotels, come alive.

Then there is the food, which for many people is the reason to come. **Alsatian cooking** is French method with a German accent: **tarte flambée** (flammekueche), the wafer-thin flatbread with crème fraîche, onion and lardons blistered in a wood oven; **choucroute garnie**, mounded sauerkraut with sausages and pork; **baeckeoffe**, the slow-cooked meat-and-potato casserole; pretzels, Munster cheese and kougelhopf. Eat it in a **winstub**, the wood-panelled Alsatian wine tavern, and drink the local **Riesling, Pinot Gris or Gewürztraminer**, or an Alsace beer. Book a table in the evening, especially at weekends and in December.

And use Strasbourg as a base. **Colmar** is 30 minutes south by frequent train — smaller, storybook-pretty, with the Unterlinden Museum and its own network of canals — and the **Alsace Wine Route** threads the vineyard villages of **Riquewihr, Eguisheim and Ribeauvillé** between and around the two cities, best explored by car or on a half-day tour. If your trip has four days in it, one or two spent in the wine country is time very well used.

## Getting Around Strasbourg

**Walk the centre.** The Grande Île and its immediate surroundings are compact, flat and largely car-free, and the cathedral, Petite France, the squares, the museums and dozens of restaurants are all within a ten-to-fifteen-minute walk of each other. For a two-day city break you will barely need anything else.

**Take the tram for the rest.** The **CTS** network is fast, frequent, clean and cheap, with several lines crossing the centre and connecting the station, the European district and the outer hotels — and one line, D, that runs across the Rhine into **Kehl in Germany**. Buy a single or a day ticket from the machine at any stop, and validate it as you board. It is the easy way to reach anything beyond walking distance, including the budget hotels out west and by the port.

**Consider a bike.** Strasbourg is one of France's best cycling cities — a dense network of protected lanes, flat terrain, and the **Vélhop** bike-share with hire points across the city. For reaching Petite France, the European district and the parks without waiting for a tram, it is genuinely the local way to move, and the riverside paths are a pleasure. Mind the pedestrian zones on the island, where bikes are restricted in the busiest stretches.

A few practicalities. The currency is the **euro**; cards and contactless work almost everywhere, but carry a little cash for market huts and some winstubs. **Tap water is safe to drink**, and a restaurant will bring a free carafe if you ask. **Tipping** is a rounded-up bonus, not an obligation — service is included by law. And plan around the two big demand events: the **Christkindelsmärik** in December and the **European Parliament session weeks** through the year both tighten rooms and lift prices, so check your dates against them before you book.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Strasbourg?**
Rarely, and not reliably. **Do UK passport holders need a visa for Strasbourg?**
No visa for tourism. UK passport holders can visit France and the rest of the Schengen area visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. Your passport needs to have been issued within the previous ten years and to be valid for at least three months after the day you plan to leave. **What are EES and ETIAS, and do they affect my trip?**
EES is the EU Entry/Exit System, a biometric border check that records a facial image and fingerprints instead of stamping your passport, and it has been rolling out in stages across Schengen external borders — expect a slightly slower first crossing while you are registered. ETIAS is a separate pre-travel authorisation that UK visitors will need to apply for online for a fee once it starts, and its launch date has been pushed back more than once. Because both timelines keep moving, check the UK government France travel advice page for the position on your own travel dates rather than trusting an older article.

**What currency does Strasbourg use?**
The euro. Alsace is French, so it is euros everywhere, and the German border town of Kehl across the Rhine uses euros too. Carry a small amount of cash for Christmas market huts, some winstubs and market stalls. Sterling is not accepted anywhere.

**Can you drink the tap water in Strasbourg?**
Yes. Tap water across France is treated to strict standards and Strasbourg is no exception, so fill a bottle at the hotel rather than buying plastic. Not really. Service is included in French menu prices by law, so a tip is a bonus rather than an obligation. Rounding up the bill or leaving a euro or two per person for good service in a winstub is normal and appreciated, and five to ten per cent is generous for a long, attentive meal.
