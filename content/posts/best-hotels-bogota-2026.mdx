  - q: "What does 'no dar papaya' mean?"
    a: "It is the most useful phrase in Colombia. Literally it means do not give papaya; in practice it means do not hand someone an easy opportunity. Colombians say it to each other constantly and it is not a comment on visitors — it is the everyday shorthand for keeping your phone in your pocket on the street, not leaving a bag on the back of a chair, not flashing cash at a market stall and not walking down a quiet road at 2am. Follow it and you are behaving exactly like a local. The companion phrase, 'papaya puesta, papaya partida', roughly means if you leave the papaya out, someone will take a slice."

  - q: "What is the Ciclovía?"
    a: "Every Sunday and on public holidays, Bogotá closes more than a hundred kilometres of major roads to cars from early morning until the early afternoon and hands them to cyclists, runners, skaters, dog walkers and families. It has been running for decades, it is genuinely enormous, and it has been copied by cities around the world. It costs nothing, bicycles are easy to hire, and riding up Carrera Séptima with the traffic gone is one of the best free things you can do in the city. Even if you do not cycle, walk a stretch of it — the street food stalls and the atmosphere are the point."
  - q: "How do I get up Monserrate?"
    a: "Monserrate is the mountain shrine that stands over the eastern edge of the city, topped by a white church and reached from a station behind La Candelaria. There are three ways up: the funicular railway, the cable car, and a steep stone footpath. The funicular and cable car take a few minutes and run on published timetables that vary by day, so check before you go; the walk is a serious climb at this altitude and is best done early, and it is not the thing to attempt on your first day off the plane. The view over the whole plateau is the definitive Bogotá photograph. Sunset is busiest and most spectacular."
  - q: "Is the Zipaquirá Salt Cathedral worth a day trip?"
    a: "Yes, it is the best day trip from Bogotá. The Salt Cathedral is a full church carved out of the tunnels of a working salt mine, roughly an hour north of the city, and the descent through dim halls of illuminated salt-rock crosses is genuinely strange and impressive. You can go by organised tour, by bus from the northern terminal, or on a tourist train at weekends. Allow most of a day with the travel, take a jumper because it is cold underground, and combine it with a wander round Zipaquirá's colonial main square."
  - q: "What is the Gold Museum and is it worth it?"
    a: "The Museo del Oro is the single best museum in Colombia and one of the finest in Latin America — tens of thousands of pieces of pre-Hispanic goldwork from the Muisca, Quimbaya, Tairona and other cultures, displayed with real care. The famous Muisca raft, a small golden model of a ceremonial rite on a lake, is the centrepiece. It sits in the centre a short walk from Plaza de Bolívar, entry is inexpensive and it is free on Sundays. Give it two hours minimum."
  - q: "Is the Botero Museum free?"
    a: "Yes. It occupies a beautiful colonial house with a courtyard, it is one of the most enjoyable hours in the old town, and it costs nothing. Check the current opening days before you go, as it closes one day a week."

  - q: "How many days do you need in Bogotá?"

  - q: "When is the best time to visit Bogotá?"
    a: "Bogotá has no summer and winter in the European sense — the temperature barely changes across the year — so you are choosing by rainfall, not heat. The drier windows are roughly December to March and July to August, which are the most reliable months for Monserrate views and for the Ciclovía. April to May and October to November are the wettest. Rain here usually means a heavy afternoon burst rather than a washed-out day, so a wet-season trip is far from ruined; just carry a jacket and plan museums for the afternoons."
  - q: "How do I get from El Dorado airport into the city?"
    a: "The straightforward options are an official taxi from the regulated rank in the arrivals hall, a pre-booked transfer, or a rideshare app. Use the official desk or an app rather than accepting an offer from someone approaching you inside the terminal — that is standard advice at any big international airport and it applies here. The airport is around 15km west of the centre and considerably further from the northern hotel districts, so allow between 30 minutes and well over an hour depending on traffic and the time of day.   - q: "How do I get around Bogotá?"

For most visitors the practical default is a rideshare app or a booked taxi, which is cheap by UK standards and the right call after dark. Bogotá is also a serious cycling city with a large network of bike lanes, and on Sundays the Ciclovía turns the main avenues over to bikes entirely."
  - q: "What should I eat in Bogotá?"

Breakfast might be changua, a milk and egg soup, or hot chocolate with a lump of cheese dropped in to melt, which is a Bogotá tradition worth trying once. The Paloquemao market is the place to eat fruit you have never seen before — lulo, guanábana, granadilla, curuba — and the Zona G is where the city's ambitious restaurants are."

## What to See in Bogotá

Start in **La Candelaria**, the colonial old town at the foot of the eastern mountains: narrow cobbled lanes, painted houses in ochre, blue and terracotta, university students spilling out of doorways, and some of the best street art in South America covering whole walls. It is the oldest part of the city and the most rewarding hour of aimless walking you will get here. Sitting at its heart is **Plaza de Bolívar**, the vast main square, with the cathedral on one side, the columned Capitolio Nacional on another and the modernist Palace of Justice on a third — the political centre of Colombia and a good place to watch the city go about its business.

The **Gold Museum (Museo del Oro)** is the reason many people come to Bogotá at all, and it deserves the reputation. Tens of thousands of pieces of pre-Hispanic goldwork from the Muisca, Quimbaya, Tairona and Calima cultures are displayed with real intelligence, culminating in the famous Muisca raft — a small golden model of the ceremonial rite that gave the world the legend of El Dorado. Entry is inexpensive, it is free on Sundays, and you should allow two hours. A few streets away, the **Botero Museum** holds the collection Fernando Botero gave to Colombia: his own instantly recognisable rounded figures, plus works he collected by Picasso, Monet, Dalí and Renoir, all in a beautiful colonial house around a courtyard. It is free.

**Monserrate** is the mountain shrine that stands over the eastern edge of the city, its white church visible from almost everywhere. Get up by funicular railway or cable car — or on foot up the steep stone path if you are properly acclimatised and start early — and the reward is the definitive view of Bogotá spreading across the plateau to the horizon. Do not make it your arrival-day activity; the summit is considerably higher than the city, and the city is already high.

On **Sundays and public holidays** the city does something no other capital does at this scale. The **Ciclovía** closes more than a hundred kilometres of major roads to cars from early morning until early afternoon and hands them over to cyclists, runners, skaters and families. It has run for decades, it is free, bicycles are easy to hire, and riding up a traffic-free Carrera Séptima is one of the great urban experiences in the Americas. The same morning, the **Usaquén flea market** fills the old village square in the north with crafts, antiques and food stalls.

For a different kind of spectacle, the **Paloquemao market** is where Bogotá actually shops: a hangar of flowers, herbs, meat, cheese and a fruit section full of things you have never seen — lulo, guanábana, granadilla, curuba, mangostino. Go early, eat breakfast there, and let a stallholder cut you slices of six things you cannot name. In the evenings, the **Zona Rosa** and the **Zona G** in the north are where the restaurants and bars are, from cheap and excellent to genuinely ambitious cooking, and where the city's specialty **coffee culture** is at its best — a relatively new thing in a country that historically exported its finest beans and kept the rest.

The best day trip is the **Zipaquirá Salt Cathedral**, roughly an hour north, where a full church has been carved out of the tunnels of a working salt mine: dim halls, illuminated crosses cut from rock salt, and a scale that is genuinely disorientating. Take a jumper — it is cold down there — and combine it with the colonial square in Zipaquirá itself. Closer to home, the **Botanical Garden**, the **Quinta de Bolívar** and the **National Museum** in its converted prison building all make good half-days when the afternoon rain arrives.

## Where to Stay in Bogotá: Choosing Your Base

**Zona Rosa and Parque de la 93 (Chicó)** is the usual first choice for visitors and rarely the wrong one. This is the smart northern quarter — the pedestrianised Zona T with its bars and shopping centres, the restaurant-ringed square at Parque de la 93, and the greatest concentration of well-maintained upmarket hotels in the city. It is the part of Bogotá that feels most comfortable to walk around in the evening, and where most of the five-stars in this guide sit. The trade-off is distance: the old town and the museums are a 30 to 45 minute ride south depending on traffic.

**Chapinero and Zona G** suit travellers who want to eat well and feel like they are in a real city rather than a hotel district. Zona G is the gastronomic pocket at the top of Chapinero, dense with good restaurants and specialty coffee; Chapinero proper runs south from it along the main corridor and is younger, scruffier, cheaper and more interesting, with the best independent bars and a lot of the city's nightlife. Several of the cheapest hotels in this guide are along this stretch, and it is the best-connected part of Bogotá for reaching both halves of the city.

**La Candelaria** gives you the most character per pound: colonial houses, street art, the Gold Museum and the Botero on your doorstep, and the cheapest hostels and guesthouses in Bogotá. It is where you should be based if the history is why you came. The honest version is that it is atmospheric by day and empties out at night, so plan on taking taxis or rideshare after dark rather than walking back through quiet streets, and do not treat it as a neighbourhood for late-night wandering. Plenty of travellers stay there very happily on exactly those terms.

**Usaquén** is the calm option — a village swallowed by the city's northern growth, with a colonial square, a church, cobbled lanes, good restaurants and a Sunday flea market that is one of the nicest mornings in Bogotá. It suits couples and older travellers who want quiet and greenery over nightlife, and it is a long way north of the historic centre, which is the whole point for some people and a dealbreaker for others.

**Near El Dorado (BOG) airport** makes sense for exactly one thing: an early departure or a late arrival. The airport is around 15km west of the centre and Bogotá traffic can turn that into an hour-plus, so an airport hotel genuinely solves a dawn flight. It is a business and industrial district with nothing to walk to, so do not book it for a holiday — book it for a night.

## Altitude and Weather — the Two Things Visitors Get Wrong

This is the section of the guide we would ask you not to skim, because almost everyone arrives in Bogotá with the wrong idea about both.

**Bogotá sits at roughly 2,640m — about 8,660ft — on a high Andean plateau.** That is higher than any city most European travellers will have visited, higher than the top of most Alpine ski resorts' villages, and high enough that your body notices. The usual experience is not dramatic: mild breathlessness on stairs, a dull headache on the first evening, broken sleep the first night, tiring faster than you expected, and alcohol hitting harder than usual. It passes. What makes it pass faster is entirely within your control — **take the first day easy**, drink plenty of water, eat lightly, go gently on the aguardiente for the first day or two, and do not schedule Monserrate, a football match or a long hike for your arrival day. Locals will offer you coca tea, which is the traditional remedy and perfectly legal in Colombia. If you have a heart or lung condition, or you are pregnant, talk to your GP or a travel clinic before booking rather than after arriving.

**And it is not hot.** Bogotá is only a few degrees from the equator, which leads people to pack for the tropics, and it is one of the most reliable ways to have a cold, miserable first evening. At 2,640m the city is **cool all year round** — mild in the daytime, genuinely chilly once the sun goes down, and with far more variation between noon and midnight than between January and July. Pack layers, a jumper or fleece, and a waterproof jacket; rain tends to arrive as a heavy afternoon burst rather than an all-day drizzle. The drier windows are roughly December to March and July to August. If you want beach heat, that is Cartagena, Santa Marta and the Caribbean coast, an hour and a half away by plane — which is exactly why so many people do both on one trip.

One practical consequence for choosing a hotel: air conditioning is close to irrelevant in Bogotá, and heating, hot water pressure and an extra blanket matter far more. Do not pay a premium for cooling you will never switch on.

## Staying Safe in Bogotá — Honest, Practical Advice

Colombia's reputation lags a long way behind its reality, and Bogotá deserves a straight answer rather than either scaremongering or a shrug. The country has changed enormously, tourism has grown accordingly, and the overwhelming majority of visits pass without incident. It is also true that street crime — phone snatching, bag theft, distraction robberies — is a real feature of parts of the city, and pretending otherwise does nobody any favours.

The local rule is **'no dar papaya'** — literally, do not give papaya; in practice, do not hand anyone an easy opportunity. Colombians say it to each other constantly, and following it is simply behaving like a resident. In practice that means: keep your **phone in your pocket** rather than navigating with it in your hand at a junction, which is the single most common way visitors get robbed. Leave the good watch and obvious jewellery at home. Carry the card and cash you need for the day and leave the rest, plus your passport, in the hotel safe. Do not hang a bag on the back of a chair. Use **Uber or another rideshare app, or a taxi booked through your hotel or an app, at night** rather than hailing one in the street. Stick to well-populated areas after dark, and be particularly deliberate about it in La Candelaria and the centre, which empty out in the evening. On crowded TransMilenio buses, keep your bag in front of you.

**Which area should I stay in in Bogotá?**

**Is Zona Rosa or Parque de la 93 a good base?**
Yes — between them they are the default answer for a first trip. Zona Rosa, built around the pedestrianised Zona T, is the nightlife and shopping heart of northern Bogotá, with bars, restaurants and two large shopping centres in walking distance. Parque de la 93 is a smarter, quieter square a few blocks north, ringed by restaurants and cafés and popular with business travellers and couples. Both sit in the northern half of the city, which is where the newer, better-maintained hotels are concentrated, and both are comfortable to walk around in the evening. The trade-off is distance: the colonial old town is a 30 to 45 minute ride south depending on traffic.

**Is La Candelaria a good place to stay?**
It depends what you want, and this deserves an honest answer. La Candelaria is the colonial old town — painted houses, steep cobbled lanes, street art, Plaza de Bolívar, the Gold Museum and the Botero Museum all on foot — and it has the city's cheapest hostels and guesthouses. By day it is the most interesting part of Bogotá. After dark it empties out quickly and it is not the area to be wandering unfamiliar streets, so plan on taxis or rideshare in the evening and do not walk back from a bar at midnight. Plenty of travellers stay there very happily on exactly those terms; if you would rather not think about it at all, stay north.

**Where should I stay in Bogotá on a budget?**
You have more options than most capitals. The £21 to £40 band in this guide covers real three-star hotel rooms with a staffed front desk, not just hostel beds, and they are spread across the central Santa Fe and museum district, the Chapinero corridor, the western neighbourhoods towards the airport, and even a few in Usaquén and the northern grid. If you want the cheapest bed nearest the sights, look at the museum-district hotels. If you want the cheapest bed in a comfortable neighbourhood, look at the Usaquén and northern options, which cost a few pounds more and are worth it.

**Should I stay near El Dorado airport?**
Only if your flight is very early or very late. El Dorado (BOG) sits roughly 15km west of the city centre, and Bogotá traffic is heavy enough that the journey can take anything from 30 minutes to well over an hour. An airport-area hotel solves that for a dawn departure or an overnight connection, and several are large, modern and well reviewed. For an actual holiday, though, the airport district is a business and industrial area with nothing to walk to — stay north or central and take a ride out on the day.

**What is the Ciclovía?**
Every Sunday and on public holidays, Bogotá closes more than a hundred kilometres of major roads to cars from early morning until the early afternoon and hands them to cyclists, runners, skaters, dog walkers and families. It has been running for decades, it is genuinely enormous, and it has been copied by cities around the world. It costs nothing, bicycles are easy to hire, and riding up Carrera Séptima with the traffic gone is one of the best free things you can do in the city. Even if you do not cycle, walk a stretch of it — the street food stalls and the atmosphere are the point.

**How do I get up Monserrate?**
Monserrate is the mountain shrine that stands over the eastern edge of the city, topped by a white church and reached from a station behind La Candelaria. There are three ways up: the funicular railway, the cable car, and a steep stone footpath. The funicular and cable car take a few minutes and run on published timetables that vary by day, so check before you go; the walk is a serious climb at this altitude and is best done early, and it is not the thing to attempt on your first day off the plane. The view over the whole plateau is the definitive Bogotá photograph. Sunset is busiest and most spectacular.

**Is the Zipaquirá Salt Cathedral worth a day trip?**
Yes, it is the best day trip from Bogotá. The Salt Cathedral is a full church carved out of the tunnels of a working salt mine, roughly an hour north of the city, and the descent through dim halls of illuminated salt-rock crosses is genuinely strange and impressive. You can go by organised tour, by bus from the northern terminal, or on a tourist train at weekends. Allow most of a day with the travel, take a jumper because it is cold underground, and combine it with a wander round Zipaquirá's colonial main square.

**What is the Gold Museum and is it worth it?**
The Museo del Oro is the single best museum in Colombia and one of the finest in Latin America — tens of thousands of pieces of pre-Hispanic goldwork from the Muisca, Quimbaya, Tairona and other cultures, displayed with real care. The famous Muisca raft, a small golden model of a ceremonial rite on a lake, is the centrepiece. It sits in the centre a short walk from Plaza de Bolívar, entry is inexpensive and it is free on Sundays. Give it two hours minimum.

**Is the Botero Museum free?**
Yes. The Museo Botero in La Candelaria is free to enter and holds the collection Fernando Botero donated to the country — his own instantly recognisable rounded figures alongside works he collected by Picasso, Monet, Dalí and others. It occupies a beautiful colonial house with a courtyard, it is one of the most enjoyable hours in the old town, and it costs nothing. Check the current opening days before you go, as it closes one day a week.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Bogotá?**
It helps a great deal. English is spoken at the upmarket hotels in the north and in tourist-facing restaurants and tour agencies, but far less widely than in Europe — expect little or none with taxi drivers, in neighbourhood cafés, at market stalls or on public transport. Colombian Spanish, and the Bogotá accent in particular, is often described as among the clearest in the Spanish-speaking world, which makes it a good place to try. Learn the courtesy phrases, keep an offline translation app on your phone, and you will be fine.

**Can I drink the tap water in Bogotá?**
Yes, generally — and this is unusual for the region, so it surprises people. Bogotá's water comes from high Andean reservoirs, is treated to a high standard and is widely drunk straight from the tap by residents. Plenty of visitors still prefer bottled out of habit or because of the taste, and that is a perfectly reasonable choice, but you do not need to brush your teeth with bottled water here. Outside the capital, particularly in rural and low-lying areas, the advice reverts to bottled or filtered.

**How many days do you need in Bogotá?**
Three full days is the sweet spot. Day one, take it gently for the altitude: La Candelaria on foot, the Gold Museum and the Botero Museum, Plaza de Bolívar and a long lunch. Day two, Monserrate in the morning, the Paloquemao market, and an evening in Zona G or Zona Rosa. Day three, the Zipaquirá Salt Cathedral, or Usaquén and the northern parks. **When is the best time to visit Bogotá?**
Bogotá has no summer and winter in the European sense — the temperature barely changes across the year — so you are choosing by rainfall, not heat. Rain here usually means a heavy afternoon burst rather than a washed-out day, so a wet-season trip is far from ruined; just carry a jacket and plan museums for the afternoons.

**How do I get from El Dorado airport into the city?**
The straightforward options are an official taxi from the regulated rank in the arrivals hall, a pre-booked transfer, or a rideshare app. Use the official desk or an app rather than accepting an offer from someone approaching you inside the terminal — that is standard advice at any big international airport and it applies here. **How do I get around Bogotá?**
TransMilenio, the red articulated-bus rapid transit system, runs in dedicated lanes along the main corridors and is by far the fastest way to cross the city at rush hour. It is also extremely crowded, and crowded buses are where pickpocketing happens, so keep bags in front of you and your phone away. For most visitors the practical default is a rideshare app or a booked taxi, which is cheap by UK standards and the right call after dark. Bogotá is also a serious cycling city with a large network of bike lanes, and on Sundays the Ciclovía turns the main avenues over to bikes entirely.

**What should I eat in Bogotá?**

Breakfast might be changua, a milk and egg soup, or hot chocolate with a lump of cheese dropped in to melt, which is a Bogotá tradition worth trying once.
