  - q: "What is the FMM tourist card and do I still need one?"
    a: "The FMM, or Forma Migratoria Multiple, is Mexico's tourist card — historically a paper form you filled in on the plane, handed half of to immigration on arrival and kept the other half to surrender on departure. Mexico has been moving to a digital and stamp-based system at many airports, including the busier ones, so you may not receive a paper slip at all. If you are given one, keep it somewhere safe with your passport for the whole trip, because losing it used to mean a fee and a queue on the way home. Either way, check the number of days written or stamped in your passport before you walk away from the desk."
  - q: "Where should I stay in Puerto Vallarta — which area?"

The Hotel Zone, or Zona Hotelera, is the strip of larger beachfront resorts north of the centre, where most of the all-inclusives sit.   - q: "When is the rainy season in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Roughly June to October, peaking in August and September. The bay and the hills behind it are spectacularly green in that window, prices drop hard, and restaurants that need booking weeks ahead in February will seat you straight away.   - q: "Is hurricane season a real risk in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "The Pacific hurricane season runs roughly mid-May to the end of November, with the highest risk in August, September and October. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than heavy afternoon rain, and Puerto Vallarta sits in a large, sheltered bay which offers some protection, but the risk is genuine and has produced serious storms in the past. If you travel then, take insurance that explicitly covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your plans, and follow local advice if a storm is forecast."

  - q: "When can I see whales in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Humpback whales migrate into Banderas Bay to calve and breed roughly from December to March, and the season is usually described locally as running from mid-December to late March. It is one of the best whale-watching bays in Mexico and sightings in the heart of the season are common rather than lucky — you can sometimes see spouts and breaches from the Malecon. Go with a licensed operator that follows the approach-distance rules, book a morning trip when the sea is calmest, and understand that nothing about wild animals is guaranteed."

Access is strictly limited to protect the site: visitor numbers are capped daily, the beach is closed on some days, only licensed operators with permits can take you, and reaching the beach itself involves a swim through a low tunnel with a life jacket. Book with a permitted operator well ahead and treat the Hidden Beach as a bonus rather than a guarantee — the snorkelling, the birdlife and the boat ride are worth the day on their own."
  - q: "What are the timeshare touts at Puerto Vallarta airport?"
    a: "This is the single most consistent complaint from first-time visitors, so know about it before you land. The rule is simple: do not stop, do not answer questions about your hotel, do not accept a free ride or a free breakfast, and keep walking until you are outside the terminal building where the official taxi and Uber pickup areas are. Hotels almost always provide purified drinking water, either bottles in the room or a refill dispenser in the lobby, restaurants use purified water and ice as standard, and brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine.   - q: "What currency should I use in Puerto Vallarta — pesos or dollars?"
    a: "The Mexican Peso (MXN) is the currency and it is what you should use. In Puerto Vallarta's old town, that money buys a genuinely large amount of food. Tacos from a street stand cost a couple of pounds. A full comida corrida — soup, main, rice, a drink — at a neighbourhood restaurant is a few pounds more. Two people eating three meals a day out of that £80 to £95 gap will, in most weeks, come out ahead — and they will have eaten in a different place every night in one of Mexico's best restaurant towns.

**So when does all-inclusive win?** Four clear cases:

- **Children.** Feeding a family of four in restaurants three times a day, every day, with the associated negotiation and waiting, is a completely different equation. All-inclusive usually wins outright.
- **Drinking.** If your holiday includes several cocktails a day, the gap closes fast — drinks in tourist-facing bars are the least cheap thing in Puerto Vallarta.
- **Staying put.** If the plan is a lounger, a pool and a book for seven days, walking out for lunch is friction you are paying to avoid.
- **Budget certainty.** Some people simply want one number paid before departure and no decisions afterwards. That is a legitimate reason and no spreadsheet overrides it.

**And when does room-only win?** If you came for Puerto Vallarta rather than for a resort. If you want Yelapa, the Marietas, Mismaloya, the Malecón at sunset and a different dinner every night. If you are a couple. If you are travelling in the cheap season and want to stretch a week into a fortnight. That is the majority of the UK travellers we see, which is why 29 of the 49 hotels on this page are budget rooms rather than resorts.

One more thing worth knowing: the all-inclusives cluster in the **Hotel Zone** and on the **southern coast road**, not in the old town. So the choice is not only about food. It is also about whether you sleep ten minutes from the Malecón or twenty minutes' bus ride from it.

## What to Do in Puerto Vallarta

- **Walk the Malecón** — the mile-long seafront promenade through the centre, lined with bronze sculptures, bars, buskers and the Los Arcos amphitheatre at its southern end. Do it at sunset on your first evening. It is free, it is the town's living room, and it will tell you more about Puerto Vallarta in an hour than any guidebook.
- **The Zona Romántica and Playa Los Muertos** — the cobbled old quarter south of the Río Cuale, the town's best beach for actually sitting on, the sculptural Los Muertos pier, and several hundred restaurants and bars in a few blocks. This is the heart of the town and, for most visitors, the reason to come back.
- **The Church of Our Lady of Guadalupe** — the crown-topped parish church in the old centre, the single most recognisable building in Puerto Vallarta and the landmark you will orient yourself by all week.
- **Isla Río Cuale** — the long, shaded island in the middle of the river dividing the old town, with craft stalls, cafés and a genuinely welcome drop in temperature under the trees. A five-minute detour that most day visitors miss.
- **Whale watching, December to March** — humpbacks come into Banderas Bay to calve and breed, and in the heart of the season you can sometimes see spouts from the shore. Go with a licensed operator in the morning when the sea is calmest.
- **Yelapa** — the roadless village on the southern shore of the bay, reached by water taxi from the Los Muertos pier or, more cheaply and quickly, from **Boca de Tomatlán** at the end of the coastal bus route. A waterfall behind the village, a beach in front of it, and no cars at all. Check the last boat back before you relax too thoroughly.
- **The Marietas Islands** — the protected island group at the mouth of the bay, famous for the **Hidden Beach** inside a collapsed crater. Access is capped, permitted and sometimes closed entirely, so book a licensed operator well ahead and treat the beach as a bonus on top of excellent snorkelling and seabird colonies.
- **Los Arcos and Mismaloya** — the offshore rock arches that form a protected marine park, and the cove where The Night of the Iguana was filmed in 1963, the production that put Puerto Vallarta on the map. Both are 20 to 30 minutes south on the cheap local bus, and Los Arcos is the bay's best snorkelling.
- **Boca de Tomatlán to Las Ánimas coastal walk** — a genuinely lovely, moderately demanding jungle path along the southern shore between beaches with no road access, ending at a beach restaurant. Take water and go early.
- **Sayulita and the Riviera Nayarit** — an hour or so north into the neighbouring state, where the surf towns start. Sayulita for beginner waves and colour, San Pancho for something quieter. Easy by bus, and a good contrast with Puerto Vallarta's polish.
- **Eat properly** — this is one of the best restaurant towns in Mexico and the range runs from a two-pound taco stand to serious contemporary cooking. Look for the busy places with Mexican customers in them, not the ones with a man outside holding a laminated menu.

## Where to Base Yourself in Puerto Vallarta

This decision matters less here than it does in Tulum, because Puerto Vallarta's cheap areas are also its best areas. But the four districts are genuinely different holidays.

**Zona Romántica (Old Town / Emiliano Zapata).** South of the Río Cuale, running down to Playa Los Muertos. Cobbled streets, terracotta roofs, small hotels, the highest concentration of restaurants and bars in town, the pier, beach clubs on the sand, and the centre of Puerto Vallarta's long-established LGBTQ+ scene around Olas Altas and Lázaro Cárdenas. **This is the right answer for most first-time visitors** — you can walk everywhere, you will eat well, and the hotels are small and cheap. The trade is noise and cobbles: it is lively late, and the hills behind the beach are steep, which matters with luggage, a pushchair or reduced mobility.

**Centro.** The old centre north of the river, behind the Malecón, around the crowned church. Slightly less restaurant-dense than the Zona Romántica and slightly cheaper still, with several of the sub-£50 hotels in this guide sitting a block or two off the promenade. Excellent for walkers and for anyone who wants to be in the middle of the town rather than beside a pool. Same caveat about evening noise near the Malecón itself.

**Zona Hotelera (the Hotel Zone).** The strip of larger beachfront resorts north of the centre, on Playa Las Glorias and the beaches around it, where most of the all-inclusives in this guide sit. Wide flat beach, big pools, buffets, and about **10 to 20 minutes by bus or taxi** from the old town. Right if you want a resort holiday with the town as an occasional excursion; wrong if you want the town as the point and the resort as a place to sleep.

**Marina Vallarta.** Further north again, near the airport: a planned district built around a yacht harbour, with a golf course, calmer streets and a slightly corporate feel. The airport run is minutes. The beach is not the town's best and the restaurant scene is thinner and more expensive. It suits golfers, families who want quiet, and travellers who value a short transfer over atmosphere.

**Conchas Chinas and the southern coast.** South of the Zona Romántica the hills come down to the sea and the coast turns rocky and green. This is the prettiest part of the bay, home to the top-end resorts and to a couple of the more interesting budget entries in this guide. Swimming means coves and rocks rather than a long flat beach, and you are on the bus or in a taxi to get to dinner.

**The honest summary:** if you are choosing on price and enjoyment together rather than price alone, the old town wins twice over. The cheapest rooms in this guide are also in the most interesting part of Puerto Vallarta. That is not true of very many beach destinations, and it is the single best thing about this one.

## Honest Practicalities for Puerto Vallarta

**Getting there is the hard part.** There is **no direct UK flight** to Puerto Vallarta and no realistic prospect of one. You will connect — through **Mexico City (MEX)** on a UK direct plus a domestic hop of around one and a half hours, or through a **US hub** such as Dallas/Fort Worth, Houston or Los Angeles, which is frequently the fastest option overall. Allow **14 to 16 hours door to door from London** and more from regional airports. If you route via the United States you will need to clear US immigration in transit and hold the appropriate US authorisation even though you are only connecting, so check that before you book a cheap-looking US itinerary. The upside comes at the other end: **PVR airport is 10 to 15 minutes from the Hotel Zone** and roughly 20 to 30 from the old town, so unlike Cancún there is no two-hour transfer waiting for you.

**Deal with the airport timeshare touts before they deal with you.** This is the most common complaint from first-time visitors and it is entirely avoidable. Inside the arrivals area at PVR there are desks and staff who present as tourist information, transport assistance or hotel greeters. They will ask where you are staying and offer a free or cut-price transfer. What they are selling is a long timeshare presentation. **Do not stop, do not answer, do not accept a free ride**, and keep walking until you are outside the terminal building where the official taxi ranks and rideshare pickups are. Thirty seconds of being politely unmoveable saves a morning of your holiday.

**Understand the seasons, because they change the price by half.** **November to April** is the dry season: warm, low humidity, barely any rain, calm seas, and **humpback whales in the bay from roughly mid-December to late March**. It is also the busy expensive stretch, with Christmas, New Year and Easter as hard peaks. **June to October** is the rainy season — hot, humid, dramatically green, with heavy downpours usually arriving in the late afternoon or overnight rather than all day. Rates in that window are frequently a third to a half below February. The **Pacific hurricane season** runs roughly mid-May to the end of November with the peak risk in August, September and October; most trips see nothing worse than rain, and the bay offers some shelter, but take insurance that covers weather disruption and keep some flexibility.

**No sargassum.** Worth stating plainly, because it is the strongest single argument for this coast over the Caribbean one: the seaweed that can bury the beaches at Cancún, Playa del Carmen and Tulum between roughly April and August is an Atlantic phenomenon and does not reach the Pacific. Puerto Vallarta's sand is golden-grey rather than powder-white and the water is deep blue rather than turquoise, but it is clear of weed all year.

**Everyday sense.** Pay in **Mexican Pesos (MXN)** rather than US dollars — dollars are widely accepted here because of the American and Canadian visitor volume, but the rate is consistently poor. Use ATMs inside bank branches, decline any card machine that offers to charge you in pounds, and carry small notes for tips, which are expected at around 15 percent in restaurants and for bar staff, housekeeping, guides and boat crew. **Drink bottled or filtered water**, not tap. Agree taxi fares before setting off since taxis are not metered, or use Uber, which operates here. Take the cheap local buses north to the Hotel Zone and Marina or south to Mismaloya and Boca de Tomatlán — they are one of the great bargains of the town. Be careful swimming where there is surf, because some southern beaches have a real undertow. And before you book anything, read the current [UK government travel advice for Mexico](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mexico), which carries state-by-state guidance that changes and has its own entry for Jalisco; for a wider look at what else the country offers beyond Banderas Bay, the official tourism site [Visit Mexico](https://visitmexico.com/) is a reasonable starting point.

## Puerto Vallarta Hotels FAQs

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Puerto Vallarta?**

No. There is no direct scheduled service between any UK airport and Puerto Vallarta, and there has not been one for years. Every UK routing involves at least one connection. The three sensible options are: connect through Mexico City (MEX) on a UK-Mexico direct and take the short domestic hop west to Puerto Vallarta; connect through a US hub such as Dallas/Fort Worth, Houston or Los Angeles, which is often the quickest total journey; or connect through a European or Canadian hub. Total door-to-door time is realistically 14 to 16 hours from London, and more from regional UK airports.

**How long does it take to get from the UK to Puerto Vallarta?**

Budget 14 to 16 hours in total from London including the connection, and add two to four hours if you are starting from Manchester, Edinburgh, Birmingham or Glasgow. The flying itself is roughly 11 to 12 hours to Mexico City or a US hub, plus a connection of one to three hours, plus a further one and a half to three hours on the onward leg depending which hub you used. Puerto Vallarta is on Mexico Central Time, which is six hours behind UK summer time, so an early departure from London usually lands you in Puerto Vallarta the same afternoon or evening.

**Which airport do I fly into for Puerto Vallarta?**

Licenciado Gustavo Diaz Ordaz International Airport, code PVR, sits about 10 to 15 minutes north of the Hotel Zone and roughly 20 to 30 minutes from the Zona Romantica depending on traffic. It is small, modern enough and genuinely convenient — this is one of the few Mexican beach destinations where the airport is effectively in town rather than an hour away. If you are also considering Guadalajara (GDL), it is around four to five hours away by road and only makes sense if you are combining the two cities.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Mexico?**

UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Mexico. You clear immigration on arrival and the officer grants you a specific number of days — check what they have written or stamped before you leave the desk, because it is not automatically the maximum. Rules and passport-validity requirements change, so confirm the current position on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly, and make sure your passport has plenty of validity left.

**What is the FMM tourist card and do I still need one?**

The FMM, or Forma Migratoria Multiple, is Mexico's tourist card — historically a paper form you filled in on the plane, handed half of to immigration on arrival and kept the other half to surrender on departure. Mexico has been moving to a digital and stamp-based system at many airports, including the busier ones, so you may not receive a paper slip at all. If you are given one, keep it somewhere safe with your passport for the whole trip, because losing it used to mean a fee and a queue on the way home. Either way, check the number of days written or stamped in your passport before you walk away from the desk.

**Where should I stay in Puerto Vallarta — which area?**

There are four practical choices and they suit very different trips. The Zona Romantica, also called Old Town or Emiliano Zapata, sits south of the Rio Cuale around Playa Los Muertos and is the most walkable, most characterful and most restaurant-dense part of town — cobbled streets, small hotels, the pier, and the heart of Puerto Vallarta's LGBTQ+ scene. Centro is the old centre around the Malecon and the church, walkable and central with cheap rooms. The Hotel Zone, or Zona Hotelera, is the strip of larger beachfront resorts north of the centre, where most of the all-inclusives sit. And Marina Vallarta, further north near the airport, is the planned yacht-harbour district with golf, calmer streets and a slightly corporate feel. For a first visit, the Zona Romantica is almost always the right answer.

**What is the Zona Romantica and who should stay there?**

The Zona Romantica is the southern old-town quarter, running from the Rio Cuale down to Playa Los Muertos and up the hill behind it. It is the part of Puerto Vallarta that people fall in love with: narrow cobbled streets, terracotta roofs, hundreds of restaurants and bars within a few blocks, beach clubs on Los Muertos, the sculptural Los Muertos pier, and a long-established, genuinely welcoming LGBTQ+ scene concentrated around Olas Altas and Lazaro Cardenas. It suits couples, solo travellers, foodies and anyone who wants to walk everywhere. It suits you less if you want a big resort pool complex and a swim-up bar, because those are mostly further north.

**Should I stay in the Hotel Zone or the Marina instead?**

Stay in the Hotel Zone if the point of your trip is a large beachfront resort with pools, buffets and a lounger — it is a strip of big properties on Playa Las Glorias and the beaches either side, about 10 to 20 minutes by bus or taxi from the old town, and it is where most of the all-inclusives in this guide sit. Stay in Marina Vallarta if you want calm, golf, a yacht harbour to walk around in the evening and a very short airport run, and you do not mind that the restaurant scene is thinner and the beach is not the town's best. **What is the Malecon in Puerto Vallarta?**

The Malecon is the seafront promenade running along the centre of town, roughly a mile of paved walkway between the sea and the old centre, lined with bronze sculptures, bars, restaurants, street performers and, at the southern end, the Los Arcos amphitheatre. Walking it at sunset is the single most reliably enjoyable free thing to do in Puerto Vallarta, and it is the axis the whole town organises itself around. **When is the best time to visit Puerto Vallarta?**

November to April is the dry season and it is close to perfect — warm days, low humidity, almost no rain, and the sea calm enough for boat trips. It is also the busy, expensive stretch, with Christmas, New Year and Easter as the hard peaks. December to March adds humpback whales in the bay, which is a genuine reason to travel then. May and November are the best-value shoulder months. June to October is the rainy season, which is hot, humid, green and considerably cheaper.

**When is the rainy season in Puerto Vallarta?**

Roughly June to October, peaking in August and September. It does not usually rain all day — the classic pattern is hot, bright, humid mornings with heavy tropical downpours and thunderstorms in the late afternoon or overnight, which is why plenty of experienced visitors happily travel then. The bay and the hills behind it are spectacularly green in that window, prices drop hard, and restaurants that need booking weeks ahead in February will seat you straight away. The trade is humidity, mosquitoes, rougher seas on some days and the small but real chance of a tropical storm.

**Is hurricane season a real risk in Puerto Vallarta?**

The Pacific hurricane season runs roughly mid-May to the end of November, with the highest risk in August, September and October. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than heavy afternoon rain, and Puerto Vallarta sits in a large, sheltered bay which offers some protection, but the risk is genuine and has produced serious storms in the past. If you travel then, take insurance that explicitly covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your plans, and follow local advice if a storm is forecast.

**Is it worth going to Puerto Vallarta in the rainy season to save money?**

For a lot of UK travellers, yes — and this is where the real bargains in this guide live. Room rates in the June-to-October window are frequently a third to a half below the February peak, the town is quiet, the landscape is at its most dramatic, and the mornings are usually fine. You are trading dry certainty for cost. If you are travelling with small children, if you cannot afford to lose a day to weather, or if the whole point is a beach lounger, pay the extra and go in the dry season instead.

**When can I see whales in Puerto Vallarta?**

Humpback whales migrate into Banderas Bay to calve and breed roughly from December to March, and the season is usually described locally as running from mid-December to late March. It is one of the best whale-watching bays in Mexico and sightings in the heart of the season are common rather than lucky — you can sometimes see spouts and breaches from the Malecon. Go with a licensed operator that follows the approach-distance rules, book a morning trip when the sea is calmest, and understand that nothing about wild animals is guaranteed.

**How do I get to Yelapa from Puerto Vallarta?**

Yelapa is a small village on the southern shore of Banderas Bay with no road connection, so you reach it by boat. Water taxis run from the Los Muertos pier in the Zona Romantica and from Boca de Tomatlan, the little cove at the southern end of the bay bus route, with the Boca crossing being shorter and cheaper. Expect a rough guide of 45 minutes or so from Los Muertos and around half an hour from Boca, weather depending. Check the last return departure carefully before you go, take cash, and consider staying a night if the place gets under your skin — plenty of people do.

**What are the Marietas Islands and can I see the Hidden Beach?**

The Marietas are a protected group of uninhabited islands at the mouth of Banderas Bay, a national park and an important seabird colony, best known for Playa del Amor — the Hidden Beach, a sand beach inside a collapsed crater open to the sky. Access is strictly limited to protect the site: visitor numbers are capped daily, the beach is closed on some days, only licensed operators with permits can take you, and reaching the beach itself involves a swim through a low tunnel with a life jacket. Book with a permitted operator well ahead and treat the Hidden Beach as a bonus rather than a guarantee — the snorkelling, the birdlife and the boat ride are worth the day on their own.

**What are the timeshare touts at Puerto Vallarta airport?**

This is the single most consistent complaint from first-time visitors, so know about it before you land. The arrivals area at PVR contains booths and staff who present themselves as tourist information, transport desks or hotel check-in assistance, and who will ask which hotel you are staying at and offer you a free or heavily discounted transfer. The transfer is real; the price is a 90-minute or longer timeshare presentation the next morning. The rule is simple: do not stop, do not answer questions about your hotel, do not accept a free ride or a free breakfast, and keep walking until you are outside the terminal building where the official taxi and Uber pickup areas are. Being politely firm once at the door saves an entire morning of your holiday.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Puerto Vallarta?**

Stick to bottled or filtered water. Puerto Vallarta's municipal water has a better local reputation than most of Mexico's and many residents drink it, but as a visitor you have no immunity to the local microbiology and it is not worth the risk on a short holiday. Hotels almost always provide purified drinking water, either bottles in the room or a refill dispenser in the lobby, restaurants use purified water and ice as standard, and brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine. Bring a reusable bottle and use the refill stations.

**Is Puerto Vallarta safe for tourists?**

Puerto Vallarta is one of Mexico's most established and most visited resort towns and the overwhelming majority of trips are completely trouble free, with the usual tourist-town issues — opportunistic theft, drink pricing, taxi overcharging — rather than anything more dramatic. Sensible precautions still apply: use official taxis or Uber rather than unmarked cars, agree taxi fares before setting off, use ATMs inside bank branches, do not carry more cash than you need, watch your drink, and take care swimming where there is surf, because some beaches south of town have a real undertow. Read the current UK government travel advice for Mexico before you travel, because it carries state-by-state guidance that changes and Jalisco has its own entry.

**Is Puerto Vallarta LGBTQ+ friendly?**

Puerto Vallarta is one of the best-known and longest-established LGBTQ+ destinations in Latin America, and the scene is concentrated in the Zona Romantica around Olas Altas, Lazaro Cardenas and the southern end of Playa Los Muertos, where there are dedicated beach clubs, bars, and a number of hotels that market to gay travellers specifically. The atmosphere in that part of town is relaxed and mainstream rather than niche. As anywhere, attitudes are more conservative outside the tourist areas and in smaller towns inland, so read the room when you travel further afield.

**How do I get around Puerto Vallarta?**

The old town — Centro and the Zona Romantica — is comfortably walkable, and that is the main reason to stay there. For longer distances the local buses are cheap, frequent and genuinely useful: routes run north along the Hotel Zone to the Marina and the airport, and south along the coast road to Mismaloya and Boca de Tomatlan for the Yelapa boats. Taxis are widely available and are not metered, so agree the fare before you get in; Uber also operates here and removes the negotiation. Water taxis from Los Muertos pier reach the southern beaches with no road access. Note that the old town is steep and cobbled in places, which matters if you have mobility issues or a pushchair.

**Do I need a hire car in Puerto Vallarta?**

Not for a normal beach and town holiday. Everything in the old town is walkable, the buses cover the Hotel Zone, the Marina, the airport and the southern coast road cheaply, and Uber fills the gaps. A car earns its keep if you want to explore the Riviera Nayarit north of the bay — Sayulita, San Pancho, Punta Mita — on your own schedule, or drive inland to the mountain towns. If you do hire one, be aware that Mexican third-party liability insurance is compulsory and is usually charged locally on top of the online rate, that parking in the old town is genuinely difficult, and that Mexico drives on the right.

**What currency should I use in Puerto Vallarta — pesos or dollars?**

The Mexican Peso (MXN) is the currency and it is what you should use. US dollars are widely accepted in Puerto Vallarta because of the volume of American and Canadian visitors, but the exchange rate you are given is almost always poor and change frequently comes back in pesos at the seller's own rate. Pay in pesos, withdraw from ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than freestanding machines in shops, carry small notes for taxis and tips, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, always decline and let your own bank do the conversion.

**How much should I tip in Puerto Vallarta?**

Tipping is customary and staff rely on it. Around 15 percent is the norm in restaurants, and you should check the bill first because some places add a service charge — propina — and you do not want to pay twice. Bar staff, hotel housekeeping, tour guides, boat crew, airport porters and the people who bag your shopping at the supermarket are all tipped. Tip in pesos rather than dollars where you can, and carry small notes specifically for it, because nobody will have change for a large one.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Puerto Vallarta?**

No. Spanish is the language, but Puerto Vallarta has been an international resort town for over half a century and English is spoken almost everywhere a visitor goes — hotels, restaurants, tour offices, shops and taxis. A few polite words are still worth having and are always appreciated: hola, por favor, gracias, la cuenta por favor. Away from the tourist areas and in the villages inland, English drops off quickly.

**Is Puerto Vallarta good for families?**

Yes, and it is a noticeably easier family destination than some of its rivals. The bay is sheltered so the sea is calmer than the open Pacific, the Hotel Zone all-inclusives are set up for children with pools and kids clubs, boat trips and whale watching work well with older children, and the airport is close enough that the transfer does not destroy the first day. The complications are that a meaningful share of Puerto Vallarta's best-known hotels are adults-only, that the old town's cobbles and steep streets are hard work with a pushchair, and that some southern beaches have a strong undertow. Families usually do best in the Hotel Zone or the Marina, or in a family-suite hotel in Centro.

**How many days do you need in Puerto Vallarta?**

Given the length of the journey from the UK, seven nights is the sensible minimum and ten to fourteen is better. That gives you time for a boat trip to Yelapa or the Marietas, a day at the southern beaches, a whale-watching morning if you are travelling in season, a day doing nothing on a lounger and several evenings on the Malecon and in the old town's restaurants. Four or five nights works only if Puerto Vallarta is one stop on a wider Mexico route, and even then the flying-to-holiday ratio is poor.

**Is there sargassum seaweed in Puerto Vallarta?**

No — and this is one of the strongest arguments for the Pacific coast over the Caribbean one. Sargassum is an Atlantic and Caribbean phenomenon, so the beaches around Puerto Vallarta are not affected by it at all, unlike Cancun, Playa del Carmen and Tulum which can be badly hit between roughly April and August. Puerto Vallarta's beaches are golden-grey volcanic-influenced sand rather than the powdery white of the Caribbean, and the water is a deeper blue rather than turquoise, but you will not be looking at a wall of rotting weed.

**What is Sayulita and can I visit from Puerto Vallarta?**

Sayulita is a small surf town about an hour to an hour and a half north of Puerto Vallarta in the neighbouring state of Nayarit, known for beginner-friendly waves, colourful streets and a bohemian, backpacker-meets-boutique atmosphere. It is an easy day trip by bus from the north bus station or by car, and it makes a good contrast with Puerto Vallarta's larger, more polished resort scene. Note that Nayarit has historically observed a different time zone from Jalisco at some points, so check local times before you plan a return bus.

**Can I visit Mismaloya and Los Arcos from Puerto Vallarta?**

Yes, both are easy. Mismaloya is a cove about 20 to 30 minutes south of town on the coast road, reachable by the cheap local bus, and it is where The Night of the Iguana was filmed in the 1960s — the film that turned Puerto Vallarta into a resort town in the first place. Los Arcos is the cluster of rock arches and islets just offshore nearby, a protected marine area and the town's best snorkelling and diving site, visited by boat trip or by kayak from the shore. Both make comfortable half-days.
