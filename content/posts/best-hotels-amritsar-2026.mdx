  - q: "What is the langar at the Golden Temple?"

## Visiting the Golden Temple — a short, practical note

Harmandir Sahib is a living place of worship, not a monument, and it is the holiest site in Sikhism. Tens of thousands of people come every day to pray, and you are joining them as a guest. The etiquette is simple and nobody will make it difficult for you.

**Cover your head** — men and women alike. Scarves are provided free at the entrance if you have not brought one, and cheap cotton ones are sold all around the complex. **Remove your shoes and socks** at the free shoe deposit, where volunteers take them and hand you a token; it costs nothing and moves quickly. **Wash your feet** in the shallow channel of water at the entrance before you step inside. **No alcohol, no tobacco and no drugs** anywhere in the complex, and do not smoke immediately outside it either. **Dress modestly**, with shoulders and legs covered.

Inside, walk clockwise around the *sarovar*, the sacred tank. Keep your voice down. Do not sit with your feet pointing towards the sanctum. **Photography** is generally accepted around the outer walkway but not inside the inner shrine, rules tighten during ceremonies, and you should never photograph someone praying without asking — look for the signs and follow what the volunteers say. If you are unsure about anything, ask; the *sevadars* who run the complex are used to first-time visitors and are unfailingly kind about it.

Go at **dawn**, when the marble is cold underfoot and the morning prayers carry across the water, and go again **at night**, when the gilded shrine is lit and doubled in the reflection. Most people who do both say the second visit is the one they remember.

## What to See and Do in Amritsar

The **Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib)** is the reason to come and will take up more of your time than you planned — it is free, open to everyone of any faith, and open around the clock. Inside the same complex is the **langar**, the world's largest free community kitchen, which serves tens of thousands of meals a day to anyone who sits down, at no charge and with no questions asked. Visitors are welcome to eat there and welcome to volunteer — an hour washing steel plates or rolling chapatis alongside the regulars is, for many people, the single best thing they do in India.

Immediately next door is **Jallianwala Bagh**, the walled garden where British troops fired on an unarmed crowd in 1919. It is now a national memorial with the bullet marks preserved, and it is a sombre place — visit quietly, and read about it beforehand if you are British. The **Partition Museum**, in the old Town Hall, covers the 1947 division of Punjab through survivors' testimony and is one of the most affecting museums in the country.

Beyond that: the **Wagah border ceremony** each evening at the Attari–Wagah crossing, an hour out of town, where guards on both sides perform a theatrical flag-lowering drill to roaring crowds — go early, seating is free and unreserved and fills hours ahead. The **Durgaina Temple**, often called the Durgiana Temple, is a Hindu shrine built in a similar tank-and-causeway form to the Golden Temple. **Gobindgarh Fort** is a restored eighteenth-century fort now run as a heritage attraction with museums and shows. And then there is the food: **Amritsari kulcha** with chole and a slab of butter, thick **lassi** in a heavy glass, Amritsari fried fish, and a proper roadside **dhaba** meal of dal and hot roti. Punjab feeds you generously and takes it personally if you stop early.

## Where to Stay in Amritsar

**Right by the Golden Temple** is the choice for most first-time visitors. The complex is at its best at dawn and again late at night, and staying five minutes away is what makes both of those effortless. The immediate area is dense, busy and mostly small independent hotels — you trade polish for position, and it is usually the right trade.

**The Heritage Walk and Hall Bazaar area** keeps almost all of that walkability with more choice. The Heritage Walk is a restored pedestrian route through the old city, and hotels along it — including the Fortune Inn and the Bloom and SureStay properties in this guide — let you reach the temple on foot while sleeping somewhere a notch smarter.

**Ranjit Avenue and the Mall** are the modern city: wider roads, the newer restaurants and malls, and most of the four-star hotels. It is quieter and greener, and the temple is a short and cheap autorickshaw ride away. Choose it if you would rather have a proper hotel room and take a ten-minute ride than be in the thick of it.

**Near the airport** makes sense for one night at the start or end of a trip — Welcomhotel Raja Sansi and Ramada Encore both sit that way. It is not a base for sightseeing, but a short transfer at 5am is worth a lot.

## Amritsar Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Amritsar?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party "visa services" charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Amritsar?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work in hotels and larger restaurants, but carry cash for autorickshaws, bazaar shopping and dhabas.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Amritsar?**
Amritsar's Sri Guru Ram Dass Jee International Airport (ATQ) has limited direct international service, and the honest answer is that most UK visitors connect. The usual routings are through Delhi on a domestic hop, or through a Gulf hub such as Dubai or Doha. Direct UK services to Amritsar have come and gone over the years, so check what is actually flying on your dates rather than assuming.

**Can I get to Amritsar from Delhi overland?**
Yes, and plenty of people do. Amritsar is roughly six hours from Delhi by fast train or by road, and the train is comfortable, cheap and scenic in a flat, agricultural way. If you are already flying into Delhi it is often simpler than adding a domestic connection, and it drops you at Amritsar Junction, which is close to the Golden Temple.

**When is the best time to visit Amritsar?**
October to March. That is the cool, dry, comfortable stretch when walking the old city and sitting by the Golden Temple's tank is a pleasure rather than an endurance test. December and January can be genuinely cold at night in Punjab, so pack a warm layer, but the days are clear and bright.

**How hot does Amritsar get in summer?**
Extremely hot. April to June is the hardest window in Punjab, with daytime temperatures that make midday sightseeing unwise. If you come then, do the Golden Temple at dawn and after dark, rest through the afternoon, drink far more water than you think you need, and pick a hotel where the air conditioning is a priority rather than a bonus.

**When is the monsoon in Amritsar?**
Roughly July to September. It is not as relentless as coastal India, but it brings heavy bursts of rain, high humidity and slower traffic. Room rates are at their lowest, the countryside is green, and the Golden Temple in the rain is a memorable sight — just build slack into your plans and pack proper waterproofs.

**Is the Golden Temple free to enter?**
Yes. Harmandir Sahib is free to enter, there is no ticket and no charge for the shoe deposit or the head covering, and it is open around the clock. Donations are voluntary. It is a living place of worship first and a sight second, and it is worth arriving with that in mind.

**Can non-Sikhs visit the Golden Temple?**
Yes — everyone is welcome, of any faith or none. That openness is part of the point: the complex has entrances on four sides, historically understood as a sign that it is open to all. Visitors are asked to observe the same simple etiquette as everyone else, and that is all that is expected of you.

**What should I wear to the Golden Temple?**
Dress modestly with shoulders and legs covered, and cover your head — this applies to men and women alike. Scarves are available free at the entrance if you have not brought one, and many visitors buy a cheap cotton scarf in the bazaar anyway. Avoid anything revealing or slogan-heavy. Comfortable clothes you can sit on the floor in are the practical choice.

**What is the etiquette at the Golden Temple?**
Cover your head, remove your shoes and socks at the free shoe deposit, wash your feet in the shallow channel at the entrance, and walk clockwise around the tank. No alcohol, no tobacco, no drugs anywhere in the complex, and do not smoke nearby either. Keep your voice low, do not point your feet at the sanctum, and follow the direction of the volunteers, who are unfailingly patient with first-time visitors.

**Where do I leave my shoes at the Golden Temple?**
At the free shoe deposit counters by the entrances, run by volunteers. You hand your shoes over and receive a token. It costs nothing, it is well organised, and it moves fast even when the complex is busy. Socks come off too, so slip-on shoes make life easier.

**Can I take photographs at the Golden Temple?**
Photography is generally allowed around the outer walkway and the tank, but not inside the inner sanctum, and rules can be tightened at busy times or during ceremonies. Look for the posted signs, follow what the volunteers tell you, and never photograph people at prayer without asking. Be a visitor before you are a photographer.

**What is the langar at the Golden Temple?**
The langar is the free community kitchen, and it is the largest in the world — it serves tens of thousands of meals every single day, to anybody who comes, free of charge, seated together on the floor regardless of background. Visitors are genuinely welcome to eat there, and welcome to volunteer washing dishes or rolling chapatis. It is one of the most moving things you can do in India.

**Do I have to pay for the langar?**
No. The langar is free to everyone and always has been. Donations are voluntary and never solicited from you at the door. If you want to give something back, volunteering in the kitchen for an hour is welcomed as warmly as money.

**What is Jallianwala Bagh?**
A walled public garden immediately beside the Golden Temple complex, and the site of the 1919 massacre in which British troops fired on an unarmed crowd. It is now a national memorial, with the bullet marks preserved in the walls. It is a sombre place and should be visited quietly. For British visitors in particular it is worth reading about beforehand.

**What is the Wagah border ceremony?**
The daily flag-lowering ceremony at the Attari–Wagah crossing on the India–Pakistan border, about an hour's drive from Amritsar. Border guards on both sides perform a highly theatrical, foot-stamping drill to enormous, cheering crowds, and it ends with the flags being lowered together at sunset. It is loud, patriotic and unlike anything else.

**How do I see the Wagah border ceremony?**
Book a car or a shared tour from Amritsar — almost every hotel can arrange one — and go early. Seating is free but unreserved and fills up long before the ceremony starts, so leaving two to three hours ahead is normal. Take your passport, carry as little as possible because bags are restricted, and check locally that the ceremony is running on your date.

**How many days do I need in Amritsar?**
Two full days is plenty for most visitors. Day one for the Golden Temple at dawn, the langar, Jallianwala Bagh and the old city bazaars; day two for the Partition Museum, Gobindgarh Fort or Durgiana Temple, and the Wagah ceremony in the late afternoon. A third day only if you want to go back to the temple repeatedly, which many people do.

**Which area should I stay in in Amritsar?**
Right by the Golden Temple if the temple is why you came — you can walk out at 5am for the dawn ceremony and back for breakfast, which is the whole point. The Heritage Walk and Hall Bazaar streets keep that walkability with a bit more choice. Ranjit Avenue and the Mall are where the modern hotels and restaurants are, and near the airport suits an early flight.

**Is it worth staying right next to the Golden Temple?**
For most first-time visitors, yes. The complex is at its most extraordinary at dawn and again late at night when it is lit and reflected in the water, and being a five-minute walk away is what makes both easy. The trade-off is that the immediate area is dense, busy and mostly small hotels — quieter and smarter rooms are out on Ranjit Avenue.

**Can I drink the tap water in Amritsar?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only, and go easy on ice at street stalls until your stomach has adjusted. Hotels almost always supply bottled water in the room. Punjabi food is one of the best reasons to be here, and busy places with high turnover are usually the safest bet.

**What food should I eat in Amritsar?**
Amritsari kulcha — a stuffed, crisp, baked flatbread served with chole and butter — is the local dish and worth queuing for. Then a thick sweet or salted lassi in a heavy glass, Amritsari fried fish, and a proper roadside dhaba meal of dal, roti and butter. Punjab does not do restraint at the table, and that is the point.
