  - q: "When is the best time to see the northern lights from Reykjavík?"
    a: "The aurora season runs roughly September to April, when nights are long and dark. October, February and March tend to give the best mix of clear skies and strong activity. You need darkness, a clear sky and a bit of luck — Reykjavík's own light pollution washes out weaker displays, so most people book a lights tour that drives 30–60 minutes out to Thingvellir or the Reykjanes coast. Check the Icelandic Met Office aurora forecast (en.vedur.is) each evening."
  - q: "Can you actually see the northern lights from inside the city?"
    a: "On a strong night, yes — from the darker edges like Grótta lighthouse on the Seltjarnarnes tip, or Perlan hill, you can catch a good display without leaving town. But Reykjavík's street lighting kills the fainter greens, so for anything reliable you want to get out to the countryside. Several hotels here (Reykjavik Lights, Fosshotel) run a free aurora wake-up call if the forecast spikes overnight."

Most UK visitors use Reykjavík as a 3–4 night base and drive out each day rather than moving hotels."

There's no train; you either pre-book the Flybus/Airport Direct coach (around 45 minutes to the BSÍ terminal, with hotel drop-off add-ons), grab a taxi (expensive, £100+), or pick up a hire car at the airport.   - q: "How do I get from the airport to my Reykjavík hotel?"
    a: "The Flybus and Airport Direct coaches meet every flight and run to the central bus terminal, with an optional door-to-door drop at most city hotels for a few pounds more. Pre-book online — it's cheaper and guarantees a seat. If you're hiring a car (recommended for the Golden Circle and South Coast), collect it at the airport and drive Route 41 straight in.   - q: "Do I need a hire car to stay in Reykjavík?"

  - q: "What is the Golden Circle and how do I do it from Reykjavík?"
    a: "The Golden Circle is a 300 km loop of three headline sights — Thingvellir National Park (where two tectonic plates meet), the Geysir geothermal area, and Gullfoss waterfall — all within 1.5–2 hours of the city. It's the classic Iceland day trip. Self-drive it in a normal 2WD car in a full day, or take a guided coach or minibus tour with hotel pickup. Many people bolt on the Secret Lagoon or Kerid crater."
  - q: "Is the Blue Lagoon worth it, and how far is it?"
    a: "The Blue Lagoon sits on the Reykjanes peninsula about 20 minutes from Keflavík Airport and 45 minutes from the city — geothermal seawater at a milky 38–40°C. It's touristy and pricey, and you must pre-book a timed entry slot (it sells out), but the setting is genuinely striking. Many visitors do it on airport day to save a separate trip. If you want a cheaper, more local soak, Reykjavík's public geothermal pools cost a few pounds."
  - q: "What are Reykjavík's geothermal pools and how do they work?"
    a: "Every neighbourhood has a public geothermal pool — Laugardalslaug (the biggest), Vesturbæjarlaug and Sundhöllin are the classics — with hot pots, saunas and lane swimming, all heated by natural geothermal water for a few pounds' entry. The etiquette is strict: you must shower thoroughly, naked, with soap before entering. It's the most authentic, cheapest thing you'll do in the city, and locals go year-round in all weather."
  - q: "What's the best area to stay in Reykjavík?"

Suburbs like Kópavogur and Hafnarfjördur are cheaper and fine for drivers, but you'll rely on a car or bus to reach the centre."
  - q: "How expensive is Reykjavík really?"

Ways to soften it: book an apartment with a kitchen and shop at Bónus or Krónan supermarkets, drink the (excellent) tap water instead of buying bottled, use the cheap public pools instead of the Blue Lagoon, and eat the famous hot dogs (pylsur) at Bæjarins Beztu."
  - q: "What currency does Iceland use and do UK cards work?"

You genuinely don't need to carry cash. Some unmanned petrol pumps ask for a card PIN, so know yours. Prices look alarming with all the zeros; roughly, 1,000 ISK is about £6."

  - q: "What is the midnight sun and when does it happen?"
    a: "Around the summer solstice (mid-June), Reykjavík gets around 21 hours of daylight and it never truly goes dark — the 'midnight sun'. It's magical for late-evening walks and long driving days, but bring an eye mask because hotel curtains vary. The flip side is winter: in December the city sees only about 4–5 hours of daylight, which is why that's aurora season, not sightseeing-marathon season."
  - q: "What should I pack for Reykjavík weather?"

A waterproof-windproof outer shell, warm mid-layers, a hat and gloves even in summer, and proper waterproof walking shoes. Reykjavík is windy year-round and rain blows in sideways; umbrellas are useless. Don't forget swimwear for the pools and Blue Lagoon, and an eye mask for the light summer nights."

F-roads are the rough mountain interior tracks (Highlands, Landmannalaugar), which are 4x4-only and closed in winter — none of them are needed for a Reykjavík-based trip. Never drive off-road; it's illegal."

## Explore more of Iceland

Reykjavík is the natural first base, but Iceland rewards those who venture further. Head north to [Akureyri](/blog/best-hotels-akureyri-2026), the buzzing "capital of the North" and gateway to whale watching and the ski slopes, or push on to the otherworldly geothermal fields around [Mývatn](/blog/best-hotels-myvatn-2026), with its lava formations, nature baths and boiling mud pots.

Chasing the great waterfalls, black-sand beaches and glaciers of the Ring Road? Our [South Iceland](/blog/best-hotels-south-iceland-2026) guide covers the stays around Vík, Skógafoss and Jökulsárlón, while [West Iceland](/blog/best-hotels-west-iceland-2026) opens up Snæfellsnes, the lava tubes and the fjords within easy reach of the capital. Base in Reykjavík first, then pick your direction.
