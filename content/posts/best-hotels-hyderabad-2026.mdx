## Where to Stay in Hyderabad: Choosing Your Base

Hyderabad is a long city with its two great attractions at opposite ends, and the single most consequential decision you will make is which end you sleep at. Here is the honest breakdown.

**Banjara Hills and Jubilee Hills** are the right answer for most visitors. This is the upmarket central-west belt — the Road No. 1 and Road No. 12 restaurant strips, the GVK One and Inorbit-style malls, KBR National Park for an early morning walk among peacocks and deer inside the city, and the film-industry money of Jubilee Hills and Film Nagar. Crucially it is *in the middle*: the Old City monuments are a manageable run south-east, Golconda Fort is a short drive west, and HITEC City is a straight shot along the main corridor. Taj Krishna, Radisson Blu Plaza, Hyatt Place, Mercure and the Banjara Hills Lemon Tree all sit here, and none of them costs what an equivalent would in Europe.

**Begumpet, Somajiguda and Tank Bund** form the central alternative. Begumpet grew up around the old city airport and is now a busy commercial belt with the advantage of being roughly equidistant from everywhere; Tank Bund runs along **Hussain Sagar**, the huge sixteenth-century lake with the monolithic Buddha statue on Gibraltar Rock in the middle of it, and it is genuinely pleasant in the evening. Vivanta Begumpet and the Hyderabad Marriott are the flagship options here, and the Marriott at around £57 is the value story of the whole luxury tier.

**HITEC City, Madhapur, Gachibowli and Kondapur** are the technology west. This is where the Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Deloitte and Indian IT campuses are, and it is why so much of this guide's hotel stock sits out here. If you are working in the district, stay in it — the corridor into town clogs badly at peak times and you will lose an hour each way. If you are not working there, be careful: it is a modern, planned, glassy district with essentially no sights in it. The redeeming feature is the **Blue Line metro**, which runs from Raidurg and HITEC City through Madhapur, Ameerpet and on to Nagole, and which makes a cheap Madhapur hotel a viable sightseeing base in a way that a cheap Gachibowli one is not.

**Abids, Koti and the Old City fringe** are for the history-first traveller. Abids is the old central commercial district — the GPO, the bookshops, the Sunday pavement book market — and it is the closest practical hotel belt to Charminar, Chowmahalla Palace, the Salar Jung Museum and Laad Bazaar. Royalton Hyderabad Abids and Taj Mahal Hotel Abids both sit here at very reasonable rates. Sleeping *inside* the Old City itself is not really a thing for visitors: the stock is thin, basic, and surrounded by noise and traffic well into the night.

**Secunderabad** is the twin city across the lake — the old British cantonment, with wider roads, colonial-era churches and a different rhythm. The practical reason to stay there is Secunderabad Junction, one of the main railway stations, which matters if you are arriving overnight from Chennai, Bangalore or Mumbai by train. Minerva Grand Secunderabad is the four-star pick.

**The airport zone at Shamshabad** is worth a night only at the ends of a trip. Rajiv Gandhi International (HYD) is about 30km south, and while the Outer Ring Road makes that a relatively painless 45 minutes to an hour, a dawn departure or a late Gulf arrival is much easier handled from Novotel Hyderabad Airport at the top end or Hotel Lake View Airport zone at the cheap end.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Hyderabad

Start where the city started. **Charminar** — the four-minaret monument built in 1591 at the founding of Hyderabad — stands at the centre of the Old City, and around it swirls one of the great urban scenes in India: **Laad Bazaar** selling lacquer bangles by the thousand, the enormous **Mecca Masjid** alongside it, and a crush of biryani houses, perfume sellers and pearl merchants. Hyderabad has been a pearl-trading city for centuries and still is. Go in the morning if you want to move, or in the evening if you want the atmosphere and can accept the crowds.

A short distance away, **Chowmahalla Palace** is the seat of the Nizams of Hyderabad — four palaces around courtyards, a chandeliered durbar hall, and the vintage cars that hint at how absurdly wealthy the last Nizams were. The **Salar Jung Museum** nearby holds one of the largest single-person art collections ever assembled, and both make excellent shaded options in the middle of a hot day.

West of the city, **Golconda Fort** is the other essential. This is the ruined hill capital of the Qutb Shahi dynasty, who ruled here before Hyderabad was founded and whose diamond mines produced the Koh-i-Noor. It is enormous, and the walk to the summit pavilion is several hundred steps of open rock — do it early or late, and take water. The famous party trick is the acoustics: a clap at the Fateh Darwaza gateway carries all the way to the top. A kilometre away, the **Qutb Shahi tombs** hold the sultans themselves under domes of granite and stucco in landscaped gardens, restored over a long conservation programme and far quieter than the fort. Fort first, tombs second, one morning — that is the plan.

Back in the middle, **Hussain Sagar** is the lake the British-era city grew around, with the Buddha statue on its island and boats out to it, plus **Lumbini Park**, **NTR Gardens** and the Necklace Road walk. **Birla Mandir**, a white marble Hindu temple on Naubat Pahad hill above the lake, has the best free view over the whole city, especially at sunset. Out east, **Ramoji Film City** — 25 to 30km from the centre — is one of the world's largest studio complexes and functions as a full theme park; it is a hit with families and cinema fans and a waste of a day for anyone who came for the Mughal and Qutb Shahi history.

And then there is the food, which for many people is the actual reason to come. **Hyderabadi biryani** is a distinct dish, not a regional variation: raw marinated meat and part-cooked basmati are sealed in a pot and slow-cooked together in the *dum pukht* method, so the rice absorbs everything rather than being stirred through afterwards. It comes with mirchi ka salan and dahi chutney, portions are enormous, the vegetarian version is everywhere, and the city argues about the best plate of it with real conviction. Around it sits a whole Hyderabadi table — haleem in Ramadan, Irani chai and Osmania biscuits in the old cafes, double ka meetha for pudding — that is worth planning meals around. Between the Old City institutions and the Banjara Hills restaurants you can eat extremely well for very little money.

## Getting Around Hyderabad

The **Hyderabad Metro** is the pleasant surprise: three lines, clean, air-conditioned, cheap and signed in English. The Red Line runs Miyapur to LB Nagar via Ameerpet; the Blue Line runs Nagole to Raidurg via Ameerpet, Madhapur and HITEC City; the Green Line links JBS to MGBS. **Ameerpet is the interchange**, which is exactly why Aditya Park at £36 is such a good-value base. The metro does not serve the airport, and it only clips the edge of the Old City, so you will still need taxis for Charminar and Golconda.

For everything else, use **Uber and Ola**. Both are widespread, give you a fixed fare and a tracked route, and let you book autorickshaws through the same app — which removes the meter negotiation that makes street rickshaws tiring. Fares are low by UK standards. Have your hotel name written down to show drivers, and expect longer pickup waits in the Old City lanes than in Banjara Hills or Gachibowli.

Traffic is real but manageable by Indian standards — Hyderabad has flyovers and the Outer Ring Road, and it is noticeably more predictable than Bangalore. Assume every journey takes half again as long as the map suggests, more at peak. Do not self-drive; there is no need, and India drives on the left with a lane discipline that will not feel familiar. Tap water is not drinkable — bottled or filtered only, including for brushing teeth. Tipping is customary but small: round up in restaurants if no service charge has been added, 50 to 100 rupees a bag for porters, nothing expected in taxis. And everything is in **rupees** — cards work at hotels, malls and chain restaurants, UPI apps dominate elsewhere, and sterling is accepted nowhere.

## Hyderabad Hotels FAQs

**Which Hyderabad is this guide about — the Indian city or the Pakistani one?**
The Indian one. There are two cities called Hyderabad: the capital of Telangana in southern India, at roughly 17.4 degrees north and 78.5 degrees east, and a much smaller city in Sindh province, Pakistan. Every one of the 49 hotels in this guide is coordinate-verified inside the Indian city — Charminar, Golconda Fort, HITEC City and Rajiv Gandhi International Airport (HYD). If you are searching for the Pakistani Hyderabad, none of this applies, and the visa, currency and flight advice below is wrong for you.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Hyderabad?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party visa agencies charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Hyderabad?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work at hotels, malls and chain restaurants, and UPI apps dominate everywhere else, but carry small rupee notes for autorickshaws, Old City stalls and tips. Sterling is not accepted anywhere.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Hyderabad?**
British Airways has operated a direct London Heathrow to Hyderabad (HYD) route at roughly nine and a half hours, but it has come and gone from the schedule, so check whether it is running on your dates before you plan around it. The reliable alternative is one stop: Emirates via Dubai, Qatar Airways via Doha, Etihad via Abu Dhabi, or a domestic connection through Delhi or Mumbai. One-stop routings are often cheaper than the direct anyway.

**When is the best time to visit Hyderabad?**
October to February, without much argument. Those are the dry, comfortable months, with warm days and pleasantly cool evenings — this is when the Deccan plateau climate is at its best and when you can walk the Old City or Golconda Fort without suffering. February and early March are still very good. Hotel rates through this window are higher than in summer, but the trade is worth it.

**How hot does Hyderabad get in April, May and June?**
Hot enough to reshape your day. April to early June is the pre-monsoon peak, and daytime temperatures regularly sit in the high thirties Celsius and can push past forty. Sightseeing at Golconda Fort or the Qutb Shahi tombs — both of which are open, exposed stone with very little shade — becomes genuinely punishing by mid-morning. If you must travel then, do outdoor sights at dawn, book air conditioning as a non-negotiable and keep the middle of the day for museums, malls and lunch.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Hyderabad?**
Banjara Hills or Jubilee Hills. This is the upmarket central-west belt: the best restaurants, the good malls, KBR National Park for a morning walk, and a sensible driving distance to both the Old City monuments in the south-east and the tech district in the west. Most of the five-star hotels in this guide sit here, and they cost a fraction of the London equivalent. Begumpet and the Tank Bund area around Hussain Sagar are the next best central options.

**Why do so many Hyderabad hotels sit in HITEC City and Gachibowli?**
Because that is where the work is. HITEC City (Madhapur) and Gachibowli, together with Kondapur and the Financial District at Nanakramguda, are Hyderabad's technology and corporate belt — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Deloitte and dozens of Indian IT firms have large campuses there. The hotels followed the expense accounts, which is why you will find international four and five-star brands and a huge stack of clean, cheap business hotels within a couple of kilometres of each other. It is a great value area and a poor sightseeing base.

**Should I stay in the Old City near Charminar?**
Visit it, but do not usually sleep there. The Old City around Charminar, Laad Bazaar and the Mecca Masjid is the historic heart of Hyderabad and the single most rewarding few hours in the city, but the hotel stock is thin, very basic and hemmed in by traffic and noise. Almost every visitor bases themselves in Banjara Hills, Begumpet, Abids or the tech west and comes into the Old City for a morning or an evening. Abids and Koti are the closest practical hotel districts.

**Where should I stay for business in Hyderabad?**
Match the hotel to the campus. If your meetings are at HITEC City or Madhapur, stay in HITEC City. If they are at Gachibowli, Nanakramguda or the Financial District, stay in Gachibowli or Kondapur. Getting this wrong costs you an hour each way in traffic every day. If you are visiting government or older corporate offices in the centre, Begumpet, Somajiguda and Banjara Hills are the right side of town.

**How far is Hyderabad airport from the city?**
Rajiv Gandhi International Airport (HYD) is at Shamshabad, roughly 30 kilometres south of the centre. The good news is that the Outer Ring Road makes it one of the smoother airport runs in India — typically 45 minutes to an hour to Banjara Hills or Gachibowli outside peak times, sometimes a little more. There are Pushpak airport coaches to several city points, plus Uber and Ola. For a dawn departure, an airport-zone hotel saves real stress.

**Is the Hyderabad Metro useful for visitors?**
Yes, more than most Indian metros. Three lines cover a lot of ground: the Red Line runs Miyapur to LB Nagar through Ameerpet, the Blue Line runs Nagole to Raidurg through Ameerpet, HITEC City and Madhapur, and the Green Line links JBS to MGBS. It is clean, air-conditioned, cheap and signed in English, and crucially the Blue Line links the tech west to the centre, which is the journey traffic punishes most. It does not reach the airport or go far into the Old City.

**How bad is Hyderabad traffic, really?**
Bad, but not Bangalore-bad. Hyderabad has flyovers, the Outer Ring Road and a genuinely useful metro, so cross-city journeys are more predictable than in some Indian megacities. That said, the corridor between the centre and HITEC City clogs badly at peak times, and the Old City lanes are slow at any hour. Assume everything takes half again as long as the map says, and use the metro when your route matches a line.

**Should I use Uber and Ola in Hyderabad?**
Yes. Both operate across the city, give you a fixed fare and a tracked route, and remove the negotiation that makes street autorickshaws tiring. You can book autorickshaws through the same apps, which is often the quickest way to move short distances. Have the hotel name in writing to show the driver, and expect to wait longer for pickups in the Old City lanes than in Banjara Hills or Gachibowli.

**What language do people speak in Hyderabad?**
Telugu is the state language of Telangana, Urdu has deep historic roots in the city and is widely spoken — especially in the Old City — and Hindi works almost everywhere. English is common in hotels, restaurants, malls, the tech districts and among younger people, and all signage on the metro and at the airport is in English. You will not struggle to be understood in any hotel in this guide.

**Can I drink the tap water in Hyderabad?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only, and use it for brushing your teeth too. Almost every hotel here supplies bottled water in the room or has a filtered dispenser. Skip ice at street stalls until your stomach has settled, and go for food that is cooked fresh and served hot, which in Hyderabad covers most of what you actually want to eat.

**Do I need to tip in Hyderabad?**
Tipping is customary but small. Restaurants often add a service charge, and if they have not, rounding up or leaving five to ten per cent is normal. For hotel porters, around 50 to 100 rupees a bag is generous; for housekeeping, a similar amount per night left at the end. Autorickshaw and app-taxi drivers do not expect a tip, though rounding up is appreciated. Everything is in rupees — tipping in pounds is no use to anyone.

**How much does a budget hotel cost per night in Hyderabad?**
Roughly £20 to £40 a night for the 29 budget properties in this guide, pulled on live searches while writing. Sitara Paradise starts around £20, Wild Wings Premium Hotel and Hotel Maritan around £21, and most of the tier lands between £22 and £35. Those are lead-in rates for the cheapest available room on flexible dates — conference weeks, festivals and wedding season push them up.

**What is the most expensive hotel in this guide?**
Hotel Deccan Serai Grande in Gachibowli, at around £141 a night on the searches we ran while writing — which is an honest quirk of Hyderabad, because it is a four-star property, not a five-star. The most expensive genuine five-star here is Trident Hyderabad at about £131, then Le Meridien Hyderabad at about £129. Everything else in the luxury tier sits between roughly £57 and £98, which is remarkable value for real five-star hotels.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Hyderabad?**
The outer tech belt and the older central commercial districts. Around HITEC City, Madhapur, Kondapur and Bachupally there is a deep stack of small business hotels built for domestic workers, and they undercut the branded competition heavily — several in this guide start around £21 to £28. Abids, Koti and the LB Nagar side in the south-east are the other cheap options, and they put you closer to the Old City monuments.

**Is Hyderabad safe for tourists?**
It is generally considered one of India's more relaxed large cities, and millions of people move around it every day without incident. The realistic risks are traffic, pickpocketing in crowded bazaars like Laad Bazaar, and being overcharged rather than anything more serious. Use app taxis rather than street cabs after dark, keep bags in front of you in the Old City crowds, and read the current UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you travel.

**What is Hyderabadi biryani and is it worth the hype?**
It is worth the hype. Hyderabadi biryani is the dum pukht style — raw marinated meat and part-cooked basmati sealed in a pot and slow-cooked together, so the rice takes on the meat, saffron and spice rather than being mixed in afterwards. It arrives with mirchi ka salan and dahi chutney. The city treats it as a point of civic pride, portions are enormous, and the vegetarian version is common. Eat one properly at least once — ideally at lunch, when it is freshest.

**Is Golconda Fort worth visiting?**
Yes — it is the single best half-day in Hyderabad after the Old City. Golconda is a vast ruined hill fortress on the western edge of the city, the Qutb Shahi capital before Hyderabad was founded, and it is famous for its acoustics: a clap at the entrance gate is audible at the summit pavilion. The climb is several hundred steps over open rock with little shade, so go early in the morning or late in the afternoon, and take water.

**What are the Qutb Shahi tombs?**
A necropolis of domed granite and stucco tombs holding the Qutb Shahi sultans, set in landscaped gardens about a kilometre from Golconda Fort. They have been through a long, careful conservation programme and are calmer, greener and far less crowded than the fort itself. Pairing the two in one morning is the standard and correct plan — the fort first while it is cool, the tombs after.

**What is Chowmahalla Palace?**
The palace of the Nizams of Hyderabad, in the Old City a short distance from Charminar. Four palaces around courtyards, a durbar hall with chandeliers, and a collection that includes the Nizams vintage cars. It is the best place in the city to understand how astonishingly wealthy Hyderabad was, and it works perfectly as the indoor, shaded half of an Old City morning. The Salar Jung Museum nearby is the other major indoor stop.

**Is Ramoji Film City worth a full day?**
It depends entirely on who you are travelling with. Ramoji Film City, about 25 to 30 kilometres east of the centre, is one of the largest film studio complexes in the world and functions as a full theme park — sets, shows, rides and gardens. Families and anyone curious about Indian cinema tend to love it. If you came for Mughal and Qutb Shahi history, skip it and give the day to the Old City instead. Either way it is a full day, not a half.

**How many days do I need in Hyderabad?**
Three full days is the sweet spot. Day one for the Old City — Charminar, Laad Bazaar, Mecca Masjid, Chowmahalla Palace and the Salar Jung Museum. Day two for Golconda Fort and the Qutb Shahi tombs in the morning, then Hussain Sagar, Birla Mandir and an evening in Banjara Hills. Day three for Ramoji Film City, or for a slower drift through the markets and one more biryani. Two days is enough if you skip the film city.

**Is Secunderabad a different city?**
Technically yes, practically no. Secunderabad was the British cantonment founded across Hussain Sagar lake from the older Hyderabad, and it kept a separate identity, its own cantonment administration and a distinctly different feel — wider roads, colonial-era churches and barracks. Today the two are one continuous urban area. Secunderabad Junction is one of the main railway stations, which is the practical reason to stay there.

**Do Hyderabad hotels charge extra fees, and is breakfast included?**
There is no resort fee, but Indian hotel rates carry GST, which varies by room-rate band, and some hotels add a service charge or price breakfast separately. At the luxury and mid-range tiers breakfast is usually included and usually good — expect idli, dosa, vada and a South Indian spread alongside the continental options. In the budget tier it is inconsistent, so check what is included on the live page before you compare two similar rates.

**Is Hyderabad a good first trip to India?**
It is a strong choice. It is less overwhelming than Delhi, has more genuine headline monuments than Bangalore, English is widely spoken, the metro works, the airport transfer is easy by Indian standards and the food is extraordinary. The main caveats are the summer heat and the fact that the Old City is intense and crowded in a way that a first-time visitor should be ready for. Many people pair it with Bangalore or Mumbai on one trip.
