  - q: "When is the best time to visit Delhi?"
    a: "October to March. That is the cool, dry window when walking Old Delhi, Humayun's Tomb and Qutub Minar is genuinely comfortable. May and June are extremely hot, with daytime temperatures that make sightseeing punishing. July to September is the monsoon — humid, with heavy downpours. October, February and March are the sweet spot if you want warmth without either extreme."
  - q: "How bad is Delhi's winter air quality?"
    a: "Honestly, it is poor — this is the one caveat worth taking seriously. From roughly November to January, cool stagnant air, crop-residue burning in neighbouring states and traffic combine to produce heavy smog, and readings regularly reach levels considered unhealthy. If you have asthma or another respiratory condition, either travel outside those months or book a hotel with in-room air purification, and check daily air quality readings while you are there. Many mid-range and luxury Delhi hotels now advertise purifiers precisely because guests ask."

If you value quiet or are travelling with family, pick Connaught Place or South Delhi instead and pay the difference."

  - q: "Should I use Uber and Ola, or street taxis, in Delhi?"

  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Delhi?"

Avoid ice at street stalls, and go easy on raw salads at the cheapest places until your stomach has adjusted. Street food in Chandni Chowk is one of Delhi's great experiences; choose busy stalls with fast turnover and food cooked in front of you."

Book taxis through an app, go straight to your hotel, and read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go."
  - q: "Is Delhi good for solo women travellers?"

Arrange an airport pickup if you land late at night."
  - q: "What are the two UNESCO sites in Delhi?"

Humayun's Tomb is the sixteenth-century Mughal garden tomb that prefigured the Taj Mahal, and the Qutub Minar is the soaring brick minaret in Mehrauli in the south of the city. ## Where to Stay in Delhi: Choosing Your Base

**Connaught Place and central New Delhi** — the first-timer's answer. The colonnaded circles of Connaught Place are Delhi's transport heart: the yellow and blue metro lines cross here, the Airport Express terminates at New Delhi station a few minutes away, and India Gate, the Kartavya Path, Janpath and the National Museum are close. Old Delhi is a short metro ride north. It is also where most of the mid-range four-stars in this guide sit, and by capital-city standards they are cheap.

**Paharganj and Arakashan Road** — the budget and backpacker belt, directly opposite New Delhi Railway Station. Honest version: it is gritty, crowded, noisy and full of touts, and it is also where you will find the cheapest beds in the city and the easiest 6am starts for trains to Agra and Jaipur. If price and train access are the priorities, stay here. If you want quiet or you are travelling with children, do not.

**South Delhi — Hauz Khas, Saket, Kailash Colony, Nehru Place and C R Park** — leafy, calmer and much greener, with the best restaurants and bars in the city and the Qutub Minar, Lodhi Garden and the Lotus Temple close by. Metro connections are good, but Old Delhi is a proper journey. The choice for repeat visitors and longer stays.

**Aerocity and Mahipalpur** — airport nights only. Aerocity holds the international five-stars minutes from Terminal 3; Mahipalpur across the highway holds the cheap ones. Both exist to remove the airport journey from your first or last day. Neither is a neighbourhood, so do not plan a holiday around them.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Delhi

Delhi is really two cities. **Old Delhi** is the Mughal one: the great red sandstone **Red Fort**, the vast courtyard of **Jama Masjid**, and the packed lanes of **Chandni Chowk**, where the street food is arguably the best in India and the **Khari Baoli spice market** is the largest in Asia. Go in the morning, take the metro to Chandni Chowk station, and accept that it will be loud. Then there is **New Delhi**, the imperial capital laid out in the twentieth century: **India Gate** at the end of the ceremonial **Rajpath, now the Kartavya Path**, the government buildings behind it, the **National Museum** with its Harappan and Mughal collections, and the calm of **Lodhi Garden**, where medieval tombs sit among joggers and picnics.

Two of Delhi's monuments are UNESCO World Heritage Sites and both are unmissable: **Humayun's Tomb**, the sixteenth-century Mughal garden tomb that set the template the Taj Mahal later perfected, and the **Qutub Minar** complex in Mehrauli, where a 73-metre brick minaret has stood since the thirteenth century. Add the marble lotus-shaped **Lotus Temple**, the enormous carved stone **Akshardham** temple complex across the Yamuna, and **Gurudwara Bangla Sahib**, the Sikh temple with a sacred tank and a community kitchen that feeds thousands of people a day, free, to anyone who comes. For an evening, **Hauz Khas Village** in South Delhi pairs a medieval reservoir and ruined madrasa with a dense run of restaurants and bars.

Delhi is also the gateway to the **Golden Triangle**. Agra and the **Taj Mahal** are around 100 minutes away on the Gatimaan Express from Hazrat Nizamuddin, which makes a day trip genuinely comfortable — just remember the Taj is closed to visitors on Fridays. **Jaipur**, the Pink City, is roughly four and a half to five hours by train or road. Most visitors use Delhi as the first stop and move on rather than day-tripping both.

## Delhi Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Delhi?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party "visa services" charge a large mark-up for the same document.

**What currency is used in Delhi?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards are widely accepted in hotels, malls and chain restaurants; carry cash for autorickshaws, street food and smaller guesthouses. Use bank ATMs rather than airport exchange desks.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Delhi?**
Yes. London to Delhi (DEL) is a direct route of roughly 8.5 to 9 hours, flown by British Airways, Virgin Atlantic and Air India depending on the season. Indira Gandhi International is India's busiest airport and the main long-haul gateway, so it is also where most onward domestic connections to Jaipur, Udaipur, Goa and Kerala start. [Search flights to DEL](/flights?to=DEL) to compare fares and departure airports for your dates.

**When is the best time to visit Delhi?**
October to March. That is the cool, dry window when walking Old Delhi, Humayun's Tomb and Qutub Minar is genuinely comfortable. May and June are extremely hot, with daytime temperatures that make sightseeing punishing. July to September is the monsoon — humid, with heavy downpours. October, February and March are the sweet spot if you want warmth without either extreme.

**How bad is Delhi's winter air quality?**
Honestly, it is poor — this is the one caveat worth taking seriously. From roughly November to January, cool stagnant air, crop-residue burning in neighbouring states and traffic combine to produce heavy smog, and readings regularly reach levels considered unhealthy. If you have asthma or another respiratory condition, either travel outside those months or book a hotel with in-room air purification, and check daily air quality readings while you are there. Many mid-range and luxury Delhi hotels now advertise purifiers precisely because guests ask.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Delhi?**
Connaught Place and central New Delhi. It is the transport hub — metro lines meet there, the Airport Express terminates nearby at New Delhi station, and India Gate, the government quarter and Janpath are close. Old Delhi's Red Fort and Chandni Chowk are a short metro ride, and Humayun's Tomb and Qutub Minar are straightforward from there too. It is the easiest base for a first trip.

**Is Paharganj worth staying in?**
It depends what you want. Paharganj is the backpacker district opposite New Delhi Railway Station: extremely cheap rooms, cheap food, travel agents and constant noise. It is gritty, crowded and not to everyone's taste, but it is unbeatable for price and for early trains. If you value quiet or are travelling with family, pick Connaught Place or South Delhi instead and pay the difference.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Delhi?**
Paharganj and the Mahipalpur airport strip. Between them they account for most of the sub-£25 rooms in this guide. Karol Bagh and outer areas like Rohini are also cheap. Central New Delhi and South Delhi's leafier colonies carry a premium, but Delhi's premium is small by UK standards — a good four-star in Connaught Place is still under £60 in this guide.

**How much does a budget hotel cost per night in Delhi?**
Roughly £19 to £35 a night for the 29 budget properties in this guide, pulled on live searches while writing. Airport Hotel Tashree starts around £19 and Hotel City Star around £20, with most of the tier landing between £22 and £30. Those are lead-in rates for the cheapest available room on flexible dates — wedding season, festivals and big conference weeks push them up.

**What is the most expensive hotel in this guide?**
Taj Palace, New Delhi, at around £156 a night on the searches we ran while writing — the Taj group's Diplomatic Enclave flagship. That is the true top of this guide's price range. Shangri-La Eros New Delhi is close behind at about £150, and the rest of the luxury tier mostly sits between £64 and £123, which by global five-star standards is remarkable value.

**Is the Delhi Metro good for tourists?**
It is excellent — one of the best metros in Asia. It is clean, air-conditioned, cheap, signed in English and covers almost everything a visitor wants, including Chandni Chowk for Old Delhi, Qutub Minar, the Lotus Temple (Kalkaji Mandir) and Akshardham. The Airport Express runs from Terminal 3 to New Delhi station in around 20 minutes. Buy a tourist card or a smart card on arrival and avoid rush hour with luggage.

**Should I use Uber and Ola, or street taxis, in Delhi?**
Use Uber or Ola. App bookings give you a fixed fare, a tracked route and no negotiation, which removes the single most common tourist hassle in Delhi. Prepaid taxi booths at the airport and station are the safe alternative. Avoid unmetered street taxis and, especially, anyone near New Delhi Railway Station who offers to take you to a "tourist office" — that is a well-known scam that ends in an overpriced tour.

**Can I drink the tap water in Delhi?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only. Most hotels supply bottled water in the room or have a filtered dispenser. Avoid ice at street stalls, and go easy on raw salads at the cheapest places until your stomach has adjusted. Street food in Chandni Chowk is one of Delhi's great experiences; choose busy stalls with fast turnover and food cooked in front of you.

**How do I do a day trip to Agra and the Taj Mahal?**
Take the Gatimaan Express from Hazrat Nizamuddin station — it is the fastest train to Agra Cantt and takes roughly 100 minutes each way, which makes a same-day return genuinely comfortable. Book ahead, since it sells out. The alternative is the Shatabdi from New Delhi station or a car with a driver on the Yamuna Expressway. Note the Taj Mahal is closed to tourists on Fridays.

**Which side of the road do they drive on in India?**
The left, as in the UK — but Delhi traffic is far denser and lane discipline is looser than you will be used to. Almost no visitor self-drives, and there is no need to: the metro, app taxis and autorickshaws cover everything. If you do want a car, hiring one with a driver is normal, cheap and much easier.

**How many days do I need in Delhi?**
Three full days is the sensible minimum: one for Old Delhi (Red Fort, Jama Masjid, Chandni Chowk and the spice market), one for the Mughal monuments (Humayun's Tomb and Qutub Minar, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites), and one for New Delhi (India Gate, the Kartavya Path, the National Museum, Lodhi Garden). Add a fourth for Akshardham, the Lotus Temple and Gurudwara Bangla Sahib, and a fifth if you want Agra.

**Is Delhi safe for tourists?**
Delhi is visited by millions of people a year without incident, but it demands more street awareness than Mumbai. The realistic risks are scams and overcharging rather than violence: touts around New Delhi Railway Station, drivers who claim your hotel is closed, and "government tourist offices" that are nothing of the sort. Book taxis through an app, go straight to your hotel, and read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go.

**Is Delhi good for solo women travellers?**
Many solo women travel Delhi successfully, but it needs more planning than most Indian cities. Use app taxis rather than street transport after dark, use the women-only carriage at the front of every metro train, dress modestly at religious sites, and choose a hotel in a well-lit central or South Delhi area rather than the cheapest room in Paharganj. Arrange an airport pickup if you land late at night.

**What are the UNESCO sites in Delhi?**
Humayun's Tomb, the Qutub Minar complex and the Red Fort are all inscribed. Humayun's Tomb is the sixteenth-century Mughal garden tomb that prefigured the Taj Mahal, and the Qutub Minar is the soaring brick minaret in Mehrauli in the south of the city. All three are easy metro trips and all three reward going early, before the coach groups arrive.

**Should I stay in Aerocity?**
Only for airport nights. Aerocity is a purpose-built hotel district minutes from Terminal 3 with a strong cluster of international five-stars — Holiday Inn and Pride Plaza in this guide — and some good budget options. It is exactly right for a late arrival, an early departure or a short stopover. It is not a neighbourhood, so do not base a sightseeing holiday there.

**What is South Delhi like as a base?**
Leafy, calmer and much greener than the centre, with the best restaurant and bar scene in the city. Hauz Khas Village, Saket, Greater Kailash, Kailash Colony and Nehru Place all sit here, close to the Qutub Minar and Lodhi Garden. It suits second-time visitors, longer stays and anyone who wants quiet evenings. The trade is a longer metro run into Old Delhi.

**Is Old Delhi worth the chaos?**
Absolutely, but treat it as an expedition rather than a stroll. Chandni Chowk is dense, loud and hot, and the lanes around the Khari Baoli spice market are barely wide enough for two people. Go in the morning, take the metro to Chandni Chowk station, consider a walking-tour guide, and build in a break. The Red Fort and Jama Masjid are right there, and the street food is the best in India.

**Do Delhi hotels charge extra fees?**
There is no resort fee, but Indian hotel rates carry GST, which varies by room rate band, and some properties add a service charge or price breakfast separately. Budget rooms are the most likely to exclude breakfast. Always check what is included on the hotel's live page before comparing two similar-looking rates.

**Is breakfast usually included in Delhi?**
At the luxury and mid-range tiers, usually yes, and often a large Indian and continental buffet. In the budget tier it is inconsistent — some include a simple breakfast, others charge a small supplement. Because breakfast is rarely expensive to add in Delhi, the room rate matters more than the inclusion when you are comparing.

**Is Delhi a good base for the Golden Triangle?**
It is the standard starting point. Delhi, Agra and Jaipur form the Golden Triangle, and Delhi has the international airport, the fastest trains and the widest choice of hotels of the three. Agra is around 100 minutes by Gatimaan Express; Jaipur is roughly four and a half to five hours by train or car. Many visitors do Delhi first, then move on rather than day-tripping both.
