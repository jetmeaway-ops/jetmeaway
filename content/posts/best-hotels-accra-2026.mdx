  - q: "Do UK travellers need a visa for Ghana?"
    a: "Yes. UK passport holders need a Ghana visa arranged in advance rather than on arrival. Ghana has been rolling out an online or e-visa system alongside the traditional consular route, so check the current official process before you book flights, and allow time for supporting documents and processing. The UK FCDO travel advice page for Ghana is the quickest way to confirm what applies on your travel dates."
  - q: "What currency is used in Accra?"
    a: "The Ghanaian Cedi, written GHS. Cards work in the better hotels, malls, larger restaurants and supermarkets, but cash still matters for markets, trotros, small eateries, tips and anything outside the smart districts. Exchange rates move, so treat every pound figure in this guide as a rough conversion for guidance rather than an exact quote."
  - q: "Are there direct flights from London to Accra?"
    a: "Yes. British Airways flies direct from London to Kotoka International Airport (ACC) in roughly six and a half hours, which makes Accra one of the easiest West African capitals to reach from the UK. There are also plenty of one-stop options through European, Gulf and African hubs, which are often cheaper if you do not mind a connection."
  - q: "Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Ghana?"
    a: "Yes. A yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Ghana and you should carry it with your passport, because it can be checked on arrival. Arrange the vaccination well ahead of travel through your GP or a travel clinic, as it needs time to take effect and appointment slots can be scarce."
  - q: "Is there malaria in Accra?"
    a: "Malaria is a real risk in Ghana, Accra included, and it should be planned for rather than shrugged off. See your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly to discuss antimalarial tablets, and pack repellent, cover up at dusk and use the air conditioning or mosquito nets your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice."
  - q: "Is Accra safe for tourists?"
    a: "Ghana is widely regarded as one of West Africa's most welcoming and stable countries, and Accra is a comparatively relaxed capital by regional standards. That does not mean no precautions: use ride-hailing apps or hotel cars rather than flagging street taxis, keep phones, bags and jewellery out of sight in traffic and in crowded markets, take care walking after dark, and use the hotel safe. Read the current UK FCDO travel advice for Ghana before you book."
  - q: "Is English spoken in Ghana?"
    a: "Yes, and this is a genuine advantage for UK visitors. English is the official language of Ghana and it is used for government, business, signage, schooling and the media, so you can book taxis, order food, ask directions and negotiate a market price without a phrasebook. Twi, Ga, Ewe and other Ghanaian languages are spoken widely alongside it, and learning a greeting or two goes down very well."
  - q: "When is the best time to visit Accra?"
    a: "The dry season, roughly November to March, is the most comfortable window and the easiest for road trips west to Cape Coast. The main rains fall roughly April to June and can make the traffic considerably worse. Between about December and February the Harmattan can blow dust south from the Sahara, which cools the nights a little and leaves a haze over the city."
  - q: "What is the Harmattan?"
    a: "A dry, dusty wind that blows down from the Sahara across West Africa roughly between December and February. In Accra it usually means hazier skies, drier air, cooler nights and a fine dust that gets onto everything. It rarely stops anyone doing anything, but photographers should know that long views are softer at that time of year."
  - q: "Can I visit Cape Coast and Elmina Castles as a day trip from Accra?"
    a: "Yes, and for many visitors it is the single most important thing they do in Ghana. Cape Coast Castle and Elmina Castle sit roughly three hours west of Accra on the coast, both UNESCO World Heritage sites, and both were holding points for enslaved Africans forced onto ships across the Atlantic. A day trip is possible and common, but it is a long day, and staying overnight in Cape Coast gives you the time these places deserve and lets you add the Kakum National Park canopy walkway."
  - q: "What is the Year of Return?"
    a: "The Year of Return was Ghana's 2019 invitation to people of African descent across the diaspora to come home, marking four centuries since the first recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia. It brought a large wave of visitors from the United States, the Caribbean, the UK and beyond, and it was followed by a longer Beyond the Return programme. Ghana's role as a place of return, and the presence of the slave-trade castles on its coast, gives travel here a weight that few other destinations carry."
  - q: "What is there to see in Accra?"
    a: "Independence Square with its Black Star Gate and Independence Arch, the Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park honouring Ghana's first president, the old Jamestown district with its lighthouse, colonial fort and fishing harbour, the W.E.B. Du Bois Memorial Centre, the enormous Makola Market, the National Museum, Labadi Beach and the surf and drumming scene at Kokrobite, and the restaurants and nightlife of Osu around Oxford Street. Out of town, Cape Coast and Elmina Castles, Kakum National Park and Lake Volta."
  - q: "What is the Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park?"
    a: "A memorial park and mausoleum in central Accra dedicated to Kwame Nkrumah, who led Ghana to independence in 1957 as the first sub-Saharan African country to break from colonial rule, and who became one of the defining voices of pan-Africanism. His remains rest here alongside a museum of his life and a striking monument set in landscaped grounds. It is a short, worthwhile visit and it explains a great deal about modern Ghana."
  - q: "What is Jamestown in Accra?"
    a: "Jamestown is one of the oldest quarters of Accra, grown up around a colonial-era fort and lighthouse on the coast, and still home to a working fishing harbour where long wooden boats land the catch. It is dense, loud, poor in parts and full of life, and it has become known for street art and the Chale Wote festival. Go with a local guide, ask before photographing people, and treat it as a lived-in neighbourhood rather than an attraction."

Osu is the best base for restaurants, bars and walkable evenings. East Legon suits modern residential comfort and longer stays. Labadi and La put you on the beach.   - q: "How do I get around Accra?"
    a: "Most visitors use ride-hailing apps, which work well in Accra and remove the haggling and the guesswork of a street taxi. Hotels will also arrange cars and airport transfers. Shared trotro minibuses are how most of the city travels and are extremely cheap, but they take local knowledge to use. Walking works within a neighbourhood such as Osu, less so between them."
  - q: "How bad is Accra traffic?"
    a: "Heavy, especially at peak times and during the rains, and it is the main reason to choose your neighbourhood carefully. Accra is a sprawling low-rise city and a journey that looks short on a map can take an hour. Plan two or three things a day rather than five, travel outside peak hours where you can, and stay near the things you came for rather than across town from them."
  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Accra?"
    a: "No, stick to bottled or properly filtered water. Hotels supply bottled water and many have filtered dispensers, and sachet water is sold everywhere. Use bottled water for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach, and be sensible about ice and raw salads at the cheapest roadside stalls."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Accra?"
    a: "Three days covers the city itself comfortably: Independence Square and the Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park, Jamestown and the National Museum, Makola Market, a beach afternoon at Labadi and a couple of evenings in Osu. Add two more if you want to travel west for Cape Coast and Elmina Castles and the Kakum canopy walkway, which is the trip most visitors say mattered most."
  - q: "Are there beaches in Accra?"
    a: "Yes. Labadi Beach, also called La Pleasure Beach, is the city's best-known stretch of sand and is busiest and liveliest at weekends with music, food and horse rides. Kokrobite, west of the city, is the surf and drumming village where backpackers head for a night or two. The Atlantic here has real currents, so take local advice before swimming."
  - q: "What is Kakum National Park?"
    a: "A protected rainforest park inland from Cape Coast, best known for a canopy walkway of rope-and-plank bridges strung high between forest giants. It is usually combined with the Cape Coast and Elmina castles on a longer trip west from Accra. Go early for the best chance of birds and cooler air, and note that the walkway is not for anyone uneasy with heights."
  - q: "What is Lake Volta?"
    a: "One of the largest man-made lakes in the world, created by the Akosombo Dam on the Volta River, stretching far north from a couple of hours east of Accra. It is a change of pace from the coast: boat trips, riverside lodges, waterfalls in the Volta Region and a slower, greener landscape. It works well as an overnight or weekend trip rather than a day out."
  - q: "Do they drive on the left in Ghana?"
    a: "No, Ghana drives on the right, unlike the UK. Most visitors do not need to drive at all in Accra, where ride-hailing apps and hotel cars are cheap and remove the stress. If you do hire a car for a trip west or to the Volta Region, allow far more time than the distances suggest."
  - q: "Is tipping expected in Ghana?"
    a: "Tipping is not obligatory but it is appreciated, and small cedi notes are useful for porters, drivers, guides and restaurant staff. Around ten per cent in a restaurant is generous and welcome. Some hotels and restaurants add a service charge, so check the bill before adding more on top."

## What to See in Accra

**Start at Independence Square**, also called Black Star Square, an enormous ceremonial parade ground on the seafront built for Ghana's independence and dominated by the Black Star Gate and the Independence Arch. It is the image of the country that most people carry, and it is where the national day parades happen every March. A short drive away, the **Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park** holds the mausoleum and museum of Ghana's first president, the man who led the country out of colonial rule in 1957 and became one of the defining voices of pan-Africanism. Both sites explain more about modern Ghana in an afternoon than a week of reading would.

**Jamestown** is the old heart of the city, grown up around a colonial fort and a lighthouse you can climb for a view over tin roofs and the Atlantic, with a working fishing harbour below where long painted boats land the catch each morning. It is poor, dense, loud and enormously alive, and it has become the home of Accra's street-art scene. Go with a local guide, ask before you photograph anyone, and remember that people live here. Nearby, the **W.E.B. Du Bois Memorial Centre for Pan African Culture** occupies the house where the American scholar and civil rights thinker spent his final years after taking Ghanaian citizenship, and where he is buried. It is a quiet, moving place.

For the everyday city, **Makola Market** is the great commercial engine of Accra, block after block of fabric, food, hardware and noise, best entered with a guide and a good temper. The **National Museum** holds Ghanaian archaeology, Ashanti gold weights, textiles and regalia. **Labadi Beach**, or La Pleasure Beach, is where Accra goes at the weekend for music, horse rides and grilled fish, while **Kokrobite**, west of the city, is the surf-and-drumming village where travellers stay a night longer than they planned. In the evenings, **Osu** and its Oxford Street strip carry the densest concentration of restaurants, bars and nightlife in the country.

### Cape Coast and Elmina — the reason many people come

About three hours west of Accra, on a beautiful stretch of the Gulf of Guinea, stand **Cape Coast Castle** and **Elmina Castle**. They are UNESCO World Heritage sites, and they are among the most significant places in the history of the Atlantic world. Behind their whitewashed walls are the dungeons where enslaved Africans were held, sometimes for months, before being forced through the door of no return onto ships bound for the Americas. Millions of people in the Caribbean, the United States, Brazil and Britain descend from those taken through these gates.

Visiting is not a sightseeing stop and it should not be treated as one. Guided tours take you into the dungeons themselves, and they are quiet, deliberate and hard. Many visitors, particularly those travelling from the African diaspora, describe it as the most important journey of their lives — a pilgrimage rather than a tour. Ghana's **Year of Return** in 2019, which invited the descendants of enslaved Africans to come home four centuries after the first recorded arrivals in Virginia, brought a wave of that travel and has continued in the years since. Go with time, go with respect, and if you can, stay overnight in Cape Coast rather than rushing back to the capital the same evening.

The same trip pairs naturally with **Kakum National Park**, whose canopy walkway strings rope bridges high through the rainforest inland from Cape Coast. In the other direction, a couple of hours east of Accra, **Lake Volta** and the Akosombo Dam open up one of the largest man-made lakes on earth, with boat trips, waterfalls and green hills in the Volta Region — a slower, cooler counterweight to the coast.

Before you book, read the current [UK FCDO travel advice for Ghana](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ghana) for entry rules, the visa process and health requirements, and browse Ghana's official tourism site at [visitghana.com](https://visitghana.com/) for what is open and on when you travel.

## Choosing Where to Stay in Accra

Accra sprawls, it is mostly low-rise, and the traffic is heavy. The single best piece of advice we can give is to **base yourself near whatever you actually came for**, because crossing the city eats hours you would rather spend somewhere else.

- **Airport Residential Area and Cantonments** — where most of the smartest hotels sit, close to Kotoka International, the embassies and the better restaurants. Calm, green, well served and the easiest first-visit choice. Cantonments in particular is quiet in a way that surprises people.
- **Osu** — the best base for eating, drinking and walking. Oxford Street and the streets around it hold most of the city's restaurants and nightlife, and Osu is one of the few parts of Accra where you can genuinely get about on foot in the evening.
- **East Legon** — modern, residential and comfortable, popular with long-stay visitors and returning diaspora families. Newer buildings and more space for the money, at the cost of being further out.
- **Labadi and La** — the beach side. Choose this if you want the Atlantic outside the door and are happy to drive into town for everything else.
- **The centre, Ridge and Adabraka** — closest to Independence Square, the memorial park, the museum and Makola Market, and where a good share of the budget listings sit. Busiest and noisiest, but the least driving.

Be honest with yourself about the traffic when you choose. A hotel that looks five minutes away on a map can be forty in the wrong direction at the wrong hour.

## Getting Around Accra and Staying Safe — the Honest Version

Two things are worth saying plainly, because they are genuine advantages and travellers rarely hear them. **Ghana is widely regarded as one of the most welcoming and politically stable countries in West Africa**, with a long run of peaceful elections and a deserved reputation for hospitality. And **English is the official language** — used in government, business, signage, schools and the press — so a UK visitor can book a car, order food, ask directions and haggle in a market without a word of anything else. Together those two facts make Ghana a noticeably easier first trip to the region than most of its neighbours.

That is not the same as no precautions. Use **ride-hailing apps or a hotel car rather than flagging a street taxi**, which also saves you the fare negotiation. Keep phones, bags and jewellery out of sight in slow traffic and in crowded markets, where opportunistic theft is the realistic risk rather than anything dramatic. Take normal care walking after dark, arrange your ride home before a night out in Osu rather than looking for one at 2am, draw cash inside banks and malls rather than at quiet street machines, and use the hotel safe. Shared **trotro** minibuses are how most of Accra travels and cost almost nothing, but they take local knowledge to use.

On health and paperwork, three things matter and none of them should be left to the last week. ⚠️ A **yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Ghana** and can be checked at the border, so arrange it early and carry it with your passport. ⚠️ **Malaria is a real risk** here, so see your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly about antimalarial tablets, and pack repellent. And UK passport holders **need a Ghana visa arranged in advance** — Ghana has been rolling out an online or e-visa system alongside the traditional consular route, so check the current official process before you book anything. Drink bottled or filtered water, remember that **Ghana drives on the right**, and expect the **Harmattan** haze if you travel between December and February.

## Accra Hotels FAQs

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Ghana?**
Yes. UK passport holders need a **Ghana visa arranged in advance** rather than on arrival. Ghana has been **rolling out an online or e-visa system** alongside the traditional consular route, so check the current official process before you book flights and allow time for supporting documents and processing. **What currency is used in Accra?**
The **Ghanaian Cedi (GHS)**. Cards work in the better hotels, malls, larger restaurants and supermarkets, but **carry some cash** for markets, trotros, small eateries and tips. Exchange rates move, so treat every pound figure in this guide as a rough conversion for guidance rather than an exact quote.

**Are there direct flights from London to Accra?**
Yes. **British Airways flies direct from London to Kotoka International Airport (ACC) in roughly 6.5 hours**, which makes Accra one of the easiest West African capitals to reach from the UK. There are also plenty of one-stop options via European, Gulf and African hubs, often cheaper if you do not mind a connection.

**Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Ghana?**
⚠️ Yes. A **yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Ghana** and it can be checked on arrival, so carry it with your passport. Arrange the vaccination well ahead of travel through your GP or a travel clinic, as it needs time to take effect and appointments can be scarce.

**Is there malaria in Accra?**
⚠️ **Malaria is a real risk** in Ghana, Accra included. **See your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly** to discuss antimalarial tablets, and pack repellent, cover up at dusk and use the air conditioning or nets your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice.

**Is Accra safe for tourists?**
Ghana is **widely regarded as one of West Africa's most welcoming and stable countries**, and Accra is a comparatively relaxed capital by regional standards. Still take normal precautions: ride-hailing apps or hotel cars rather than street taxis, phones and bags out of sight in traffic and crowded markets, care walking after dark, and use the hotel safe. Read the current UK FCDO travel advice for Ghana before you book.

**Is English spoken in Ghana?**
Yes, and it is a **genuine advantage for UK visitors**. **English is the official language** of Ghana, used for government, business, signage, schooling and the media, so you can book a car, order food, ask directions and haggle in a market without a phrasebook. Twi, Ga, Ewe and other Ghanaian languages are spoken widely alongside it, and learning a greeting goes down very well.

**When is the best time to visit Accra?**
The **dry season, roughly November to March**, is the most comfortable window and the easiest for the road trip west to Cape Coast. The **main rains fall roughly April to June** and make the traffic worse. Between about **December and February the Harmattan** blows dust south from the Sahara, cooling the nights and leaving a haze over the city.

**What is the Harmattan?**
A dry, dusty wind that blows down from the Sahara across West Africa roughly **December to February**. In Accra it means hazier skies, drier air, cooler nights and fine dust on everything. It rarely stops anyone doing anything, though long views are softer for photographers at that time of year.

**Can I visit Cape Coast and Elmina Castles as a day trip from Accra?**
Yes, and for many visitors it is the most important thing they do in Ghana. **Cape Coast Castle and Elmina Castle sit roughly three hours west of Accra**, both **UNESCO World Heritage sites**, and both were holding points where enslaved Africans were confined before being forced onto ships bound for the Americas. A day trip is possible and common, but it is a long day — **staying overnight in Cape Coast** gives these places the time they deserve and lets you add the Kakum canopy walkway.

**What is the Year of Return?**
Ghana's **2019 invitation to people of African descent across the diaspora to come home**, marking four centuries since the first recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia. It brought a large wave of visitors from the United States, the Caribbean, the UK and beyond, and was followed by a longer Beyond the Return programme. Ghana's role as a place of return, and the slave-trade castles on its coast, give travel here a weight few other destinations carry.

**What is the Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Park?**
A memorial park and mausoleum in central Accra dedicated to **Kwame Nkrumah**, who led Ghana to independence in **1957** as the first sub-Saharan African country to break from colonial rule and became a defining voice of **pan-Africanism**.
