  - q: "What is hotel rate parity and is it still enforced?"
    a: "Rate parity was a clause OTAs used to force hotels into charging the same price everywhere, direct included. The EU banned narrow rate-parity clauses in 2024 (building on earlier national bans in France, Germany, Austria and Italy), and the UK's CMA reached a similar settlements with the major OTAs back in 2015 that already loosened wide parity in Britain. In 2026 European and UK hotels are legally free to undercut OTAs on their own websites, and a growing share of chains actively do — narrow parity (matching, not beating, the OTA rate) still shows up in some contracts, but true price-matching enforcement is largely gone."

## The 15–30% You Can't See — What OTAs Actually Charge Hotels

Every OTA price you see has the OTA's commission baked into it. **Large OTAs average around 15% commission on hotel bookings, with rates ranging from 10% to 25% depending on country, property type and visibility programmes** ([Cloudbeds OTA commission guide](https://www.cloudbeds.com/online-travel-agencies/commissions/)). Sign a hotel up to a top-visibility partner programme and the effective cost climbs past 25% per booking. Expedia is one of the more aggressive large OTAs on this front: **independent hotels pay 15–30% commission across the Expedia family (Hotels.com, Vrbo, Orbitz), with a typical range of 18–22%** ([Cloudbeds OTA commission guide](https://www.cloudbeds.com/online-travel-agencies/commissions/)). Big chains negotiate themselves down to 10–15% but pay it across thousands of properties, which is exactly why chain loyalty programmes exist — every booking they can pull off an OTA and onto their own site is pure margin recovered.

That commission isn't an abstract industry stat — it's the headroom every hotel has to undercut the OTA on its own website without losing a penny. A £100 OTA room is, from the hotel's side, an £85 booking after commission. List the same room at £92 on the hotel website and the hotel earns £7 more *and* the guest saves £8. Both sides win. Until relatively recently, hotels across large parts of Europe couldn't legally do this because of "rate parity" clauses baked into their OTA contracts; that's now largely over — see the [price parity rules section](#price-parity-rules) below for exactly what changed and when.

The commission also explains why OTAs are so aggressive about "member deals," "genius-tier" discounts and flash pricing — none of that is the OTA eating its own margin. It's the hotel quietly discounting its own rate *inside* the OTA's platform to try to compete with what it could offer on its own site, while still paying the OTA its cut on top. The traveller sees a "deal"; the actual saving mostly comes out of the hotel's side, not the platform's.

## The Pattern: When Direct Is Cheaper (and When It Isn't)

Run the same search across the three channels — hotel-direct, large-OTA, bed-bank-connected engine — and a clear pattern emerges. **At the major chains — Hilton, Marriott, IHG, Accor — the brand's own website beats large OTAs on the room rate by roughly 2–10% once the loyalty member rate is applied**, and that's before adding the perk stack (free Wi-Fi, late checkout, sometimes breakfast at higher tiers) that doesn't transfer to OTA bookings. On a £150/night room over a two-night weekend that's typically £6–£30 saved on the rate plus another £15–£50 of in-kind value. The exact figure on any given date depends on the property, the season and which member rate you qualify for, but the directional pattern is consistent enough to be the default assumption for chain stays.

The pattern flips for independents. A boutique riad in [Marrakech](/destinations/marrakech) or a family-run guesthouse in [Lisbon](/destinations/lisbon) often shows the *same* price on large OTAs as on its own website, because the property either has a price-parity habit it hasn't broken or knows the OTA's traffic funnel is worth more than the 15% it costs. In those cases the OTA isn't ripping anyone off — it's effectively the property's outsourced marketing department, and the hotel has no commercial reason to undercut its own biggest sales channel.

There's a third, less obvious layer: bed-bank-connected engines. Comparison sites that plug directly into wholesalers like LiteAPI, RateHawk and Webbeds sit one step closer to the hotel's net rate than a retail OTA does, which is why a search on [JetMeAway](/hotels) sometimes surfaces a price below *both* the hotel's own public rate and the big-name OTA figure — no loyalty membership required. Our companion piece goes deep on [exactly how that supply chain works](/blog/hotel-bed-banks-explained-2026).

The skill is in knowing which side of the line your hotel sits on. ## Loyalty-Rate Maths — Bonvoy, Hilton Honors, IHG One, Accor All Compared

Every major chain's loyalty programme is free to join and gives you a member rate that beats the public rate. The discounts are real but smaller than the marketing suggests — the bigger value usually sits in the perk stack, not the headline percentage off.

### Marriott Bonvoy

Exclusive Member Rates run "at least 2% on weekdays and up to 5% on weekends" globally ([Marriott member benefits](https://www.marriott.com/loyalty/member-benefits/member.mi)). Free Wi-Fi and mobile check-in for every tier; Platinum Elite (50 nights/year, or via certain co-branded card spend thresholds) unlocks free breakfast at most properties and guaranteed 4pm late checkout. Bonvoy's real strength for a UK traveller is portfolio breadth — 30+ brands from Moxy and Aloft at the budget end through to Ritz-Carlton and St Regis, meaning your points and status carry across a genuinely wide range of trip types. The **Best Rate Guarantee** is the most generous of the four programmes: match the lower verifiable rate *plus* 25% off the room (20% for Design Hotels), or 5,000 Bonvoy points instead.

### Hilton Honors

Discounts start at roughly 2% off the eligible rate, with advance-purchase rates running up to 17% off Best Available Rate. Free in-room Wi-Fi, no resort fees on points redemptions, and digital key are standard at every tier — a genuinely useful baseline ([Hilton Honors benefits](https://www.hilton.com/en/hilton-honors/member-benefits/)). Gold status (achievable via 4+ qualifying stays a year, or free as a companion benefit on an American Express Platinum card) adds complimentary breakfast at most international properties — this is the single most accessible route to real loyalty value for a UK traveller who doesn't stay in hotels often enough to earn status the slow way.

### IHG One Rewards

Member Rate is guaranteed to be the lowest publicly bookable rate, capped at Best Flexible Rate. 2026 promotions have been running 15–25% off for advance-purchase or 3+ night stays at participating properties ([IHG offers](https://www.ihg.com/content/us/en/offers)). IHG's edge is portfolio depth at the value end specifically — Holiday Inn Express, easyHotel-adjacent budget tiers and Holiday Inn cover a huge share of UK regional and European city-break stays that Marriott and Hilton's UK footprint doesn't reach as densely.

### Accor Live Limitless (ALL)

2–10% off public rates with the standard Members Rate, rising to up to 15% off when booking 15+ days ahead with Advance Saver ([Accor members rate](https://all.accor.com/a/en/offers/ena/members-rate.html)). Accor's real strength for UK travellers is European and South-East Asian density — Ibis, Novotel and Mercure at the mid-market end through to Sofitel, Raffles and Fairmont at the top, all under one loyalty account, which matters more than the raw discount percentage if most of your trips are within Europe.

Book the same hotel via an OTA and you'll usually still earn points, but you forfeit the rate discount, the free breakfast, and the upgrade priority that comes with a direct booking.

## The Best Rate Guarantee Cheat — Find the Room on an OTA, Book It on the Chain

Every major chain runs a Best Rate Guarantee that says: find a verifiable lower rate elsewhere within 24 hours of booking, and we'll match it. **Marriott goes furthest — match the lower rate plus 25% off the room (20% for Design Hotels) or 5,000 Bonvoy points** ([Marriott BRG](https://www.marriott.com/online-hotel-booking.mi)). Hilton, IHG, Hyatt and Accor all run similar programmes, typically matching plus a small bonus.

The practical workflow: search [JetMeAway](/hotels) to surface the price across all seven providers, screenshot the cheapest, then book direct on the chain's site. If the OTA is genuinely cheaper after free-cancellation rules and member-rate maths, file a Best Rate Guarantee claim within 24 hours and the chain effectively pays you to use them. The mechanic exists because chains *want* you off OTAs — they're paying you a fraction of the commission they would otherwise have lost.

A few practical rules that trip people up: the comparison room has to be genuinely identical (same room category, same dates, same cancellation policy — not a flexible OTA rate compared against a non-refundable direct rate), the claim window is short (usually within 24 hours of the direct booking, sometimes before check-in), and packages, opaque rates (where the hotel name is hidden until after booking) and third-party wholesale rates are typically excluded from eligibility. Read the specific programme's terms before assuming a claim will be honoured — but when it qualifies, it's close to free money.

## The Hidden Value of Perks (Breakfast, Late Checkout, Upgrades)

Large OTAs list thousands of "free breakfast included" rates, but it's a marketing layer over a fixed package — you're paying for it whether you eat it or not. Loyalty-programme breakfast is different: it's *added* to your stay regardless of rate type, so the cheapest member-rate room still gets it.

For a couple staying three nights at a Hilton with Gold status, complimentary breakfast at £20 per person per day saves **£120 across the trip** — more than enough to wipe out any small OTA price advantage. Add late checkout (so you don't have to dump your bags somewhere on departure day), the occasional room upgrade, and lounge access at higher tiers, and the loyalty maths is rarely close.

Two practical routes in for UK travellers: an **American Express Platinum card** (annual fee applies, but includes complimentary Hilton Gold and Marriott Gold status — equivalent to roughly 30+ paid Hilton nights or 25 paid Marriott nights of effort), or simply hitting **four stays a year at one chain** to reach the lowest paid status tier organically. Neither route requires spending more than you already do on hotels — it's a matter of consolidating spend into one or two programmes rather than scattering stays across every chain.

Room upgrades are the perk most travellers undervalue. A confirmed or waitlisted upgrade at check-in — even just from a standard room to a slightly larger or better-view category — is routinely worth £30–£80 a night if you priced the higher category separately, and it's available almost exclusively to direct-booking loyalty members, not OTA guests. Front-desk staff have no incentive to prioritise an upgrade for a booking that generated a 20% commission payout to a third party over one that came in commission-free.

<a id="when-ota-wins"></a>

## When the OTA Actually Wins — Independents, Last-Minute, No-Loyalty Stays

Don't assume direct is always cheaper. There are three solid cases for staying on a large OTA:

- **Independent boutique hotels and B&Bs.** Most don't have a competitive direct-booking engine, don't run a member programme, and rely on large OTAs for 60–80% of their reservations. The OTA price is often the *only* price.
- **Last-minute stays.** OTAs' "tonight only" deals and flash drops surface unsold rooms at 20–30% off. Hotels would rather sell at a discount than have an empty room, and OTAs are the fastest sales channel for moving that inventory.
- **One-off destinations where you have no loyalty.** Visiting Tbilisi once for a weekend? You're not going to chase Marriott Bonvoy points across a single stay, so the OTA's selection and reviews are more useful than a chain's small local footprint.

There's a fourth, narrower case worth naming: OTA-exclusive packages. Occasionally a hotel — even a major chain — runs a genuine OTA-only bundle (spa credit, resort credit, a free extra night on a 5+ night stay) to move off-peak inventory without discounting its own public rate and triggering a Best Rate Guarantee claim from its own direct guests. These are worth spotting because they can beat even a loyalty member rate, but they're the exception, not the rule — always read what's actually excluded before assuming the bundle beats a plain direct booking.

<a id="price-parity-rules"></a>

## Price Parity Rules in 2026 — What Actually Changed

"Rate parity" is the clause, historically written into nearly every hotel-OTA contract, that forced a hotel to charge the *same or higher* price on its own website as it did on the OTA. It existed purely to protect the OTA's price leadership — a hotel couldn't legally undercut Expedia or a comparable large OTA on its own site even if it wanted to, because doing so would breach the distribution contract.

That regime has substantially unwound over the past decade, and the pace picked up again through 2024–2026:

- **France, Germany, Austria and Italy** led the way with national bans on "wide" parity clauses (which also barred hotels from discounting on *other* OTAs, not just their own site) starting as early as 2015–2016.
- **The UK's Competition and Markets Authority** reached a settlement with major OTAs back in 2015 that already loosened wide-parity enforcement across Britain specifically.
- **The EU-wide ban on narrow rate-parity clauses** (the remaining restriction on undercutting the OTA specifically on the hotel's *own* website) landed more recently across the bloc, giving European hotels — including many UK travellers' favourite Mediterranean and city-break destinations — clear legal room to price their own site below any OTA.

The practical upshot for 2026: hotels in the EU and UK are now legally free to undercut large OTAs on their own websites, and a meaningfully growing share of chains actively do, because the commission saved on a direct booking is real, immediate margin. Some contracts still contain a weaker "meet, don't beat" clause — a hotel agrees not to charge *more* direct than on the OTA, which still permits it to charge less. Genuinely enforced wide parity (where a hotel can't discount on any channel including its own) is now rare in Europe and the UK specifically, though it persists in some other global markets with weaker regulatory intervention.

What this means practically: don't assume a hotel's own website price is capped at the OTA rate anymore. Check both — the legal floor that used to guarantee price matching no longer exists, and the gap can run either way depending on how aggressively a given property has chosen to compete on its direct channel.

<a id="hidden-fees"></a>

## Hidden Fees — City Tax, Resort Fees and What the Headline Price Leaves Out

The room rate on either channel is rarely the whole story. Four costs consistently get left out of the headline price, and they apply regardless of whether you book direct or through an OTA — the channel affects the *rate*, not whether these charges exist.

**City tax (tourist tax).** A local-government levy, typically £1–£6 per person per night across most of Europe, charged separately at check-in or checkout rather than folded into the booking price. Badge cities charge noticeably more: Venice's tourist tax can run into double digits per person per night in peak season and varies by hotel star rating and time of year; Barcelona, Amsterdam and Paris all levy their own scaled versions. Neither direct bookings nor OTA bookings include it in the advertised rate as standard — budget for it separately on every trip, and check the specific city's current rate before you travel, since several European cities have raised theirs in the past two years.

**Resort fees.** A US, Mexican and Caribbean speciality: a mandatory daily charge from the hotel itself (not a government) covering pool, gym, Wi-Fi and similar amenities, whether you use them or not — typically £20–£50+ a night in Las Vegas, Orlando, Cancún and similar leisure-heavy markets. This is one of the most-complained-about hidden costs in US travel precisely because it's disclosed inconsistently: some hotels show it clearly on their own booking page, some large OTAs bury it until the final confirmation screen. Always scroll to the full price breakdown, on either channel, before comparing two "final" prices.

**Non-refundable deposit terms.** A wholesale or advance-purchase rate — available both direct and via OTA — often runs 10–20% below the flexible equivalent, but the cancellation terms can be genuinely stricter than a comparable "free cancellation" OTA listing implies at a glance. Read the actual policy line, not just the badge that says "best price."

**Dynamic currency conversion.** If a card machine at check-in offers to charge you in GBP "for your convenience" instead of the local currency, decline it — DCC exchange rates are reliably worse than your card issuer's own conversion rate, typically costing an extra 3–5% on the total bill. This applies at the front desk regardless of which channel you originally booked through.

None of these four costs are a reason to prefer direct over OTA or vice versa — they sit *on top of* whichever base rate you're comparing. The only channel-specific advantage is that a hotel's own website is sometimes (not always) more transparent about disclosing resort fees upfront than a large OTA's search-results page, which tends to foreground the headline nightly rate and defer the full breakdown to a later step.

<a id="wholesale-rate-maths"></a>

## Wholesale Rate Maths — Why the Same Room Has Five Different Prices

Understanding the supply chain explains almost everything about why prices vary so much between sites. A hotel room typically moves through some combination of these layers before it reaches you:

**Layer 1 — the hotel's net cost.** The absolute floor: what the property is willing to accept for the room on a given night, factoring in occupancy targets and how far out the search is. Call it £70 for a mid-range European city hotel on a Tuesday in shoulder season.

**Layer 2 — bed banks (wholesalers).** LiteAPI, RateHawk, Webbeds and similar B2B wholesalers buy blocks of that inventory at a negotiated rate close to the net cost — say £75–£80 — because they're committing to volume across many properties at once. Their margin is thin per booking but wide across scale.

**Layer 3 — retail OTAs.** Large consumer-facing OTAs buy from bed banks or negotiate directly with hotels, then add their 15–25% commission on top to fund marketing, customer service and platform costs. That same £75–£80 net rate becomes roughly £92–£106 on a big-name retail OTA listing.

**Layer 4 — the hotel's own direct rate.** Now that parity restrictions have loosened (see above), the hotel can list its own site anywhere it likes above its net cost. Many chains price direct just below the retail OTA figure — say £88–£95 — to win the booking and avoid the commission entirely, while independents with weak direct engines often just mirror the OTA price out of habit or inertia.

**Layer 5 — bed-bank-connected comparison engines.** A site like [JetMeAway](/hotels) that plugs directly into the bed-bank layer can show something close to Layer 2's price with a thin margin added — sometimes £78–£85 for the identical room, undercutting both the retail OTA and, on a given night, even the hotel's own site.

| Layer | Who | Typical price on £70 net-cost room |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. Hotel net cost | The property itself | £70 (not consumer-facing) |
| 2. Bed bank | LiteAPI, RateHawk, Webbeds | £75–£80 |
| 3. Retail OTA | Large consumer OTAs | £92–£106 |
| 4. Hotel direct site | The property's own booking engine | £88–£95 |
| 5. Bed-bank-connected engine | JetMeAway and similar | £78–£85 |

This is why comparison shopping matters more than brand loyalty to any single site: the "cheapest" layer changes hotel by hotel and night by night, depending on how each property has chosen to allocate inventory across its contracts. <a id="cancellation-rights"></a>

## Cancellation Rights — Direct vs OTA

Cancellation policy is where the "which channel is cheaper" question gets complicated, because the cheapest rate on either channel is frequently the least flexible one, and flexibility has real monetary value if your plans might shift.

**Booking direct with a chain.** Cancellation policy is set chain-wide and published clearly — Marriott, Hilton, IHG and Accor all publish a standard flexible-rate cancellation window (commonly 24–48 hours before arrival, property-dependent) alongside a cheaper non-refundable "advance purchase" rate. Because it's the same policy structure across the whole brand, it's easy to know what you're getting before you book, and customer service for a cancellation dispute goes straight to the hotel or the chain's central support line.

**Booking via a large OTA.** Cancellation terms vary rate-by-rate and are sometimes presented less prominently than the headline price — a "free cancellation" badge can apply to one rate on the page while a cheaper adjacent rate on the same listing is non-refundable, and it's easy to click the wrong one without noticing. The upside is that OTAs standardise the *display* of cancellation terms across thousands of different hotels' individual policies, which can make comparing options across many properties faster than checking each hotel's own site individually.

**Booking via an independent hotel's own site.** Cancellation rights are governed entirely by that specific property's policy — there's no chain-wide standard and no OTA-style guarantee layered on top. Read the actual terms rather than assuming a generic "24-hour free cancellation" norm; smaller independents, especially in peak season, sometimes require 7–14 days' notice or charge the first night regardless.

**The practical rule:** if your dates are genuinely fixed (flights already booked, non-movable work trip), the cheaper non-refundable rate is usually the right call on either channel — you're not paying for flexibility you won't use. If there's real uncertainty in your plans, the flexible direct rate from a major chain is usually the safer bet, because the cancellation terms are clearer and the customer-service path is more direct than disputing a cancellation through an OTA's support layer.

<a id="section-75-atol"></a>

## Section 75, ATOL and the Protection Question

Here's where the channel choice has consequences beyond price. **Paying with a UK credit card gives you Section 75 protection on any element over £100 — your card issuer becomes jointly liable with the supplier if the booking goes wrong.** That protection requires an unbroken debtor-creditor-supplier link.

Book direct on a hotel's website with your credit card and the link is clean: you, your card, the hotel. Book the same hotel via an OTA and the link is contested — courts have repeatedly found that **OTAs act as intermediaries, not suppliers**, breaking the Section 75 chain ([MoneySavingExpert forum discussion on OTAs and Section 75](https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6416297/section-75-does-not-apply-to-booking-com)). The FCA acknowledges the uncertainty and says outcomes depend on the "specific commercial structure" of each booking ([FCA guidance on s.75 and travel](https://www.fca.org.uk/freedom-information/information-how-consumer-credit-act-1974-s75-applies-travel-services-payments-september-2021)).

Hotel-only bookings aren't covered by ATOL either way — that's flight-inclusive packages only ([financial protection guide](/financial-protection)). ATOL doesn't distinguish between a direct hotel booking and an OTA hotel booking, because neither has a flight bundled into it — the protection simply doesn't engage until you buy flight + accommodation together from a licensed UK package operator. If you'd rather have that layer of cover, that's the case for booking a flight + hotel as a genuine [package](/packages) rather than two standalone purchases — we cover exactly when that trade-off is worth it in our companion piece on [booking flight and hotel separately vs as a package](/blog/book-flight-hotel-separately-vs-package), and unpack the ATOL rules themselves in [the DIY travel myth](/blog/atol-direct-booking-myth-2026).

So the practical safety stack for hotel-only travel, on either channel: pay direct on credit card where possible for the cleanest Section 75 chain, buy [travel insurance](/insurance) the day you book for cancellation cover, and keep all confirmations in one place. If you do book via an OTA and want the strongest available protection, still use a credit card rather than a debit card — even a contested Section 75 claim is a materially stronger position than no statutory protection at all.

## Worked Example — Mid-Range Chain Stay (3 Nights, Hilton Garden Inn, City Break)

A solo traveller with Hilton Honors Gold status (via an American Express Platinum companion benefit) booking three nights at a Hilton Garden Inn in Amsterdam.

This is also the segment where luxury independents most often hold their direct rate deliberately close to the OTA figure (protecting perceived exclusivity, as covered in the FAQ above) rather than undercutting hard — the saving is real but modest, and the perks are where the real value sits.

## The Bottom Line

For chain hotels — Hilton, Marriott, IHG, Accor, Hyatt — **booking direct almost always wins** in 2026. Member rates are 2–25% cheaper depending on the programme and booking window, free perks add another £10–£40 per night in real value, Best Rate Guarantees catch the rare exceptions, and Section 75 keeps your credit card protection intact. The regulatory unwinding of rate-parity clauses across the EU and UK means chains are now legally free to undercut large OTAs on their own websites, and most have started to.

For independent boutiques, last-minute stays, and one-off trips with no loyalty in play, **the OTA is fine** — sometimes it's the only sensible option, and our worked budget example above shows a genuine tie. Luxury independents sit in between: the direct-rate saving is usually real but modest, with the bigger value in service perks that only attach to a direct relationship.

Always factor in the costs neither headline price shows — city tax, resort fees, and the cancellation terms buried below the "book now" button — before deciding a price comparison is actually finished. The trick is knowing which case you're in before you click.
