If you love green landscapes and dramatic skies over cheaper hotel rates, the monsoon has its fans; if you want reliable dry sightseeing, come October to February instead."

## Where to Stay in Pune: Choosing Your Base

Pune is a big, spread-out city where the traffic between districts is the single most consequential thing about your choice of hotel. Get the area right and everything is a short ride away; get it wrong and you will spend an hour each way in the car. Here is the honest breakdown.

**Koregaon Park and Kalyani Nagar** are the right answer for most leisure visitors. Koregaon Park — universally shortened to KP — is Pune's most pleasant central district: quiet, leafy lanes laid out in a numbered grid, the best concentration of restaurants, cafés and bars in the city, the famous German Bakery, boutique shopping and the Osho International Meditation Resort. It is walkable in a way little else in Pune is, it feels relaxed and safe, and it sits a sensible middle distance from both the airport and the old-city sights. Blue Diamond Pune IHCL SeleQtions is the landmark here; neighbouring Kalyani Nagar offers cheaper options like Magnus Riverside and Magnus Vosiv Suites in the same leafy belt.

**The central Bund Garden, Camp and railway-station belt** is the practical all-rounder. This is the heart of the modern city — Bund Garden Road, the old cantonment shopping district of Camp with its MG Road, and Pune Junction railway station, which matters if you are arriving or leaving by train. From here Koregaon Park, the old city and the station are all within a short ride, which is why so much of this guide's mid-range and central-budget stock sits here: Conrad Pune, Sheraton Grand Pune Bund Garden, Lemon Tree Premier City Center, Crowne Plaza, Hotel Aurora Towers, Hotel Centro and the central Treebo and Shantai options. If you want one base that does a bit of everything, this is it.

**Viman Nagar and the Nagar Road corridor** are the airport side. Pune Airport (PNQ) at Lohegaon is only about 7 to 10 kilometres out, and Viman Nagar is effectively its neighbourhood — with the huge Phoenix Marketcity mall, plenty of restaurants and a stack of hotels from the Novotel down to the ibis and Lemon Tree. Hyatt Pune and Four Points by Sheraton sit along the same Nagar Road corridor. It is a genuinely convenient base for an early or late flight, close to the eastern tech parks, and only a short drive from Koregaon Park — a strong choice for a first, short trip.

**Kharadi, Hinjewadi, Magarpatta and Pimpri-Chinchwad** are the working districts. Kharadi (EON, World Trade Center) on the east and Hinjewadi (the Rajiv Gandhi Infotech Park) on the far west are Pune's two great IT clusters; Magarpatta at Hadapsar is a planned tech township in the south-east; and Pimpri-Chinchwad in the north-west is the manufacturing belt. Between them they explain most of the hotel stock in this guide — Radisson Blu Kharadi, Hyatt Place Hinjawadi, Fairfield Kharadi, the ibis and Treebo Hinjewadi properties, Bloom Magarpatta, the StayBird Kharadi apartment-hotels and the Pimpri budget chains. **If you are working in one of these, stay in it** — the cross-city commute is brutal at peak times. **If you are not, be careful**: these are functional office and factory suburbs with essentially nothing to see, and Hinjewadi in particular is a long, traffic-heavy way from the old city.

**Shivaji Nagar and the Deccan colleges area** are the value-and-character option on the central-west side. This is the academic and old-Pune heart near the University, Fergusson College, the Law College and the JM Road and FC Road food scenes — student territory, which means the eating is cheap and excellent. The Pride Hotel is the four-star pick, and there is a deep run of value Treebo and FabHotel properties here (Sahara, Luxe Suite, Ranjeet, Deccan 8, The Deccan Royaale) that keep you close to both the colleges and the old-city monuments.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Pune

Start where the Marathas ruled. **Shaniwar Wada** is the 18th-century fortified palace of the Peshwas, the prime ministers who ran the Maratha Empire from Pune at the height of its power. A great fire in the 1820s gutted the wooden interior, so what survives is the massive stone fortification, the grand Delhi Gate studded with elephant-proof spikes, and the foundations and gardens within — more atmosphere and story than intact palace, with an evening sound-and-light show. A short walk away, the **Dagdusheth Halwai Ganpati temple** is one of the most beloved Ganesh temples in India, a riot of gold and devotion that comes utterly alive during the Ganesh Chaturthi festival, and the **Lal Mahal** and the old-city lanes of Kasba Peth complete a rewarding morning on foot.

The most moving stop in the city is the **Aga Khan Palace**, on Nagar Road across the river. This elegant Italianate palace, donated by the Aga Khan, is where the British interned Mahatma Gandhi, his wife Kasturba and his secretary Mahadev Desai after the Quit India movement of 1942 — and both Kasturba Gandhi and Mahadev Desai died here in captivity, their memorials standing quietly in the grounds. It now houses a Gandhi museum set in peaceful gardens, and it is an easy, worthwhile half-day that puts Pune at the centre of the independence story.

Pune's other faces are cultural and academic. The **Raja Dinkar Kelkar Museum** holds an extraordinary one-man collection of Indian decorative and everyday arts; the **Pataleshwar cave temple** is a rock-cut 8th-century Shiva shrine tucked improbably beside busy JM Road; **Parvati Hill** rewards a short climb with a temple complex and the best free view over the city; and **Saras Bagh** and the **Empress Botanical Garden** are pleasant green breaks. The **Osho International Meditation Resort** in Koregaon Park is a destination in its own right for some visitors, and the surrounding KP lanes are the city's café-and-restaurant heartland.

Then there are the hills, which for many people are the real reason to base yourself here. **Sinhagad Fort** — the Lion's Fort — sits on a Sahyadri ridge about 30 kilometres south-west, tied to the legendary 1670 night assault by Tanaji Malusare; you can drive most of the way up or trek the popular trail, and the reward is sweeping Deccan views and roadside stalls selling hot pithla-bhakri and buttermilk. Further out, the twin hill stations of **Lonavala and Khandala**, about 65 to 70 kilometres towards Mumbai, are the classic Pune escape — misty valleys, waterfalls in the monsoon, the ancient Karla and Bhaja Buddhist caves and the local chikki sweet to take home. All of this is at its most spectacular and green in and just after the **monsoon**, which is exactly when the trails are slippery and the crowds are largest.

And then the food, because Pune eats extremely well and extremely cheaply. This is one of the most **vegetarian-friendly** cities you can visit, with a deep Maharashtrian tradition: **misal pav** (a fiery sprouted-bean curry with bread), **vada pav**, **sabudana khichdi**, **puran poli** and the unlimited **Maharashtrian thali** that places like Hotel Shreyas built their name on. Around it sits a whole café-and-bakery culture — Shrewsbury biscuits, the old Irani cafés, the thick mango milkshake called **mastani**, and the student-institution South Indian houses on Fergusson College Road. Between the old-city snack stalls and the Koregaon Park restaurants you can eat superbly for very little money.

## Getting Around Pune

The **Pune Metro** is the pleasant surprise: a growing, clean, air-conditioned and cheap network that already runs a broadly north-south line between Pimpri-Chinchwad and Swargate and a broadly east-west line between Vanaz and Ramwadi through the central Deccan, Shivaji Nagar and the river area. It is genuinely useful when your route happens to match a line — central Pune to the Deccan colleges, for instance — but it does not yet reach every district a visitor wants, so you will still rely on other transport for the airport, Koregaon Park and the outer tech parks.

For everything else, use **Uber and Ola**. Both operate across Pune, give you a fixed fare and a tracked route, and let you book autorickshaws through the same app — which removes the meter haggling that makes street rickshaws tiring, and autos are often the quickest way to slice through traffic on short hops. Fares are low by UK standards. Have your hotel name and area written down to show drivers, since duplicate place names and pronunciation cause confusion, and expect longer pickup waits in the old-city lanes than in Koregaon Park or Viman Nagar.

Traffic is the real constraint. Pune grew fast around narrow old-city streets and sprawling new suburbs, and the commute corridors — especially out to Hinjewadi and across the east-west divide — clog heavily at peak times, with two-wheelers weaving through everything. It is not quite Bangalore, but assume every cross-city journey takes half again as long as the map suggests, plan around the morning and evening rush, and lean on the metro where you can. Do not self-drive; there is no need, and while India drives on the left as the UK does, the lane discipline will not feel familiar. Tap water is not drinkable — bottled or filtered only, including for brushing teeth. Tipping is customary but small: round up in restaurants if no service charge has been added, 50 to 100 rupees a bag for porters, nothing expected in taxis. And everything is in **rupees** — cards work at hotels, malls and chain restaurants, UPI apps dominate elsewhere, and sterling is accepted nowhere.

## Pune Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Pune?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is not issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party visa agencies charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Pune?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work at hotels, malls and chain restaurants, and UPI apps dominate everywhere else, but carry small rupee notes for autorickshaws, market stalls and tips. Sterling is not accepted anywhere.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Pune?**
No. There is no direct flight between the UK and Pune (PNQ). Pune's airport is mainly a domestic hub with a handful of Gulf and regional international routes, so the two practical options from Britain are both one stop: fly a Gulf carrier — Emirates via Dubai, Etihad via Abu Dhabi, Qatar Airways via Doha — connecting onward to Pune, or fly to Mumbai (BOM), which has direct London services, and continue to Pune by road or a short domestic hop. Compare both on your dates, because the cheaper routing changes week to week.

**Should I fly into Mumbai or Pune?**
It depends on your onward plan. Mumbai (BOM) is the main long-haul gateway, with direct flights from London and the widest choice of fares, and Pune is only about three to four hours away by road on the expressway — so flying to Mumbai and transferring is completely normal and often cheaper. Fly straight into Pune (PNQ) if you can find a good one-stop Gulf connection and you want to skip the Mumbai transfer, which saves you half a day and the expressway drive. For a Pune-only trip, PNQ is more convenient; for a trip that also takes in Mumbai, land at BOM.

**How far is Pune from Mumbai, and how do I get between them?**
About 150 kilometres, and the two cities are joined by the Mumbai-Pune Expressway — one of India's best motorways. A private car or taxi takes roughly three to four hours depending on traffic leaving Mumbai and the ghat section through the hills. There are also frequent air-conditioned coaches and a scenic mainline train through the Western Ghats. A short domestic flight exists but rarely makes sense once you count airport time. Most UK visitors who land at Mumbai pre-book a car for the expressway run.

**When is the best time to visit Pune?**
October to February. Those are the dry, pleasant months, with warm days and genuinely cool evenings — Pune sits on the Deccan plateau at around 560 metres, so its winter nights are milder and fresher than coastal Mumbai just over the hills. This is the season for sightseeing, forts and day trips, and hotel rates are at their firmest through it. February and early March are still very good before the summer heat builds.

**What is Pune like during the monsoon (June to September)?**
Lush, dramatic and very wet. The south-west monsoon soaks Pune and the surrounding Western Ghats from June to September, turning the hills, forts and waterfalls a brilliant green — it is the reason nearby hill stations like Lonavala and Khandala are so popular in these months. The trade-off is real rain: sightseeing gets interrupted, Sinhagad and the trekking trails turn slippery and misty, and city traffic worsens. If you love green landscapes and dramatic skies over cheaper hotel rates, the monsoon has its fans; if you want reliable dry sightseeing, come October to February instead.

**How hot does Pune get in summer?**
March to May is the hot, dry season, with daytime temperatures commonly in the high thirties Celsius and occasionally higher. It is genuinely warm, but Pune's plateau altitude means it is usually less oppressive than the coast because the humidity is lower and the evenings cool down more. Air conditioning is still a sensible non-negotiable in this window, and outdoor sights like Shaniwar Wada, Sinhagad Fort or Parvati Hill are best done early in the morning or late in the afternoon.

**Is Pune a tourist city or mainly a business and university city?**
Honestly, more the latter — and it is better to know that going in. Pune is a huge university and IT and manufacturing city: it is nicknamed the Oxford of the East for its dozens of colleges and students, it has vast technology parks at Hinjewadi and Kharadi, and an industrial belt at Pimpri-Chinchwad. That is why so many of its hotels are polished business properties near office parks rather than tourist hotels near monuments. It still has a strong cultural core — Shaniwar Wada, the Aga Khan Palace, Dagdusheth Ganpati temple, the Koregaon Park scene and nearby hill forts — but it rewards a curious traveller more than a bucket-list one, and many visitors pair it with Mumbai or use it as a base for the Sahyadri hills.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Pune?**
Koregaon Park or the central Bund Garden and Camp belt. Koregaon Park is the leafy, upmarket district — tree-lined lanes, the best restaurants and cafes, the Osho meditation resort and an easy, walkable feel that first-time visitors like. The central belt around Bund Garden Road, Camp (MG Road) and Pune railway station puts you close to the old-city monuments and transport. Both are a sensible middle distance from the airport and from the tech parks, which is what you want if you are mixing sightseeing with a meeting or two.

Because that is where the work is. Hinjewadi (the Rajiv Gandhi Infotech Park) and Kharadi (EON and World Trade Center) are Pune's two biggest IT clusters, and Viman Nagar sits next to the airport with malls and offices around it. The upside for a visitor is a lot of clean, modern, well-run hotels at sharp prices; the downside is that these districts are functional office suburbs with little to see, and Hinjewadi in particular is a long, traffic-heavy way from the old city.
