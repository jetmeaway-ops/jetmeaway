  - q: "Is Lima safe for tourists?"

  - q: "Is Lima at altitude — will I get altitude sickness?"
    a: "No. This is the single most useful thing to know before a Peru trip and a lot of people get it wrong. Lima sits on the Pacific coast, essentially at sea level — the clifftop parks in Miraflores are about seventy metres above the water and that is as high as the tourist city gets. There is no altitude effect in Lima whatsoever. You can eat a huge lunch, drink a pisco sour and go for a run on the Malecón on your first afternoon with no consequences at all. Altitude becomes a real issue the moment you fly inland: Cusco sits at around 3,400 metres and that is high enough to make a meaningful number of visitors feel genuinely unwell for a day or two. Spending your first nights in Lima is not acclimatisation — it does nothing for you at sea level — but it does mean you arrive in Cusco rested rather than straight off a fifteen-hour flight, which helps more than people expect."
  - q: "Is Machu Picchu a day trip from Lima?"
    a: "No, and anyone selling you one is selling you a very bad day. Machu Picchu is roughly 1,100km from Lima and reaching it is a chain of connections: a flight from Lima to Cusco of about an hour and twenty minutes, a road transfer of one and a half to two hours down into the Sacred Valley to Ollantaytambo, a train of about an hour and a half to Aguas Calientes, and then a shuttle bus up the switchbacks to the entrance. Entry is by timed ticket on a fixed circuit, bought in advance, and the popular slots sell out weeks ahead in high season. The realistic minimum from Lima is three days and two nights, and four days is far more comfortable because it lets you sleep at the lower altitude of the Sacred Valley before you go up. Plan it as its own trip within the trip, not as an excursion."
  - q: "How do I get from Lima to Cusco?"
    a: "Fly. LATAM, Sky Airline and JetSMART all run the Lima to Cusco route several times a day and the flight takes about an hour and twenty minutes, with fares that are frequently cheaper than a UK domestic train. Book morning departures wherever possible, because Cusco sits in a bowl in the Andes and afternoon weather causes far more delays and diversions than morning weather does. The overland alternative is a bus of 20 to 24 hours through the mountains, which the long-distance Peruvian coach companies do far more comfortably than the description suggests, but it is a serious commitment of holiday time. Whichever you choose, remember that you are going from sea level to 3,400 metres in one step — take the first afternoon in Cusco very gently, drink a lot of water and skip the alcohol for the first night."
  - q: "When is the best time to visit Lima?"
    a: "December to April, if you are choosing purely on Lima. That is the southern summer on the coast: blue skies, daytime highs in the mid to high twenties, warm evenings and a city that spends its weekends on the beaches south of town. May to November is the garúa season, when a persistent grey marine layer sits over the city, and it is atmospheric rather than pleasant. The complication is that these two seasons are the exact opposite of what you want in the Andes: the Cusco and Machu Picchu dry season runs roughly May to September, which is Lima's grey half. If you are doing both — and most people are — the compromise most travellers land on is April to May or September to October, when the coast still gets sun and the mountains are mostly dry."
  - q: "What is the garúa, and why is Lima grey for half the year?"
    a: "The garúa is a thick, low marine fog that sits over Lima from roughly May to November, produced by the cold Humboldt current running up the coast meeting warmer air above it. It gives the city month after month of flat grey skies, high humidity, a fine damp drizzle that never becomes proper rain, and daytime temperatures around 15 to 19°C. It is the defining fact of Lima's climate and the reason Herman Melville is said to have called it the saddest city in the world. What it is not is cold, and it is not wet either — Lima sits in one of the driest coastal deserts on earth and measurable rainfall is a few millimetres a year. The practical effect is on your photographs and your mood rather than your comfort, and the food, the museums and the restaurants are entirely unaffected."
  - q: "Do I need to pack for cold weather in Lima?"
    a: "Not cold, but bring layers, because Lima is chillier than a lot of people expect from a city fourteen degrees south of the equator. In the garúa months of May to November, days run around 15 to 19°C with heavy humidity and there is no sun to warm you up, so a light jacket or a fleece plus a jumper is genuinely useful, particularly on the clifftops where the wind comes off the Pacific. In the December to April summer you want shorts, sunglasses and strong sun cream, because the UV on a clear Lima day is fierce. In either season, note that Peruvian buildings very rarely have central heating, so a grey July evening indoors can feel colder than the thermometer suggests. And if you are continuing to Cusco, pack a proper warm layer regardless of the month — the Andes at 3,400 metres get close to freezing at night."
  - q: "Is the tap water safe to drink in Lima?"
    a: "No — treat Lima tap water as not drinkable. It is chlorinated and technically treated, but the mineral content and the state of some older building plumbing mean visitors regularly come unstuck, and the standard advice from every Peruvian and every guidebook is to drink bottled or filtered water. Most hotels provide bottled water in the room and many have filtered dispensers on each floor, which is worth asking about, because it saves both money and plastic. Brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine. Be a little careful with ice and raw salads at the very cheapest street stalls, though established cevicherías and restaurants use treated water and ice as a matter of course."
  - q: "What should I eat in Lima?"
    a: "Lima is one of the great eating cities of the world and the reason is migration. Peruvian coastal cooking is what happened when Spanish, indigenous Andean, West African, Chinese, Japanese and Italian kitchens spent four centuries in the same port. The results have their own names: chifa is Chinese-Peruvian, and lomo saltado — beef stir-fried with onion, tomato, soy and chips — is its most famous export; nikkei is Japanese-Peruvian, which is where tiradito, the sashimi-shaped cousin of ceviche, comes from. Beyond that, eat ají de gallina, a mild creamy chilli chicken over rice; causa, layered whipped yellow potato with fillings; anticuchos, skewers of marinated beef heart grilled at street corners in the evening; and pollo a la brasa, the spit-roasted chicken that is Peru's true national dish by volume. And eat potatoes with everything, because Peru has thousands of varieties and takes them very seriously indeed."
  - q: "What is ceviche and where should I eat it in Lima?"
    a: "Ceviche is raw fish cured in the juice of Peruvian limes with sliced red onion, salt and hot ají limo chilli, served with sweet potato, large-kernel choclo corn and toasted cancha corn on the side, and it is the national dish. The cloudy citrus marinade left in the bowl is leche de tigre, which you drink, and which Limeños will tell you cures a hangover. The single most important rule is timing: ceviche is a lunch dish, the fish comes in that morning, and a proper cevichería closes in the early afternoon rather than serving it at nine at night. Anywhere offering ceviche at dinner is either very good and buying twice a day, or not worth your money. Miraflores and Barranco have the well-known dining rooms, the market cevicherías in Surquillo do the same thing for a fraction of the price, and the fishing port of Chorrillos does it with the boats in view."
  - q: "Can I get a table at Central or Maido?"
    a: "Only if you plan a long way ahead. Central, Virgilio Martínez and Pía León's restaurant in Barranco, and Maido, Mitsuharu Tsumura's nikkei restaurant in Miraflores, are the two names that have made Lima a global food destination — Central was named the world's best restaurant in 2023 and Maido took the same title in 2025. Both release tables online, typically a couple of months in advance, and both are effectively sold out within hours of opening a new period. If that is the trip you want, book the restaurant before you book the flight. If you miss out, Lima is unusually rich in the tier below: Kjolle in the same Barranco building as Central, Astrid y Gastón in a colonial hacienda in San Isidro, Rafael and La Mar in Miraflores and Isolina in Barranco are all serious kitchens, and a market lunch in Surquillo for a few pounds is closer to the soul of the city than any tasting menu."
  - q: "What is a pisco sour, and what else should I drink in Lima?"
    a: "The pisco sour is Peru's national cocktail: pisco, the clear unaged grape brandy distilled on the south coast, shaken with fresh lime juice, sugar syrup and egg white and finished with a few drops of Angostura bitters over the foam. Peru and Chile argue about who invented pisco and you should not take sides while holding a Peruvian glass. Beyond that, try chilcano, which is pisco with ginger ale and lime and is the everyday drink rather than the special-occasion one; chicha morada, a non-alcoholic purple corn drink with pineapple and cinnamon that comes with almost every set lunch; and Inca Kola, the fluorescent yellow bubblegum-flavoured soft drink that outsells Coca-Cola in Peru and that you should try exactly once. Peruvian coffee is excellent and increasingly well brewed in Barranco and Miraflores."
  - q: "Are Uber and taxi apps available in Lima?"
    a: "Yes, and you should use them. Uber, Cabify, DiDi and InDrive all operate across Lima and between them they cover every district a visitor will use, with fares that are extremely low by UK standards — a cross-city trip from Miraflores to the Centro Histórico typically costs a few pounds. Booking in an app removes the two problems with Lima's street taxis: they are unmetered, so every ride begins with a negotiation you will lose, and they are not all licensed, which is the specific risk the FCDO flags. If you do take a street taxi, use one your hotel calls for you. Set the apps up with a working payment method before you fly, because signing up on Peruvian mobile data at midnight in an airport arrivals hall is nobody's idea of a good start."
  - q: "Do I need a hire car in Lima?"
    a: "No, and for most visitors it would be an actively bad decision. Lima's traffic is heavy, fast and governed more by confidence than by lane markings, parking in Miraflores and San Isidro is difficult and expensive, and ride-hailing is so cheap that a full day of taxis costs less than a day's car hire before you have paid for fuel. The city's own transport is also better than its reputation: the Metropolitano bus rapid transit runs in a dedicated corridor down the Vía Expresa between the Centro and Barranco, and Metro Line 1 serves the eastern and southern districts. Hire a car only if you are driving out along the Panamericana under your own steam — south to Pachacamac, Paracas and Huacachina, or north to the Caral pyramids — and even then, a bus or an organised trip is usually simpler."
  - q: "What currency does Peru use — should I take US dollars?"
    a: "The currency is the Peruvian sol, written S/ and abbreviated PEN, and it is what you should be spending. US dollars are accepted in some hotels, tour offices and upmarket restaurants, but at a rate set for the convenience of whoever is taking them, and change will come back in soles anyway. Take a small amount of cash for the first day, then withdraw soles from ATMs inside bank branches or shopping centres rather than from machines on the pavement, and always decline the machine's offer to charge you in pounds — that is dynamic currency conversion and it costs you every time. Cards are widely accepted in Miraflores, San Isidro and Barranco and much less so in markets, at street food stalls, in the smaller Centro hotels and for short taxi rides, so carry some notes and keep small ones."
  - q: "How much should I tip in Lima?"
    a: "Around 10 per cent in restaurants, and check the bill first, because many mid-range and upmarket places already add a servicio charge of about 10 per cent, in which case anything more is a bonus rather than an obligation. Tipping is not the deeply embedded expectation it is in the United States: taxi and app drivers are not tipped, bar staff are not tipped per drink, and nobody will chase you down the street. Small tips are normal and appreciated for hotel porters and housekeeping, for guides on a day tour, and for the men who help you park. Tip in soles rather than in dollars or pounds, because changing small foreign notes in Peru is a nuisance for the person you are trying to be kind to."
  - q: "Do I need to speak Spanish in Lima?"
    a: "You will get by without it, but you will have a better trip with a little. English is common in the international hotels, in the Miraflores and San Isidro restaurants, at the museums and among younger staff generally. It is much less common in taxis, in markets, at the cheaper cevicherías, in the smaller budget hotels in this guide and in the Centro Histórico. Peruvian Spanish is clear and relatively slow compared with, say, Chilean, which makes it a friendly place to use a phrasebook. Download an offline translation pack before you go, learn the numbers and the food words, and be aware that a menú del día is a fixed-price set lunch — usually a starter, a main and a drink for a very small sum — and knowing that phrase alone will save you a fortune over a fortnight."
  - q: "Is Lima good for families?"
    a: "Better than most people assume, though it is a city break rather than a holiday. The Malecón clifftop parks run for around ten kilometres with playgrounds, cycle hire and skate parks, and children love watching the paragliders launch straight off the cliff at Miraflores. The Circuito Mágico del Agua in the Parque de la Reserva is a genuine hit — thirteen illuminated, walk-through fountains with an evening light and music show. The catacombs beneath the San Francisco monastery are the sort of thing that makes a trip memorable for a nine-year-old, the Parque de las Leyendas zoo sits inside an archaeological site, and the beaches south of the city at Punta Hermosa are an easy day out in summer. The constraints are traffic, the amount of car time between districts, and the grey garúa months when the coast is not much fun for small children. Family rooms and apart-hotels with kitchens are widely available and cheap by UK standards."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Lima?"
    a: "Three full days is right, and two is the minimum that does not feel like a waste. Day one for the Centro Histórico — the Plaza Mayor, the cathedral, the San Francisco catacombs, the balconies and a market lunch — then back to Miraflores for the evening. Day two for the museums and the coast: the Museo Larco in Pueblo Libre in the morning, the Huaca Pucllana pyramid in Miraflores, then the Malecón walk from Larcomar down to Barranco in the late afternoon, ending at the Puente de los Suspiros. Day three for eating properly, a cevichería lunch, the MATE museum and the Barranco street art, or a half-day out to the Pachacamac ruins south of the city. A fourth day buys you the beaches or a long lunch you do not have to rush. If Lima is bookending a Machu Picchu trip, take two nights on the way in and one on the way out."

## What to Do in Lima

- **The Centro Histórico** — the colonial city Pizarro laid out in 1535 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1988. The **Plaza Mayor** holds the cathedral, the Archbishop's Palace and the Government Palace, whose changing of the guard is a proper brass-band spectacle around midday. Look up everywhere for the enclosed wooden **balconies** in dark Moorish latticework, the signature of old Lima. Do it in daylight and take a car back out.
- **Convento de San Francisco** — the yellow-and-white Baroque monastery a few streets from the Plaza Mayor, with a magnificent library of ancient volumes, a Moorish-tiled cloister and, underneath it, the **catacombs**: bone-lined ossuary passages holding the remains of an estimated 25,000 people, arranged in patterns. The single most memorable hour in the Centro, and a guided visit is compulsory.
- **Museo Larco** — in Pueblo Libre, and the reason to give Lima a proper day. A private collection of pre-Columbian art housed in an eighteenth-century mansion built on a pre-Columbian pyramid, with a garden of bougainvillea, an open storeroom holding tens of thousands of catalogued ceramics, a gold and silver room, and the notorious pre-Columbian erotic pottery gallery. The café in the garden is one of the nicest lunches in the city.
- **The Malecón and the Miraflores clifftops** — around ten kilometres of connected clifftop parks running above the Pacific from Magdalena through Miraflores to Barranco, with lawns, cycle paths, cat-filled squares, the **Parque del Amor** and its enormous kissing-couple sculpture, and a launch site where paragliders take off directly over the traffic. Walk it at sunset. In the garúa months you will not see one, but walk it anyway.
- **Huaca Pucllana** — a stepped adobe pyramid from around 500 AD, sitting in the middle of residential **Miraflores** with apartment blocks on all four sides. Built from millions of hand-made mud bricks stood on end, it is floodlit at night and there is a well-regarded restaurant beside it with a view over the ruins. **Huaca Huallamarca** in San Isidro is the same idea on a smaller scale.
- **Barranco** — the bohemian district south of Miraflores: republican-era mansions in faded ochre and blue, the wooden **Puente de los Suspiros** and the steps of the Bajada de Baños running down to the sea, murals on every other wall, the **MATE** museum in Mario Testino's converted townhouse, and the bar and live-music scene that fills the streets on Friday and Saturday.
- **Circuito Mágico del Agua** — thirteen illuminated fountains in the Parque de la Reserva, including tunnels you walk through and a choreographed evening show of water, light and music. It is unashamedly a spectacle and children love it. Go after dark, which is when the whole thing exists.
- **Mercado de Surquillo** — the market across the Vía Expresa from Miraflores, and the best cheap eating in the city. Rows of Andean potatoes and Amazonian fruit you have never seen, and cevicherías at the back charging a few pounds for what the clifftop restaurants charge twenty for. Go at lunchtime, which is when the fish is fresh and the stalls are open.
- **Pachacamac** — a huge pre-Inca and Inca pilgrimage complex about 40km south of the city, with temples and plazas spread across a desert bluff above the sea and a good modern site museum. The easiest and most rewarding half-day out of Lima, and a useful preparation for what you are going to see in Cusco.
- **The food** — treat it as a sight in its own right. Book **Central** in Barranco or **Maido** in Miraflores months ahead if you want the two restaurants that have topped the world list; otherwise eat ceviche at lunch, anticuchos from a street grill in the evening, a menú del día somewhere unglamorous, and pollo a la brasa at least once. It is the best-value world-class eating on the planet.

## Where to Base Yourself in Lima

Lima is enormous and spread thin along the coast, so the district you choose decides how much of your holiday you spend in traffic. Four names cover almost every visitor.

**Miraflores — the default, and the right one for most people.** The clifftop district with the biggest concentration of hotels at every price on this page, the Malecón parks, Parque Kennedy and its famous cats, the Larcomar centre cut into the cliff face, Huaca Pucllana in the middle of the housing, and more restaurants per street than anywhere else in Peru. It is safe-feeling, well lit, walkable, well served by taxis and used to visitors. The budget tier here is unusually strong — five branded rooms between £30 and £39 — which is what makes Lima different from most capitals. The criticism is that it can feel a little polished and international; the answer to that is a twenty-minute walk south.

**Barranco — the character.** Three kilometres along the clifftop, and a different city: nineteenth-century mansions in peeling colour, the Bridge of Sighs, street art, small galleries, the MATE museum, Central in a converted building on Avenida Pedro de Osma, and bars that fill up late. It is compact and genuinely walkable, and it is the district Limeños go out in. Fewer hotel rooms, more noise at the weekend, and the best evenings in the city.

**San Isidro — the quiet money.** The financial district and the most expensive residential one, built around the **El Olivar** grove of centuries-old olive trees, with embassies, wide green streets, the El Golf country-club quarter and the Huaca Huallamarca pyramid. Calm, extremely comfortable, excellent for serious restaurants and terrible for spontaneity — you will take a car out in the evening. Several of the best-value four-stars in this guide are here, including a Novotel at £42 and a boutique four-star at £44.

**Centro Histórico — the history and the value.** Where the colonial city actually is, and where the cheapest genuinely central rooms sit. Fascinating by day, deserted by early evening, and ringed by districts you should not wander into. Book it if churches and museums are your priority and you are content to travel out for dinner; book Miraflores if this is your first night in South America.

**Callao and the airport corridor.** Purely functional, and cheap: the £18 room in this guide is here. Callao is Lima's port and its roughest large district, with the notable exception of the regenerated **Monumental Callao** arts quarter, and there is nothing walkable near the terminal. Book it for the night before a dawn flight to Cusco and for nothing else.

**Magdalena del Mar, Pueblo Libre and Surco.** Ordinary residential Lima, cheaper than the big four, better connected than they look and increasingly used by longer-stay visitors. Pueblo Libre has the Museo Larco and a famous old tavern; Magdalena has its own stretch of clifftop park. Sensible on a second visit or a long stay, less so on a short first trip.

## Honest Practicalities for Lima

**Getting there always means a connection.** No UK airport flies non-stop to Lima. The European routings — **Madrid** with Iberia, Air Europa or LATAM, **Amsterdam** with KLM, **Paris** with Air France, **Frankfurt** with Lufthansa — are the least stressful because you clear immigration only once, in Peru, and the long leg is a single twelve-hour hop. The Americas routings through **Miami**, Dallas, Houston or Atlanta mean clearing US immigration in transit, which requires a valid **ESTA** and a generous layover, and Avianca via **Bogotá** adds a second short flight. Reckon on **15 to 17 hours door to door** from London, and remember Peru runs on UTC−5 all year with no daylight saving.

**Entry is simple.** UK passport holders need no visa for tourism. Stays are granted on arrival at the officer's discretion, commonly up to 90 days with a maximum of 183 in any twelve months, and the old paper Andean Migration Card is now electronic. Check the days you have been given, carry proof of onward travel and your first hotel booking, and confirm the current rules before you fly.

**The climate is the opposite of what you expect.** Lima sits in a coastal desert and gets almost no rain — a few millimetres a year — but from roughly **May to November** the **garúa**, a thick marine fog produced where the cold Humboldt current meets warmer air, parks itself over the city. That gives months of flat grey skies, high humidity, a damp drizzle that never becomes rain and daytime temperatures of **15 to 19°C**. It is not cold and it is not wet; it is simply grey, and it catches out travellers who packed for a South American summer. **December to April** is the sunny season, mid to high twenties, and it is when Lima is at its best. The complication is that Cusco and Machu Picchu have their dry season from roughly **May to September**, exactly when Lima is under cloud — so if you are doing both, **April to May** or **September to October** is the pragmatic compromise. Note also that Peruvian buildings rarely have heating, so pack a jumper for a grey July evening indoors.

**Altitude is a Cusco problem, not a Lima one.** Lima is at sea level and has no altitude effect whatsoever — you can arrive and go straight out for a run on the Malecón. **Cusco sits at around 3,400 metres**, which is high enough to lay a meaningful number of visitors out for a day or two, and Machu Picchu itself is lower at about 2,430 metres. Nights in Lima do nothing to acclimatise you, but they do mean you go up rested rather than straight off a long-haul flight. Take the first afternoon in Cusco slowly, drink water, skip the alcohol, and consider spending the first night lower down in the Sacred Valley instead.

**Machu Picchu is a separate trip, not a day out.** It is around 1,100km away: a flight of about an hour and twenty minutes to Cusco, a road transfer down to Ollantaytambo, a train to Aguas Calientes and a bus up to the gate, with timed entry tickets on fixed circuits that must be bought in advance and sell out in high season. Three days and two nights is the realistic minimum from Lima; four is better.

**Safety, honestly stated.** As of writing, the FCDO does not advise against travel to Lima — its all-but-essential advice covers the **VRAEM** emergency zone in the Apurímac, Ene and Mantaro river valleys and the Colombian border area of Loreto, both far from the capital. What it does flag for Lima is crime: street robbery, phones snatched by passengers on passing motorbikes, and unlicensed taxis. It also notes that Peru periodically declares **states of emergency** in parts of Lima and Callao as a policing measure. In practice the rules are simple and they work: use Uber, Cabify, DiDi or InDrive rather than hailing a taxi, keep your phone out of your hand on the street, keep your bag in your lap, use ATMs inside banks and malls by day, avoid La Victoria, Rímac and old Callao, and do not walk the Centro Histórico after dark. Read the current [UK government travel advice for Peru](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/peru) before you book and again before you fly; for planning the rest of the country, the official national tourism site [Peru.travel](https://www.peru.travel/) is the sensible starting point.

**Money, water and the everyday.** The currency is the **Peruvian sol (PEN)**, written S/. Spend soles rather than dollars, withdraw from ATMs inside bank branches or shopping centres, and always decline the machine's offer to bill you in pounds. Cards work fine in Miraflores, San Isidro and Barranco and much less reliably in markets, at street stalls and in the cheaper Centro hotels. **Do not drink the tap water** — bottled or filtered only, though brushing your teeth is fine. Tip around **10 per cent** in restaurants after checking whether a servicio charge is already on the bill; drivers are not tipped. Some Spanish goes a long way outside the tourist districts, and the phrase **menú del día** — a fixed-price set lunch for a very small sum — will save you more money over a fortnight than any other two words in the language.

## Lima Hotels FAQs

**Is Lima worth visiting, or is it just a stopover on the way to Machu Picchu?**

It is genuinely worth three or four days in its own right, and treating it as an airport with a city attached is the most common mistake British travellers make in Peru. Lima is a city of around ten million people, roughly a third of the entire country, spread along seventy kilometres of desert coastline with cliffs dropping straight into the Pacific. It holds the best museum of pre-Columbian art anywhere in the Museo Larco, a UNESCO-listed colonial centre built by Pizarro in 1535, adobe pyramids sitting between apartment blocks in the middle of residential districts, a ten-kilometre clifftop park you can walk end to end, and — the real reason to linger — what is arguably the best food scene in the Americas. If your entire Peru trip is a week, give Lima two nights on the way in and one on the way out. If you have ten days, give it three at the start and you will land in Cusco already understanding the country.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Peru?**

No. British passport holders do not need a visa for tourism in Peru. You are granted a stay on arrival at the immigration officer's discretion, commonly up to 90 days and with a maximum of 183 days in any twelve-month period, and the old paper Andean Migration Card has been replaced by an electronic record, so there is usually no form to keep hold of. Check the number of days you have actually been given before you leave the desk, because overstaying carries a daily fine payable on departure. Carry proof of onward or return travel and your first hotel booking, as you may be asked for both, and make sure your passport has plenty of validity left. Entry rules change, so confirm the current requirements on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Lima?**

No. There is currently no non-stop service between any UK airport and Lima, so every routing involves at least one connection. The cleanest options for a UK traveller are through Madrid with Iberia, Air Europa or LATAM, through Amsterdam with KLM, through Paris with Air France or through Frankfurt with Lufthansa — all of which mean one European hop and then a single long leg across the Atlantic with no immigration formalities until Peru. The alternatives run through the Americas: Miami, Dallas or Houston with the US carriers, Atlanta with Delta, or Bogotá with Avianca. The European routings are usually the least painful, and the Madrid connection is the one most people end up on.

**How long does it take to fly from the UK to Lima?**

Budget 15 to 17 hours door to door on a well-timed itinerary, and more if your layover is awkward. The London to Madrid leg is about two and a half hours, and Madrid to Lima is roughly twelve, which with a two-hour connection lands you at about seventeen hours from leaving home. Amsterdam and Paris are similar. Routings through Miami or Bogotá can be quicker in pure flying time but add a US immigration queue or a second short-haul leg. Coming home is longer, because you are flying east into the wind and against the clock. Peru runs on UTC minus five all year with no daylight saving, which puts Lima five hours behind the UK in winter and six hours behind during British Summer Time.

**Do I need an ESTA if I connect through Miami, Houston or Dallas?**

Yes, and it catches people out on every South America trip. The United States has no international transit-without-visa arrangement, so if your connection is at a US airport you clear US immigration and customs, collect your bags, re-check them and go back through security, even though you never leave the terminal complex. That requires a valid ESTA under the Visa Waiver Programme, or a full US visa if you are not ESTA-eligible. Apply on the official US government site well in advance and nowhere else, and give yourself a generous connection, because the queues at Miami in particular can be long. Route through Madrid, Amsterdam or Paris instead and none of this applies.

**What does the FCDO say about travelling to Peru?**

As of writing, the FCDO does not advise against travel to Lima. Its advice against all but essential travel is confined to specific regions well away from the capital — principally the VRAEM emergency zone in the Apurímac, Ene and Mantaro river valleys, and the area along the Colombian border in the Loreto region. For Lima itself the guidance is about crime rather than travel restrictions, and it flags street robbery, phone snatching and the risk of unlicensed taxis. It also notes that Peru periodically declares states of emergency in parts of Lima and Callao as a policing measure in response to crime or protests, which allows extra police and military deployment and can affect the right to assemble. None of that is a reason not to go, but it is a reason to read the current page rather than an old one. Advice is reviewed regularly, so check it before you book and again before you fly.

**Is Lima safe for tourists?**

Lima is a very large Latin American capital, and it behaves like one. The districts visitors actually use — Miraflores, Barranco, San Isidro, and the Centro Histórico by day — are policed, busy and used without incident by hundreds of thousands of residents every day. The realistic risk is opportunistic property crime rather than anything worse: phones snatched from hands by passengers on passing motorbikes, bags taken from the back of restaurant chairs, distraction thefts on crowded streets. The rules that deal with almost all of it are simple. Do not walk along a main road with your phone out. Use Uber, Cabify, DiDi or InDrive rather than flagging a taxi in the street. Keep your bag on your lap or between your feet. Use ATMs inside bank branches and shopping centres during the day rather than on the pavement. Do not wear an obviously expensive watch. Avoid La Victoria, Rímac and the older parts of Callao, and do not walk the Centro Histórico after dark — take a car back. Do that and Lima is straightforward.

**How do I get from Lima airport (LIM) into the city?**

Jorge Chávez International Airport sits in Callao, about 12km north-west of the Centro Histórico and 18 to 20km from Miraflores. Reckon on 40 to 60 minutes to Miraflores in normal conditions and up to 90 in Lima's genuinely serious rush-hour traffic. The airport moved into a large new terminal in 2025, so any directions or pickup instructions published before then may be out of date — check your airline's terminal information before you travel. Your options on arrival are the authorised taxi desks inside the terminal, where you pay a fixed fare up front, the Airport Express Lima bus which runs to Miraflores, a pre-booked hotel transfer, or a ride-hailing app collected from the designated pickup area. Do not accept a lift from anyone who approaches you inside the building, and do not walk out to the public road to meet a cheaper car.

**Which area of Lima should I stay in?**

Four districts cover almost every sensible trip. Miraflores is the default and the right answer for most first visits: clifftop parks, the Malecón walk, the biggest concentration of restaurants and hotels at every price, and the easiest place in the city to be a visitor. Barranco is the one next door with the character — bohemian, walkable, full of bars, street art and the best of the new restaurants, quieter by day and louder by night. San Isidro is the money district: the financial towers, the El Olivar olive grove, leafy streets, excellent restaurants and a calm, residential feel that suits older travellers and anyone who wants a garden rather than a party. And the Centro Histórico is where the colonial city actually is, where the cheapest central rooms are, and where you should be careful after dark. Pick Miraflores if you are unsure, Barranco if you want atmosphere, San Isidro if you want quiet, and the Centro if you want value and history.

**Miraflores or Barranco — which is better?**

They are three kilometres apart along the same clifftop and you can walk between them in under an hour, so this is less consequential than it sounds. Miraflores is bigger, busier, better supplied with hotels and shops, and safer-feeling at night, with Parque Kennedy, the Larcomar clifftop centre, the Malecón parks and the paragliding launch. It is where the international chains are and where a nervous first-timer should book. Barranco is smaller, prettier and considerably more interesting: nineteenth-century republican mansions in faded colours, the Puente de los Suspiros, murals down every side street, the MATE photography museum, and a bar and restaurant scene that pulls Limeños across the city at the weekend. The trade is noise and a thinner supply of rooms. If you are here for four nights, a very good answer is two in each.

**Is San Isidro a good place to stay in Lima?**

San Isidro is Lima's financial district and its most expensive residential one, and it is the quietest comfortable base in the city. The centrepiece is El Olivar, a park of several hundred olive trees planted in the sixteenth century and now surrounded by embassies and apartment blocks, and there is a restored adobe pyramid, Huaca Huallamarca, sitting in the middle of the housing. Streets are wide, green and calm, the restaurants are serious rather than showy, and the hotel stock leans towards business towers and boutique conversions. It suits couples who want to sleep, travellers here on business, and anyone who finds Miraflores a bit relentless. The trade-off is that it is not a going-out district — you will take a car to Barranco in the evening — and it is the one part of the city where budget rooms are genuinely scarce.

**Should I stay in the Centro Histórico?**

You can, and it is the cheapest central option, but go in with clear eyes. The Centro Histórico is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and it holds the things you came to see in the colonial city: the Plaza Mayor with the cathedral and the Government Palace, the yellow Baroque church and catacombs of San Francisco, the wooden Moorish balconies, Casa de Aliaga, the Convento de Santo Domingo. By day it is busy, fascinating and perfectly fine to walk. After dark it empties of visitors and the surrounding districts of La Victoria, Barrios Altos and Rímac are ones the FCDO and every Limeño will tell you to avoid, so evenings mean taking a car out to Miraflores or Barranco and taking one back. If your budget is tight and your priority is museums and churches, that is a reasonable trade — the room saving is real. If it is your first night in South America, book Miraflores instead.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Lima?**

The Centro Histórico and the districts immediately around it are the cheapest place to sleep in a useful location, and the airport corridor in Callao is cheaper still. In this guide, the very cheapest room is an airport property at around £18 a night, and the sub-£30 rooms are all either near the airport or in and around the old city. The next band up is more interesting: Lima's budget tier is unusually strong in Miraflores itself, where the three ibis properties, the Arawi express hotel and several of the Casa Andina standards all sit between roughly £30 and £39 a night in one of the best districts in the city. That is the single most useful pricing fact in this guide — in Lima you do not have to trade neighbourhood for price the way you do in most capitals.

**Is Lima at altitude — will I get altitude sickness?**

No. This is the single most useful thing to know before a Peru trip and a lot of people get it wrong. Lima sits on the Pacific coast, essentially at sea level — the clifftop parks in Miraflores are about seventy metres above the water and that is as high as the tourist city gets. There is no altitude effect in Lima whatsoever. You can eat a huge lunch, drink a pisco sour and go for a run on the Malecón on your first afternoon with no consequences at all. Altitude becomes a real issue the moment you fly inland: Cusco sits at around 3,400 metres and that is high enough to make a meaningful number of visitors feel genuinely unwell for a day or two. Spending your first nights in Lima is not acclimatisation — it does nothing for you at sea level — but it does mean you arrive in Cusco rested rather than straight off a fifteen-hour flight, which helps more than people expect.

**Is Machu Picchu a day trip from Lima?**

No, and anyone selling you one is selling you a very bad day. Machu Picchu is roughly 1,100km from Lima and reaching it is a chain of connections: a flight from Lima to Cusco of about an hour and twenty minutes, a road transfer of one and a half to two hours down into the Sacred Valley to Ollantaytambo, a train of about an hour and a half to Aguas Calientes, and then a shuttle bus up the switchbacks to the entrance. Entry is by timed ticket on a fixed circuit, bought in advance, and the popular slots sell out weeks ahead in high season. The realistic minimum from Lima is three days and two nights, and four days is far more comfortable because it lets you sleep at the lower altitude of the Sacred Valley before you go up. Plan it as its own trip within the trip, not as an excursion.

**How do I get from Lima to Cusco?**

Fly. LATAM, Sky Airline and JetSMART all run the Lima to Cusco route several times a day and the flight takes about an hour and twenty minutes, with fares that are frequently cheaper than a UK domestic train. Book morning departures wherever possible, because Cusco sits in a bowl in the Andes and afternoon weather causes far more delays and diversions than morning weather does. The overland alternative is a bus of 20 to 24 hours through the mountains, which the long-distance Peruvian coach companies do far more comfortably than the description suggests, but it is a serious commitment of holiday time. Whichever you choose, remember that you are going from sea level to 3,400 metres in one step — take the first afternoon in Cusco very gently, drink a lot of water and skip the alcohol for the first night.

**When is the best time to visit Lima?**

December to April, if you are choosing purely on Lima. That is the southern summer on the coast: blue skies, daytime highs in the mid to high twenties, warm evenings and a city that spends its weekends on the beaches south of town. May to November is the garúa season, when a persistent grey marine layer sits over the city, and it is atmospheric rather than pleasant. The complication is that these two seasons are the exact opposite of what you want in the Andes: the Cusco and Machu Picchu dry season runs roughly May to September, which is Lima's grey half. If you are doing both — and most people are — the compromise most travellers land on is April to May or September to October, when the coast still gets sun and the mountains are mostly dry.

**What is the garúa, and why is Lima grey for half the year?**

The garúa is a thick, low marine fog that sits over Lima from roughly May to November, produced by the cold Humboldt current running up the coast meeting warmer air above it. It gives the city month after month of flat grey skies, high humidity, a fine damp drizzle that never becomes proper rain, and daytime temperatures around 15 to 19°C. It is the defining fact of Lima's climate and the reason Herman Melville is said to have called it the saddest city in the world. What it is not is cold, and it is not wet either — Lima sits in one of the driest coastal deserts on earth and measurable rainfall is a few millimetres a year. The practical effect is on your photographs and your mood rather than your comfort, and the food, the museums and the restaurants are entirely unaffected.

**Do I need to pack for cold weather in Lima?**

Not cold, but bring layers, because Lima is chillier than a lot of people expect from a city fourteen degrees south of the equator. In the garúa months of May to November, days run around 15 to 19°C with heavy humidity and there is no sun to warm you up, so a light jacket or a fleece plus a jumper is genuinely useful, particularly on the clifftops where the wind comes off the Pacific. In the December to April summer you want shorts, sunglasses and strong sun cream, because the UV on a clear Lima day is fierce. In either season, note that Peruvian buildings very rarely have central heating, so a grey July evening indoors can feel colder than the thermometer suggests. And if you are continuing to Cusco, pack a proper warm layer regardless of the month — the Andes at 3,400 metres get close to freezing at night.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Lima?**

No — treat Lima tap water as not drinkable. It is chlorinated and technically treated, but the mineral content and the state of some older building plumbing mean visitors regularly come unstuck, and the standard advice from every Peruvian and every guidebook is to drink bottled or filtered water. Most hotels provide bottled water in the room and many have filtered dispensers on each floor, which is worth asking about, because it saves both money and plastic. Brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine. Be a little careful with ice and raw salads at the very cheapest street stalls, though established cevicherías and restaurants use treated water and ice as a matter of course.

**What should I eat in Lima?**

Lima is one of the great eating cities of the world and the reason is migration. Peruvian coastal cooking is what happened when Spanish, indigenous Andean, West African, Chinese, Japanese and Italian kitchens spent four centuries in the same port. The results have their own names: chifa is Chinese-Peruvian, and lomo saltado — beef stir-fried with onion, tomato, soy and chips — is its most famous export; nikkei is Japanese-Peruvian, which is where tiradito, the sashimi-shaped cousin of ceviche, comes from. Beyond that, eat ají de gallina, a mild creamy chilli chicken over rice; causa, layered whipped yellow potato with fillings; anticuchos, skewers of marinated beef heart grilled at street corners in the evening; and pollo a la brasa, the spit-roasted chicken that is Peru's true national dish by volume. And eat potatoes with everything, because Peru has thousands of varieties and takes them very seriously indeed.

**What is ceviche and where should I eat it in Lima?**

Ceviche is raw fish cured in the juice of Peruvian limes with sliced red onion, salt and hot ají limo chilli, served with sweet potato, large-kernel choclo corn and toasted cancha corn on the side, and it is the national dish. The cloudy citrus marinade left in the bowl is leche de tigre, which you drink, and which Limeños will tell you cures a hangover. The single most important rule is timing: ceviche is a lunch dish, the fish comes in that morning, and a proper cevichería closes in the early afternoon rather than serving it at nine at night. Anywhere offering ceviche at dinner is either very good and buying twice a day, or not worth your money. Miraflores and Barranco have the well-known dining rooms, the market cevicherías in Surquillo do the same thing for a fraction of the price, and the fishing port of Chorrillos does it with the boats in view.

**Can I get a table at Central or Maido?**

Only if you plan a long way ahead. Central, Virgilio Martínez and Pía León's restaurant in Barranco, and Maido, Mitsuharu Tsumura's nikkei restaurant in Miraflores, are the two names that have made Lima a global food destination — Central was named the world's best restaurant in 2023 and Maido took the same title in 2025. Both release tables online, typically a couple of months in advance, and both are effectively sold out within hours of opening a new period. If that is the trip you want, book the restaurant before you book the flight. If you miss out, Lima is unusually rich in the tier below: Kjolle in the same Barranco building as Central, Astrid y Gastón in a colonial hacienda in San Isidro, Rafael and La Mar in Miraflores and Isolina in Barranco are all serious kitchens, and a market lunch in Surquillo for a few pounds is closer to the soul of the city than any tasting menu.

**What is a pisco sour, and what else should I drink in Lima?**

The pisco sour is Peru's national cocktail: pisco, the clear unaged grape brandy distilled on the south coast, shaken with fresh lime juice, sugar syrup and egg white and finished with a few drops of Angostura bitters over the foam. Peru and Chile argue about who invented pisco and you should not take sides while holding a Peruvian glass. Beyond that, try chilcano, which is pisco with ginger ale and lime and is the everyday drink rather than the special-occasion one; chicha morada, a non-alcoholic purple corn drink with pineapple and cinnamon that comes with almost every set lunch; and Inca Kola, the fluorescent yellow bubblegum-flavoured soft drink that outsells Coca-Cola in Peru and that you should try exactly once. Peruvian coffee is excellent and increasingly well brewed in Barranco and Miraflores.

**Are Uber and taxi apps available in Lima?**

Yes, and you should use them. Uber, Cabify, DiDi and InDrive all operate across Lima and between them they cover every district a visitor will use, with fares that are extremely low by UK standards — a cross-city trip from Miraflores to the Centro Histórico typically costs a few pounds. Booking in an app removes the two problems with Lima's street taxis: they are unmetered, so every ride begins with a negotiation you will lose, and they are not all licensed, which is the specific risk the FCDO flags. If you do take a street taxi, use one your hotel calls for you. Set the apps up with a working payment method before you fly, because signing up on Peruvian mobile data at midnight in an airport arrivals hall is nobody's idea of a good start.

**Do I need a hire car in Lima?**

No, and for most visitors it would be an actively bad decision. Lima's traffic is heavy, fast and governed more by confidence than by lane markings, parking in Miraflores and San Isidro is difficult and expensive, and ride-hailing is so cheap that a full day of taxis costs less than a day's car hire before you have paid for fuel. The city's own transport is also better than its reputation: the Metropolitano bus rapid transit runs in a dedicated corridor down the Vía Expresa between the Centro and Barranco, and Metro Line 1 serves the eastern and southern districts. Hire a car only if you are driving out along the Panamericana under your own steam — south to Pachacamac, Paracas and Huacachina, or north to the Caral pyramids — and even then, a bus or an organised trip is usually simpler.

**What currency does Peru use — should I take US dollars?**

The currency is the Peruvian sol, written S/ and abbreviated PEN, and it is what you should be spending. US dollars are accepted in some hotels, tour offices and upmarket restaurants, but at a rate set for the convenience of whoever is taking them, and change will come back in soles anyway. Take a small amount of cash for the first day, then withdraw soles from ATMs inside bank branches or shopping centres rather than from machines on the pavement, and always decline the machine's offer to charge you in pounds — that is dynamic currency conversion and it costs you every time. Cards are widely accepted in Miraflores, San Isidro and Barranco and much less so in markets, at street food stalls, in the smaller Centro hotels and for short taxi rides, so carry some notes and keep small ones.

**How much should I tip in Lima?**

Around 10 per cent in restaurants, and check the bill first, because many mid-range and upmarket places already add a servicio charge of about 10 per cent, in which case anything more is a bonus rather than an obligation. Tipping is not the deeply embedded expectation it is in the United States: taxi and app drivers are not tipped, bar staff are not tipped per drink, and nobody will chase you down the street. Small tips are normal and appreciated for hotel porters and housekeeping, for guides on a day tour, and for the men who help you park. Tip in soles rather than in dollars or pounds, because changing small foreign notes in Peru is a nuisance for the person you are trying to be kind to.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Lima?**

You will get by without it, but you will have a better trip with a little. English is common in the international hotels, in the Miraflores and San Isidro restaurants, at the museums and among younger staff generally. It is much less common in taxis, in markets, at the cheaper cevicherías, in the smaller budget hotels in this guide and in the Centro Histórico. Peruvian Spanish is clear and relatively slow compared with, say, Chilean, which makes it a friendly place to use a phrasebook. Download an offline translation pack before you go, learn the numbers and the food words, and be aware that a menú del día is a fixed-price set lunch — usually a starter, a main and a drink for a very small sum — and knowing that phrase alone will save you a fortune over a fortnight.

**Is Lima good for families?**

Better than most people assume, though it is a city break rather than a holiday. The Malecón clifftop parks run for around ten kilometres with playgrounds, cycle hire and skate parks, and children love watching the paragliders launch straight off the cliff at Miraflores. The Circuito Mágico del Agua in the Parque de la Reserva is a genuine hit — thirteen illuminated, walk-through fountains with an evening light and music show. The catacombs beneath the San Francisco monastery are the sort of thing that makes a trip memorable for a nine-year-old, the Parque de las Leyendas zoo sits inside an archaeological site, and the beaches south of the city at Punta Hermosa are an easy day out in summer. The constraints are traffic, the amount of car time between districts, and the grey garúa months when the coast is not much fun for small children. Family rooms and apart-hotels with kitchens are widely available and cheap by UK standards.

**How many days do you need in Lima?**

Three full days is right, and two is the minimum that does not feel like a waste. Day one for the Centro Histórico — the Plaza Mayor, the cathedral, the San Francisco catacombs, the balconies and a market lunch — then back to Miraflores for the evening. Day two for the museums and the coast: the Museo Larco in Pueblo Libre in the morning, the Huaca Pucllana pyramid in Miraflores, then the Malecón walk from Larcomar down to Barranco in the late afternoon, ending at the Puente de los Suspiros. Day three for eating properly, a cevichería lunch, the MATE museum and the Barranco street art, or a half-day out to the Pachacamac ruins south of the city. A fourth day buys you the beaches or a long lunch you do not have to rush. If Lima is bookending a Machu Picchu trip, take two nights on the way in and one on the way out.

**Do Lima hotels include breakfast, and do they do airport transfers?**

Breakfast is included more often than not, particularly in the three and four-star tier, and Peruvian hotel breakfasts are generally a proper buffet rather than a pastry and a coffee. Always confirm it on the room page before booking rather than assuming, because the very cheapest rooms often price it separately. Airport transfers are a different matter: most Lima hotels can arrange one but very few include it, and the cost of a hotel car is usually two or three times an app fare. The exceptions are the airport hotels themselves, several of which run a free shuttle, which is the entire reason to book them. If you land late at night on a long-haul connection, a pre-arranged transfer is worth the premium once, for the simple pleasure of someone holding a sign with your name on it.
