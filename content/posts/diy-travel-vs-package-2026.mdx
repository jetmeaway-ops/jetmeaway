  - q: "What exactly does ATOL protect against?"
    a: "ATOL (Air Travel Organisers' Licensing) is a CAA-run financial safety net that applies only when you buy a flight bundled with at least one other travel element — a hotel, car hire or transfer — from a single provider as a package. If that provider goes bust before or during your trip, ATOL either refunds you or, if you're already abroad, repatriates you at no extra cost. It does not cover flight delays, cancellations for weather, missed connections, or any other everyday travel disruption — those are handled through UK261 passenger-rights law or your own travel insurance, regardless of whether you booked a package or DIY."
  - q: "Does ABTA offer the same protection as ATOL?"
    a: "No — they cover different things and often overlap on the same package. ABTA (Association of British Travel Agents) is a trade body whose members agree to a code of conduct and a dispute-resolution scheme; if an ABTA member holiday firm collapses, ABTA can arrange refunds or repatriation similarly to ATOL, but its scope is broader and includes holidays that don't involve a flight at all — coach holidays, cruises, package trips within the UK. Many operators (TUI, Jet2 Holidays, easyJet Holidays) are both ATOL-licensed and ABTA members, so you get both layers on the same booking. Neither exists at all once you book flights and hotels separately."
  - q: "If I book a flight directly with an airline, am I protected if it goes bust?"
    a: "Not by ATOL — scheduled airlines selling their own seats are exempt from the ATOL scheme entirely, because you hold a direct e-ticket contract with the airline itself. If the airline collapses, you're not automatically refunded by a CAA-backed scheme the way a package customer would be. Your recourse is Section 75 (if you paid over £100 on a UK credit card, the card issuer is jointly liable) or Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI) bought as part of your travel insurance — which is exactly why both matter so much more once you go DIY."
  - q: "What is Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI) and do I actually need it?"
    a: "SAFI is a travel insurance add-on that refunds you if the specific airline or accommodation provider you booked with financially collapses before or during your trip — filling the exact gap ATOL leaves once you book independently. Defaqto's review of the UK insurance market found 88% of annual multi-trip policies do not include SAFI as standard, so you typically have to add it deliberately or choose a provider that bundles it. For a DIY trip of any real value, SAFI is one of the cheapest, highest-leverage additions you can make — often a few pounds on top of a standard policy."
  - q: "What is Section 75 and how does it protect a DIY booking?"
    a: "Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes your UK credit card issuer jointly and severally liable with the retailer for any purchase between £100 and £30,000 paid on that card — including flights, hotels and car hire booked directly. If the airline or hotel goes bust, or the service is never delivered, you can claim the refund from your card issuer instead of chasing the (possibly defunct) company. It doesn't apply to debit cards, and doesn't cover ordinary service complaints (a noisy room, a delayed flight) — only non-delivery or the provider ceasing to exist. Pay the largest single element of your trip on a credit card specifically to trigger this cover."
  - q: "What's the actual difference in flexibility between DIY and a package?"

DIY lets you choose any UK departure airport, any hotel on the open market, any trip length down to a single night, and any combination of cities or regions in one trip. The trade-off is that a package bundles the logistics for you in one transaction, while DIY means making — and living with — every one of those choices yourself."

## 1. The "hidden surcharge" you can't price-check

Tour operators bundle flights, hotel, transfers and luggage into one headline figure, then quietly mark up the components you can't see. **TUI's hotel division alone added £91m in underlying earnings year-on-year**, while Jet2 publicly tells investors that package customers are "higher-margin per passenger" than flight-only travellers — a roughly 6% operating margin against £7.17bn FY2025 revenue. That margin doesn't appear from thin air; it's the price of not being able to compare each element.

Think about what actually sits inside a package price. The operator has pre-negotiated a bulk hotel allotment months or years in advance, chartered or block-booked aircraft seats at a wholesale rate, and bundled in transfers and luggage as a single line the customer can't unpick. Every one of those components has its own margin baked in, and none of them is visible to you as the buyer — you see one number, not four. That opacity is the entire commercial logic of the package model: it's far harder to negotiate down, or shop around, a bundle than it is to price-check a flight against a flight and a hotel against a hotel.

Booking separately means you can. A £45 Ryanair return + a £90-a-night central [hotel](/hotels) in Barcelona, Lisbon or Berlin will routinely undercut the equivalent operator package by **£100–£250 per person**. We did the full breakdown — including when packages still genuinely win — in our [package vs separate guide](/blog/book-flight-hotel-separately-vs-package).

Average European package prices climbed from £1,038 per person in summer 2024 to £1,157 in summer 2025 — an 11% jump in a single year, well ahead of general UK inflation over the same period. Italy rose 7.4%, Portugal 6.8%, Cyprus 6.4%. Hotel-cost inflation is the primary driver: as wholesale room rates rise, operators pass the increase through to package prices faster than independent hotels pass it through to their own public rates, because the operator's negotiated allotment locks in a cost base that then gets marked up on top. A traveller pricing the same trip independently is exposed to the hotel's actual public rate movement, not the operator's compounded version of it.

None of this makes the package a bad product by design — it makes it a product optimised for convenience and bulk purchasing power, not for price transparency. The two aren't the same thing, and 2026's more price-literate UK traveller increasingly notices the difference.

## 2. Slow travel and hobby-led trips just don't fit packages

The biggest holiday trend of 2026 isn't a destination — it's a *pace*. Travellers spending a week in one neighbourhood, picking up a hobby, learning a craft, or building a sabbatical around a yoga school or a hike. Packages can't accommodate that shape: they're built for 7-night beach blocks and 4-night city sweeps, not for "I want three days surfing in Tarifa and then four days slow-cooking in Cádiz."

This isn't a niche preference any more. Search interest and booking patterns across UK travel comparison sites have shifted visibly toward longer, single-location stays and toward itineraries with a genuine activity anchor — a language course, a cycling route, a wellness retreat — rather than a checklist of sights. The package industry's entire operating model, built on charter flight schedules and negotiated hotel blocks with fixed check-in and check-out days, simply cannot flex to match that. A tour operator's charter aircraft flies out on a Saturday and back the following Saturday because that's the rotation that makes the aircraft economics work — not because it's the ideal trip length for the traveller.

DIY lets you anchor on the activity, then build the logistics around it. Want to start in [Lisbon](/destinations/lisbon), train down to Lagos, then fly back from [Porto](/destinations/porto)? Trivial to book. Try asking a tour operator for that itinerary — most won't quote it, and the few that do will charge a multi-stop premium that wipes out the savings.

The same logic applies to sabbatical-style trips that are increasingly common among UK travellers taking career breaks or extended remote-work stints: three weeks in one place, working mornings and exploring afternoons, doesn't map onto any package product at all. A package operator sells nights in a hotel room at a rate calculated against average occupancy and turnover — a guest staying 21 nights straight, using minimal housekeeping and no daily transfers, breaks that model rather than fitting neatly into it. Independent long-stay hotel rates, serviced apartments and even monthly Airbnb-style rentals are built for exactly this shape of trip, and a DIY search across [hotels](/hotels) and accommodation platforms will surface options a package brochure never will.

## 3. Total flexibility on flights, hotels and dates

Packages lock you in. The rigid Saturday-to-Saturday slot, the pre-allocated hotel, the single airport. Want a Thursday-to-Monday city break? Tough. Want to switch from a beachfront hotel on day three to a riad in the old town for the second half? The package doesn't bend.

Booking separately means you mix and match like a traveller, not a customer being processed. Compare [flights](/flights) from your nearest UK airport across whole-month windows. Pick a [hotel](/hotels) in the actual neighbourhood you want to wake up in. Switch one element without losing the lot — change a refundable hotel without forfeiting your flight, or rebook a flight if a fare drops without abandoning your accommodation.

Departure airport choice is a flexibility lever that's easy to overlook. A package operator typically sells a specific route from a specific UK airport — Manchester to Malaga on a Tuesday, say — and if that departure doesn't suit you, your only options are a different (often pricier) departure date on the same route, or a different package entirely. DIY lets you compare Manchester against Liverpool, Leeds Bradford or even a longer drive to a London airport if the fare difference justifies it, something no single package listing will ever surface for you.

Amendment fees are where this really bites on the package side: most operators charge £30–75 per person to shift dates after booking, on top of any fare difference, and some discounted fare classes can't be changed at all. A flexible-rate DIY hotel, by contrast, is often cancellable free up to 24–48 hours before arrival — a level of control a package rarely offers at any price. This matters more than it sounds: UK weather, work schedules and school-term dates all shift plans more often than travellers expect, and the cost of that shift compounds quickly on a rigid package contract.

Flexibility also extends to room category and length of stay within a single hotel. A package typically locks you into whatever room type the operator negotiated in bulk — often a standard double, with sea-view or suite upgrades sold at a premium that's rarely as competitive as booking that upgraded category directly. DIY lets you compare the hotel's own website, a comparison engine and sometimes a loyalty-programme rate side by side for the exact room category you actually want, rather than accepting the operator's default allocation.

## 4. ATOL and ABTA explained: what actually protects you

This is the part of the DIY-vs-package decision most UK travellers get wrong, so it's worth being precise about what each scheme actually does.

**ATOL (Air Travel Organisers' Licensing)**, run by the Civil Aviation Authority under its [official ATOL protection scheme](https://www.caa.co.uk/atol-protection/), is a financial safety net that applies *only* when you buy a flight bundled with at least one other element — hotel, car hire, transfer — from a single provider in one transaction. If that provider collapses, ATOL refunds you if you haven't travelled, or repatriates you if you're already abroad. Thomas Cook's 2019 collapse, which saw around 150,000 UK holidaymakers repatriated under the scheme, remains the reference case for how well it works at scale.

**ABTA (Association of British Travel Agents)** is a separate trade body whose members follow a code of conduct and dispute-resolution scheme. Its scope is broader than ATOL's — it covers holidays that don't involve a flight at all, like coach trips and UK package holidays — and many operators (TUI, Jet2 Holidays, easyJet Holidays) carry both ATOL and ABTA cover on the same booking, giving two overlapping layers of protection.

**What neither scheme covers:** ordinary flight delays, cancellations for weather, missed connections, or a hotel that simply isn't as good as the photos. Those sit with UK261 passenger-rights law (for flight disruption) and your travel insurance (for everything else) — regardless of whether you booked a package or DIY.

Here's the comparison in one table, because the two schemes get conflated constantly:

| | ATOL | ABTA |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Run by | UK Civil Aviation Authority | Industry trade body |
| Covers | Flight-inclusive packages only | Broader — includes non-flight package holidays (coach, cruise, UK trips) |
| Triggers on | Provider financial collapse | Provider financial collapse or code-of-conduct dispute |
| Funded by | ATOL Protection Contribution (£2.50pp) via the Air Travel Trust Fund | Member subscriptions |
| Applies to standalone flights or hotels | No | No |
| Applies to flight-plus / dynamic packages | Yes, via the bundling provider | Sometimes, if the provider is also an ABTA member |
| Verify before booking | CAA website ATOL checker | ABTA member checker |

**The critical gap:** book a flight directly with an airline, or a hotel directly with a property, and neither ATOL nor ABTA applies at all. Scheduled airlines selling their own seats are exempt from ATOL entirely — you hold a direct e-ticket contract with the airline, and if it cancels or delays you, UK261 obliges it to refund, rebook or compensate you directly. But if the airline or hotel goes *bust*, there's no CAA-backed refund waiting — which is exactly why the safety net in the next section matters. We go deeper on this specific point, with worked examples, in [the DIY travel ATOL myth](/blog/atol-direct-booking-myth-2026).

A middle path exists too: book a **flight-plus** (or "dynamic package") — your own flight and hotel choice, bundled into a single transaction through one ATOL-licensed provider — and you get CAA protection while keeping real control over the hotel and dates. JetMeAway's Expedia (ATOL 5788) and Trip.com (ATOL 11572) partners both offer this hybrid.

**How the CAA actually pays out.** When an ATOL-licensed operator collapses, the Air Travel Trust Fund — built from a small ATOL Protection Contribution (currently £2.50 per person) added to every protected booking — funds the refunds and repatriation flights. If you're already abroad when the collapse happens, the CAA typically charters replacement flights and coordinates directly with the affected hotels to keep guests housed until they can be flown home, at no extra cost to you beyond the inconvenience. If you haven't yet travelled, you get a full refund of everything you've paid, including any deposit. This is a genuinely different experience to chasing a Section 75 claim through your own bank, which can take weeks and requires you to assemble the evidence yourself.

**Check before you book, not after.** Every ATOL-protected package or flight-plus booking must come with an ATOL certificate showing a licence number — it's a legal requirement, and its absence on your confirmation is the clearest possible sign you're not protected. You can verify any ATOL number directly on the CAA's own website before paying a deposit, which takes under a minute and removes any ambiguity about what you're actually buying.

## 5. Building your own safety net

DIY's one real downside is protection. When you book separately, ATOL doesn't cover you. So you build your own three-layer net:

- **Pay by credit card** for any element over £100. Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act makes your card issuer jointly liable if the airline or hotel fails — it doesn't cover ordinary complaints, only non-delivery or the provider ceasing to exist, and it doesn't apply to debit cards.
- **Buy [travel insurance](/insurance) the day you book**, not the day you fly. Look for £2m+ medical cover for Europe (£5m+ for worldwide, including the USA), cancellation matching your trip cost, and crucially **Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI)** — Defaqto found 88% of annual policies don't include SAFI as standard, so check for it specifically or add it as an extra.
- **Use an itinerary app** like TripIt or Wanderlog to consolidate every confirmation into one place. Forwarding emails to plans@tripit.com auto-builds the trip, so every booking reference is findable instantly if a provider's own system goes down.

A GHIC card gets you state healthcare across the EU but won't fly you home or refund a missed connection. Insurance still does the heavy lifting.

**A fourth layer worth adding for higher-value trips: the flight-plus hybrid.** If the idea of self-insuring against a provider collapse still feels uncomfortable — a genuinely reasonable reaction for an expensive long-haul trip or a big family booking — bundle your flight and hotel through one ATOL-licensed provider instead of building the net entirely yourself. You keep the freedom to choose your own hotel and dates, and you trade a small amount of DIY-style price optimisation for the CAA's financial backstop. It's not an all-or-nothing choice between full DIY and full package; the flight-plus route exists precisely for travellers who want most of the DIY upside without carrying all of the DIY risk.

**Timing matters more than most travellers realise.** Buy your travel insurance on the same day you make your first non-refundable booking, not the week before departure. Most policies' cancellation cover only applies to events that happen *after* the policy start date — if you book a non-refundable flight in March for a September trip and don't buy insurance until August, you're uninsured for any cancellation-triggering event (illness, bereavement, redundancy) that occurs in those five months. SAFI cover works the same way: it only protects bookings made after the policy is live, so a policy bought after the airline or hotel has already shown signs of financial trouble typically won't pay out on that specific booking.

## 6. Baggage rules: the cost nobody adds up

This is where a lot of "DIY was cheaper" maths quietly falls apart if you're not careful. Package holidays typically bundle a 20–23kg hold bag per person as standard — genuinely one of the easier savings to underestimate, especially for a family checking multiple bags.

Budget airline hand-luggage-only fares, by contrast, usually include just one small under-seat bag (roughly 40 × 20 × 25cm) and nothing else. Add a hold bag online during booking and it typically runs **£30–60 return**; leave it until the airport and that fee can double or triple — a Which? investigation found actual gate fees frequently exceeded the airline's advertised figure by a wide margin. If you know you'll need a hold bag, add it the moment you book, not the day you fly.

The rule of thumb: when comparing a DIY quote against a package price, always add back the realistic cost of the baggage, seat selection and transfers the package would have bundled in before declaring DIY the winner. It usually still wins outside the traditional beach-holiday template — but the margin is smaller once everything's accounted for honestly.

**Airport transfers are the second most commonly forgotten line item.** A package holiday almost always includes a coach or private transfer between the airport and the resort as standard — for a family of four heading somewhere without a convenient rail link, that's often £60–150 of value that a DIY quote needs to explicitly add back in the form of a pre-booked private transfer, a taxi, or a hire car. Some destinations (most of mainland Spain's Costa del Sol, for instance) have decent public transport alternatives that narrow the gap; others (parts of the Algarve, most Greek islands) effectively require a car or transfer either way, making this a genuine, unavoidable DIY cost rather than an optional extra.

**Seat selection and priority boarding follow the same pattern as baggage.** Budget airlines increasingly charge separately for allocated seating — routinely £5–15 per person per flight if you want to sit next to your travel companion rather than being randomly allocated at check-in. Families travelling with young children should budget for this specifically, since airlines don't guarantee adjacent seating on the cheapest fare tier even for accompanied minors, and the cost of getting it wrong (a stressful boarding experience, a request to swap seats with a stranger) is worth the £10–20 it typically costs to fix in advance.

## 7. Rep support vs going it alone

A package holiday typically comes with a resort rep or a 24-hour operator helpline — someone who can rebook a cancelled excursion, liaise with the hotel directly on your behalf, or arrange emergency transport. It's a genuine service layer beyond the ATOL financial protection, and it's the part of a package that's hardest to replace with a clever hack.

DIY travellers lean on three substitutes instead: the individual airline or hotel's own customer service, the 24-hour emergency assistance line nearly all travel insurance policies include, and increasingly, app-based support from providers like Expedia or Trip.com. It works, but it's more fragmented — you're managing three or four separate relationships instead of one. For travellers who specifically value having a single phone number to call when something goes wrong (a genuinely common preference for solo older travellers, or families with young children), this is one of the strongest, most underrated arguments for a package.

**Where a rep genuinely earns their keep:** a cancelled excursion mid-trip, a hotel that's materially different from what was booked (wrong room category, building work not disclosed at booking), or a family needing help navigating a local hospital visit. In each of these scenarios, a rep who already has a relationship with the hotel and the local ground operator can resolve things faster than a DIY traveller working a call centre queue from a foreign mobile number. This is genuinely valuable and shouldn't be dismissed as marketing fluff — it's one of the few parts of a package that's hard to replicate at any price once you're already at the destination.

**Where DIY support actually holds up fine:** flight disruption (covered by UK261 regardless of booking method, and airlines' own apps have become considerably better at rebooking and compensation claims since 2020), routine hotel issues (a call to reception or the booking platform's support line resolves most of these), and anything insurance-related, where a good travel insurer's 24-hour line is functionally equivalent to an operator's emergency service. The gap between DIY and package support has narrowed noticeably over the past few years as airline and hotel-platform apps have improved — it hasn't closed, but it's smaller than it was even three or four years ago.

## 9. The planning-effort spectrum

DIY and package aren't the only two options — most real UK bookings sit somewhere on a spectrum between them:

1. **Full package.** One operator, one price, one rep, ATOL and usually ABTA protected. Minimum effort — 20–30 minutes to browse and pay. Best for traditional beach holidays and large family groups.
2. **Flight-plus / dynamic package.** Your own flight and hotel choice, bundled into a single ATOL-protected transaction through one provider. Moderate effort, CAA protection retained. A genuine middle path if you want real control without building your own safety net from scratch.
3. **Full DIY, single destination.** Flight and hotel booked separately, same city. Realistically 3–6 hours of research across a few sessions: comparing flights, shortlisting hotels, checking transfers, buying insurance. No ATOL — you build the Section 75 + SAFI net yourself.
4. **Full DIY, multi-stop.** Multiple cities, multiple bookings, your own connections. Can run to a full day of planning the first time, though the process gets faster on repeat trips. Maximum flexibility, maximum personal responsibility for the safety net.

Most experienced travellers don't pick one lane for life — they mix tiers by trip. A flight-plus package for the anchor beach leg of a longer trip, DIY city-break nights either side of it, is an increasingly common pattern precisely because it protects the highest-value, least-flexible part of the trip while keeping flexibility everywhere else.

It's worth being honest about where each tier's effort actually goes, because "effort" isn't just time spent clicking — it's also the cognitive load of holding multiple bookings, dates and confirmation numbers in your head at once. A full package compresses that load to almost zero: one confirmation email, one itinerary, one point of contact. Full DIY, especially multi-stop, means actively managing several independent relationships simultaneously, each with its own cancellation policy, its own confirmation format and its own customer service channel. Neither is objectively "better" — they're different trades between control and cognitive overhead, and the right choice depends on how much of that overhead you're willing to carry in exchange for the flexibility and savings.

One underused strategy for reducing that overhead without giving up flexibility: batch your bookings by provider where it doesn't cost you the savings. Booking your outbound and return flights, or your hotel and a car hire, through the same comparison search at the same time means fewer separate emails to track and a single receipt to reference if a dispute arises — while still giving you the freedom to pick the cheapest individual flight, hotel and car hire rather than being locked into one operator's bundle.

## 10. When to DIY

DIY wins clearly when your trip has any of these shapes:

- **City breaks and long weekends** — Thursday-to-Monday, Friday-to-Tuesday, any pattern that isn't a Saturday-to-Saturday week. Almost no mainstream UK operator sells a genuine 3 or 4-night city-break package at a competitive rate, so DIY isn't just cheaper here, it's frequently the only realistic option.
- **Shoulder-season trips** — April, May, late September, October, where budget airlines and independent hotels compete hardest on price, and package operators have thinner charter schedules that reduce their pricing flexibility.
- **Multi-city or multi-country itineraries** — start in one city, move overland, fly home from somewhere else entirely. Open-jaw flight bookings (flying into one city, out of another) are simple to build DIY and essentially unavailable as a single package product.
- **Hobby-led or slow-travel trips** — anchored on a cooking class, a hike, a festival, a sabbatical, rather than a beach. These trips don't fit the 7/10/14-night template a package is built around.
- **Solo travellers and couples comfortable managing their own bookings** and building a Section 75 + SAFI safety net. The admin overhead of DIY falls almost entirely on whoever is willing to own it, and it's genuinely lower for one or two travellers than for a group of six.
- **Long-stay or extended trips** — 3 weeks or more in one location, where long-stay accommodation rates (often 20–40% below the equivalent nightly rate for a standard week-long booking) simply have no package equivalent.
- **Anyone willing to spend 3–6 hours comparing options** in exchange for £100–£250 per person in typical savings — treat that time as an hourly rate and, for most UK earners, it comfortably beats their day job.

## 11. When to book a package

A package earns its premium when your trip has any of these shapes:

- **Traditional 7, 10 or 14-night all-inclusive beach holidays** to Spain, Greece, Turkey, the Canaries or similar — Which? found packages cheaper on 8 out of 10 of these trips, and the all-inclusive meal plan specifically is one of the hardest things to replicate at equivalent value through DIY booking.
- **Large family or multi-generational groups**, where block-booked adjoining rooms, included kids' clubs and one rep coordinating logistics for six or eight people genuinely save admin, not just money. The larger the group, the more the coordination overhead of DIY grows non-linearly.
- **Travellers who want a single phone number** to call if something goes wrong, rather than managing airline, hotel and insurer separately — particularly valuable for less experienced travellers, older travellers, or anyone travelling with young children where in-resort disruption is more likely and harder to absorb solo.
- **Anyone who values CAA-backed ATOL protection** more than the last pound of savings — particularly relevant after high-profile operator collapses like Thomas Cook in 2019, and worth weighing seriously for any trip where the total spend is a meaningful chunk of a household's annual travel budget.
- **Trips where you genuinely don't want to spend hours comparing** — the 20–30 minutes it takes to browse and pay is the entire point, and that convenience has a real, legitimate value even if it's hard to put a precise number on it.
- **First-time visitors to an unfamiliar destination**, where a package's included transfers, on-site rep and pre-vetted hotel remove a layer of research and risk that a confident DIY traveller might not mind but a first-timer often prefers to avoid.

**A regional nuance worth flagging:** the traditional-package advantage is strongest specifically on well-trodden Mediterranean and Canary Islands beach routes, where operators have decades of negotiated hotel relationships and charter capacity. It's noticeably weaker on long-haul, city-break-heavy or emerging destinations, where operators have thinner route networks and DIY search tools have more genuinely competitive inventory to surface. Always weigh the destination itself, not just the trip length, when deciding which side of this list your trip falls on.

## 14. Five mistakes that quietly erase your DIY savings

**1. Comparing headline prices only.** A £75 flight and a £300 hotel look like they beat a £420 package — until you add £60 in baggage, £40 in transfers and £25 in insurance, at which point the two are within a few pounds of each other. Always build the full DIY total before declaring it the winner.

**2. Delaying travel insurance.** Buying insurance a week before departure instead of the day you book leaves you uninsured for cancellation-triggering events in the gap, and can void SAFI cover on bookings made before the policy started. This is the single most common — and most easily avoided — DIY mistake.

**3. Paying on debit instead of credit for anything over £100.** Section 75 only applies to credit cards. A debit card purchase of the same size gets none of that protection if the provider collapses, leaving you entirely reliant on insurance (and specifically SAFI, if you have it).

**4. Assuming a "protected" badge means ATOL.** Some booking platforms display reassuring-sounding trust badges that aren't ATOL certificates. Always look for the specific ATOL number, and verify it on the CAA's site if the booking is large enough to matter.

**5. Under-budgeting time for multi-stop itineraries.** A three-city trip with connecting trains or flights takes meaningfully longer to plan than a single-destination trip — often a full afternoon rather than an hour — because you're matching arrival and departure times across multiple independent bookings. Block out that time deliberately rather than trying to squeeze complex planning into a lunch break, or the itinerary ends up compromised by whichever option was fastest to book rather than best value.
