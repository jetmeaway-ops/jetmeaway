ABTA (the Association of British Travel Agents) is a trade body whose members must meet a code of conduct and offer a separate financial protection scheme for non-flight package holidays (like a coach tour or a hotel-only package with no flight). ## 1. The hidden margins behind those tempting package prices

Tour operators don't itemise. They quote you one figure for flights, hotel, transfers and luggage, then quietly mark up the bits you can't see. **TUI's combined airline-and-markets margin sat at just 1.5% in 2024, with a target of "more than 3%"** — but that's the headline. The real money is upstairs in TUI's hotel division, where bulk-bought beds at TUI Blue and Marella properties produced underlying earnings that jumped £91m year-on-year. Jet2 is even more upfront about it, telling investors that package holiday customers are "higher-margin per passenger" than flight-only travellers, and posting a **£593m pre-tax profit on £7.17bn revenue in FY2025** — an implied operating margin around 6%.

That margin doesn't appear from thin air. It comes from charter aircraft seats, bulk hotel allotments, and the simple fact that you can't price-check the components. Operators throw in 22kg luggage and transfers as standard precisely because most DIY comparisons forget to add them. Once you control for the bundle, the maths gets very different — particularly outside traditional summer beach trips.

**The four big UK operators price the bundle differently, too.** TUI leans hardest on owned inventory — TUI Blue and Marella hotels — which means it captures margin on both sides of the transaction (the flight and the room) rather than marking up a wholesale rate it doesn't own. Jet2holidays and easyJet Holidays are closer to a flight-plus-third-party-hotel model: they run (or charter) the aircraft themselves but mark up hotel rates sourced from wholesalers, similar to how a DIY booker would buy a hotel room, just with a bulk-buying advantage. BA Holidays sits somewhere in between — a dynamic packaging layer built on top of scheduled British Airways flights and a wide roster of partner hotels, priced per combination rather than off owned inventory. None of the four publish the per-trip margin, and none are required to — that's the entire reason this comparison exercise is worth doing before every booking, not just once.

**Why the margin is invisible by design.** A single package price is, structurally, a black box: you cannot see what portion of your £650 is the flight, what portion is the room, and what portion is the operator's cut, because the operator has no obligation to show you. This isn't unique to travel — it's the same reason a set restaurant menu is often priced differently to ordering the same dishes à la carte — but travel is one of the few categories where the components are individually, easily price-checkable by any consumer with ten minutes and a search engine. That asymmetry is exactly what this guide exists to close.

**A word on "free" child places and other headline hooks.** Operators frequently advertise "kids go free" or "£100 off per family" as a headline hook, and these offers are real, but they're calculated against the operator's own bundled price, not against the true cost of the components. A family that books a "kids go free" package that's £200 more expensive than the same family could put together themselves hasn't actually saved £100 — they've paid £100 less than an inflated starting point. Always compare the final total, never the discount percentage off the original figure.

## 2. The 10-minute price test — how to actually compare the two

Before committing to either route, run this five-step check. It takes about ten minutes and it's the single habit that separates travellers who consistently save money from travellers who guess.

1. **Price the package first.** Get a like-for-like quote from TUI, Jet2holidays, easyJet Holidays or BA Holidays for your exact dates, hotel star rating, board basis and party size. Note the total per person.
2. **Price the flight alone.** Search [JetMeAway flights](/flights) or a comparator for the same route and dates on a scheduled or budget carrier. Note whether the fare includes a hold bag — if not, add the realistic online add-on cost (£40–70 return for most European short-haul).
3. **Price a comparable hotel.** Search [JetMeAway hotels](/hotels) for a property matching the package's star rating and board basis as closely as possible — not a random cheaper option, or the comparison is meaningless. Multiply the nightly rate by the length of stay.
4. **Add the invisible extras.** Airport transfers (£15–45 per person each way depending on destination), any resort or tourist tax not shown in the headline rate, and travel insurance if you don't already hold an annual policy. These three line items are what most DIY comparisons forget, and forgetting them is why people wrongly conclude they've saved more than they have.
5. **Compare the two final totals, not the two headline prices.** Only once transfers, luggage and insurance are added to the DIY side is the comparison honest. If DIY still wins by £50 or more per person, book separately. If the gap is under £30-40 per person, the package's ATOL protection and included transfers/luggage usually make it the simpler, lower-hassle choice.

This same five-step process is exactly how the five route comparisons in the next section were built — the numbers below aren't headline flight-only or hotel-only prices, they're full landed costs on both sides.

## Package vs Separate Booking Savings: Real 2026 UK Route Pricing

Average European package prices have surged from **£1,038 per person for summer 2024 to £1,157 for summer 2025**, an 11% jump driven largely by hotel-cost inflation. Italy is up 7.4%, Portugal 6.8%, Cyprus 6.4%. Booking the components yourself lets you sidestep that operator-controlled price hike entirely — but the size of the saving (or the size of the package's win) depends heavily on exactly where you're going. Here's how five of the most-searched 2026 UK holiday routes actually compare, built from typical shoulder-season pricing across each destination.

### Spain — Costa del Sol / Costa Blanca, 7 nights, 2 adults

Spain remains the UK's most-booked overseas destination, and it's also where the package-versus-DIY gap narrows the most for traditional beach weeks. A typical 7-night, 3-star Costa del Sol package (flight + hotel + transfers + one checked bag each) from a South East airport runs roughly **£520–650 per person** in June/September shoulder season through TUI or Jet2holidays. Building the same trip yourself — a £70–110 return easyJet or Ryanair flight, a £55–75-a-night 3-star hotel on a bed-and-breakfast basis, plus a £25–35 each-way private or shared transfer and £45–65 return for a hold bag — lands close to **£480–600 per person** once every line item is added honestly. The gap is real but modest, typically **£30–80 per person** in the package's favour once transfers and luggage are counted properly, which is exactly why Which? found packages winning on 8 of 10 traditional beach trips: DIY bookers who forget to price transfers and bags routinely think they've saved more than they actually have.

### Turkey — Antalya / Dalaman all-inclusive, 7 nights, 2 adults

Turkey is where the package format earns its reputation. A 7-night all-inclusive Antalya package through TUI or Jet2holidays typically runs **£550–780 per person** in shoulder season, all food, drink and often entertainment included. Replicating genuine all-inclusive dining and drinks independently is close to impossible at the same price point — Turkish resort hotels rarely sell a comparable all-inclusive rate direct to independent bookers at a discount versus the operator's bulk-contracted price, and the flight-plus-transfer overhead (Antalya and Dalaman transfers can run 45–75 minutes and £30–45 each way) adds further cost. This is consistently one of the strongest package-wins-outright routes in this guide; DIY only makes sense here if you're deliberately choosing bed-and-breakfast or half-board over all-inclusive.

### Greece — Crete / Rhodes / the Greek Islands, 7 nights, 2 adults

Greece splits down the middle depending on which island and which hotel style you want. A 7-night half-board package to Crete or Rhodes through Jet2holidays or TUI runs roughly **£580–720 per person** in shoulder season. Building it yourself — a £90–150 return flight (Greek island routes have less budget-carrier density than Spain, so fares run higher), a £60–90-a-night boutique or family-run hotel, and a £20–35 each-way transfer — comes out close to **£520–680 per person**, a genuine **£40–90 per person saving**, and crucially gives you a hotel you actually chose rather than a bulk allocation. Greece is a good example of a "depends on the trip" route: chase a large resort all-inclusive and the package likely wins; chase a boutique whitewashed hotel in a specific village and DIY usually wins.

### Dubai — 5 nights, 2 adults

Dubai is overwhelmingly a DIY-favourable route for anyone not wanting the specific hotel an operator has allocated. A 5-night package (flight + 4-5 star hotel, room-only or breakfast) through BA Holidays or Expedia's package builder typically runs **£950–1,300 per person** depending on hotel category and season, spiking well above that over Christmas and New Year. Booking separately — a £320–550 return economy flight on Emirates or BA (Gatwick often undercuts Heathrow on the same Emirates schedule), plus a genuinely well-chosen 4-star hotel at £90–140 a night — lands closer to **£770–1,150 per person**, a saving that can exceed **£150–250 per person** specifically because Dubai's hotel market is enormous and highly comparison-shoppable, unlike a small Greek island with limited inventory. Dubai is also where the freedom to pick your own hotel category matters most: the difference between a package's allocated 4-star and a hand-picked 4-star with a rooftop pool is significant, and DIY is the only way to guarantee the second option.

### USA — Florida / New York, 7-10 nights, family of four

The USA is the clearest illustration of "it depends on family size." For a couple, a 7-night Florida flight-plus-hotel package through Virgin Holidays-style operators or a BA Holidays dynamic package runs **£1,100–1,600 per person** in shoulder season (avoiding the punishing school-holiday surcharge), and DIY booking — a £380–700 return flight plus a well-chosen hotel or aparthotel at £90–150 a night — frequently undercuts that by **£150–300 per person**, especially with a car hire booked separately rather than bundled. For a family of four with two young children, however, the maths flips: operator child discounts (often free or heavily reduced for under-12s) can make the package cheaper outright once you price two full-fare children's flights and a family room supplement into the DIY comparison. **Route the same trip both ways before booking if you're travelling as a family** — this is the single route category in this guide where the "who wins" answer depends most on party composition rather than destination.

<a id="freedom-factor"></a>

## 3. The freedom factor: flexibility you can't get with a package

Packages lock you in. Want to fly out Thursday and back Monday instead of the rigid 7-night Saturday-to-Saturday slot? Tough. Fancy switching from a beachfront resort on day three to a boutique stay in the old town for the second half of your trip? The package doesn't bend.

Booking separately means **you mix and match airports, dates and properties** like a traveller, not a customer being processed. Fly into Malaga, drive down to Tarifa, train it back from Seville. Stay two nights at a riad in [Marrakech](/destinations/marrakech), three at a beach hotel, two in a cool city pad. You can also cancel one element without losing the lot — change a refundable hotel without forfeiting your flight, or rebook a flight if a fare drops without abandoning your accommodation.

<a id="flight-timing"></a>

## 4. Cracking the flight code: timing, days and budget airlines

The booking-window rules have shifted. **Skyscanner's UK data points to 1–3 months ahead for European short-haul and 2–6 months for long-haul**, while Expedia's 2025 Air Hacks Report found international flights are surprisingly cheapest **18–29 days out**, saving up to 17% versus booking three months in advance. Whichever window you target, flexibility is the real saver: Skyscanner's "Whole Month" search routinely cuts another 30%, and the "Everywhere" tool lets you find the cheapest *destination* rather than the cheapest *flight*.

For day-of-week, **KAYAK puts Wednesday as the cheapest day to fly long-haul or domestically from the UK**, while Tuesday departures and Wednesday returns win for European hops. Sunday is the priciest day to fly home — adding around £93 to the average return — but, oddly, Sunday is the **cheapest day to actually book**, saving up to 17% on international fares. Forget the incognito-browser myth: Consumer Reports tested 372 searches and found prices were identical 88% of the time.

Budget airlines are the engine of cheap DIY travel, but Ryanair, easyJet and Wizz Air make their money on extras. A Which? investigation found Ryanair's advertised £12 cabin bag fee appeared just twice across 600+ flights, with an average actual fee of £20.50; easyJet's headline £5.99 figure never showed up at all in 500 tests, averaging £30. **Pre-pay your luggage online, check in at home, and bring a bag that genuinely fits the 40 × 20 × 25 cm under-seat dimensions.** Free flight-deal alert services like Jack's Flight Club and Secret Flying are worth signing up to — error fares from London routinely undercut packages by hundreds of pounds.

It's also worth checking Jet2 and TUI's own "flight only" fares before defaulting to a budget carrier. On the routes their charter aircraft already fly heavily — Antalya, Palma, Faro, Tenerife — flight-only fares can genuinely compete with or beat the equivalent easyJet or Ryanair price, because these are core network routes for the charter operators rather than opportunistic add-ons. On city-break routes where budget carriers dominate capacity (Barcelona, Amsterdam, Prague), the charter flight-only fares are rarely competitive — always check both before booking.

<a id="hotel-hacks"></a>

## 5. Hotel hacks: last-minute, direct booking and loyalty perks

Hotels work the opposite way to flights. **KAYAK's data shows booking around a week before your stay is typically cheapest**, and a NerdWallet study of 2,500 rates found booking 15 days out was 13% cheaper than booking four months ahead — and 22% cheaper at luxury properties. Apps like HotelTonight push that further, claiming savings of up to 27% with daily flash drops of 33% off. The exception is peak periods (school holidays, Christmas, big events) where you should still book three to six months out.

Once you've shortlisted on a comparison site, **check the hotel's own website**. Hilton Honors, Marriott Bonvoy, IHG One Rewards and Accor Live Limitless all offer member rates roughly 10% below public prices, plus free Wi-Fi, late checkout and breakfast that don't transfer if you book through a third party. OTA commissions run 15–25%, and hotels actively want to win you back direct. For UK leisure travellers, Hilton Honors gives the strongest return on free breakfast (especially via Amex Platinum's complimentary Gold status), while Accor's fixed-value points are easiest to make sense of for European trips. Tuesday check-ins and Thursday check-outs are typically cheapest; Friday arrivals can cost up to 20% more.

**Remember to price the invisible extras.** A refundable rate typically costs 10-20% more than non-refundable — worth paying on long-haul or peak-season trips where you've taken on the flight-cancellation risk a package would otherwise absorb for you, less necessary on a low-risk short-haul city break. And when comparing a DIY hotel-plus-transfer cost against a package's bundled transfer, use a real quote (£15-45 per person each way depending on destination and distance) rather than assuming public transport will cover it — that's the line item DIY comparisons most often skip, and skipping it is what makes a package look worse than it actually is.

<a id="packages-win"></a>

## 6. When a package genuinely makes sense

Be honest: packages aren't always the enemy. They tend to win on **traditional 7, 10 and 14-night all-inclusive beach holidays** to Spain, Greece, Turkey, the Canaries and the Caribbean, where operators bulk-contract food, drinks and entertainment that simply can't be replicated piecemeal — Turkey is the clearest example in this guide's route pricing above. Family holidays with kids' clubs, child discounts and adjoining rooms are also natural [package territory](/packages): operator child pricing (often free for under-2s, 20-50% off for older children) is very hard to beat booking separately at full adult airline and hotel rates, which is exactly why the USA family-of-four comparison above tips toward the package once two children's flights are priced in.

The other reason to bundle is protection. **ATOL — the Civil Aviation Authority's safety net — only covers flight-inclusive [packages](/financial-protection).** If your operator collapses, you're repatriated or refunded, full stop. ABTA's 2025 Holiday Habits survey found 54% of UK travellers would actually pay more for that ABTA-protected booking, and 43% who chose a package said it was the best value option. For older travellers, big lump-sum bookings, or anyone who lost money in 2020, that peace of mind has a real price.

<a id="atol-rights"></a>

## 7. ATOL protection and your consumer rights, explained properly

ATOL is the single most misunderstood piece of UK travel law, so it's worth being precise about what it actually does and doesn't cover.

**What counts as a package in 2026.** Under the Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements Regulations 2018, a package exists when you buy two or more different travel services (a flight plus a hotel, for example) for the same trip, from a single trader, in one transaction or via a linked booking process within 24 hours. Crucially, this covers "dynamic packages" too — where a site lets you pick your own flight and hotel combination but bills it as one transaction. If Expedia or Trip.com sell you a flight-and-hotel combination as a single checkout, that's a package, even though you assembled it yourself from live inventory rather than picking a fixed brochure holiday.

**What ATOL actually guarantees.** If a licensed UK operator goes bust, the CAA repatriates you if you're already abroad, and refunds you (or arranges an alternative) if you haven't yet travelled. It does not cover ordinary delays, cancellations or poor service — those are handled through the airline's own passenger-rights obligations (UK261) or the operator's customer service, not through ATOL. ATOL exists for one scenario specifically: financial collapse.

**Checking a licence before you pay.** Search any operator's name directly on the CAA's ATOL checker at atol.org/atol-holder-search before handing over a deposit. TUI, Jet2holidays, easyJet Holidays, BA Holidays, Expedia (ATOL 5788) and Trip.com (ATOL 11572) are all currently licensed. A legitimate operator will also show their ATOL number on your booking confirmation — if a deal looks unusually cheap and the operator can't produce a clear ATOL number, that's a real warning sign, not a technicality.

**ABTA vs ATOL — not the same scheme.** ATOL specifically covers flight-inclusive packages. ABTA is a trade body whose members commit to a code of conduct and offer separate protection for packages that don't include a flight (a coach tour, a hotel-only bundled deal). Many operators carry both logos because they sell both types of package — check which protection actually applies to your specific booking rather than assuming the presence of either logo covers everything.

**If it all goes wrong abroad.** Keep your ATOL certificate accessible while travelling (it's issued at the point of booking), and if you hear your operator has collapsed, contact the CAA's dedicated helpline directly rather than paying any "top up" fee a hotel might demand on the ground while chasing an unpaid operator invoice — report demands like that to the CAA instead of paying twice.

<a id="staying-protected"></a>

## 8. Stay covered: protecting your DIY trip

Booking separately means building your own safety net. Three layers do the job:

- **Pay by credit card** for any element over £100 to trigger Section 75 protection — your card issuer becomes jointly liable if the airline or hotel fails. Book direct with the supplier where possible to keep that link unbroken. Note that Section 75 and ATOL are separate schemes and don't stack or substitute for each other — Section 75 protects a card purchase from a specific trader's insolvency; ATOL protects an entire flight-inclusive package.
- **Buy [travel insurance](/insurance) the moment you book**, not the day you fly. Look for at least £5m medical cover, cancellation matching your trip cost, and crucially **Scheduled Airline Failure Insurance (SAFI) and End Supplier Failure cover** — Defaqto found 88% of annual policies don't include SAFI as standard. Post Office, Aviva and Staysure are consistently rated by Which?. A single annual multi-trip policy is usually enough to cover a whole multi-city itinerary, provided the trip-length cap and destination list match your plans — you don't need a separate policy per leg.
- **Use an itinerary app** like TripIt or Wanderlog to consolidate confirmations into one place, with flight alerts and offline access. Forwarding emails to plans@tripit.com auto-builds the entire trip.

A GHIC card gets you state healthcare in the EU but won't repatriate you or refund a missed flight. Insurance still does the heavy lifting. And remember the practical trade-off with DIY bookings: your hotel stays live and chargeable even if your flight is cancelled, so a refundable rate — while 10-20% pricier — buys back some of the flexibility a package would otherwise give you automatically.

## The bottom line

Packages aren't dying — they still win on traditional all-inclusive sun trips, large family bookings and offer ATOL protection that DIY can't match. But for **city breaks, shoulder-season escapes, multi-stop adventures and almost anything outside the Saturday-to-Saturday template**, booking your flight and hotel separately routinely saves £100–£300 per person, gives you a hotel you actually picked, and keeps your itinerary genuinely yours. The route-by-route pricing above shows why: Turkey and large-family USA trips lean package, Dubai and couples' USA trips lean DIY, and Spain and Greece genuinely depend on the specific hotel and dining style you want. The skill isn't in being loyal to one approach — it's in pricing both, every time, and only paying the package premium when it earns its place.
