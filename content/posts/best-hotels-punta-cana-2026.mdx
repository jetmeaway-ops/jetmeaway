  - q: "What is sargassum and when does it affect Punta Cana?"
    a: "Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts across the Atlantic and washes onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and yes, Punta Cana's east-facing coconut coast can get it. It is typically most likely from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach, and plenty of trips see clear sand and turquoise water. When it does land in volume it piles up on the beach and smells of sulphur as it rots, which makes swimming there unpleasant. We are not going to pretend it never happens, because it is the main beach disappointment for visitors expecting an unbroken postcard: the big resorts rake and clear their own sand each morning and some run offshore barriers, but nobody can promise you a flawless sea on a given week. Cap Cana's Juanillo beach and some sheltered spots often fare better than the open Bavaro strip."

## What to Do in Punta Cana

- **Bávaro Beach and the coconut coast** — the reason almost everyone comes: kilometres of white sand and leaning coconut palms along a warm, shallow Caribbean. ## Where to Base Yourself in Punta Cana — Bávaro, Cap Cana or Uvero Alto?

Punta Cana is a spread-out resort region, not a city, so where you base yourself matters — and unlike a city break, the three main zones feel quite different.

**Bávaro.** The heart of Punta Cana and the coconut coast: the longest run of resorts, the widest choice at every price, the shortest transfers from the airport at roughly 15 to 25 minutes, and — crucially — the only zone with a genuine walkable town. **El Cortecito** and neighbouring **Los Corales** give you a public beach, a strip of open-air seafood restaurants, dive shops and small stores, and it is where every budget and room-only hotel in this guide sits. For a first visit, for families, and for anyone watching the budget, Bávaro is the natural default.

**Cap Cana.** The upmarket, master-planned enclave just to the south, built around a marina and the exclusive **Juanillo beach**, with the glossiest resorts, championship golf and Scape Park on the doorstep. It is quieter, more polished and often escapes the worst of the sargassum, but it is pricier and there is little to do outside the resort gates without a taxi. The choice for a splurge or an upscale, do-nothing week.

**Uvero Alto.** The quieter stretch further north, around 40 minutes to an hour from the airport, with a run of large resorts on a wilder, emptier beach. Lovely for switching off completely, but genuinely remote — you will rely on your resort's facilities and organised excursions, and trips into Bávaro or beyond are a real drive. Best for a pure fly-and-flop, all-inclusive holiday where you have no intention of leaving the resort.

**Be honest with yourself about the trade.** If you want a full-board beach resort with everything on tap and no plans to leave it, any of the three works and Cap Cana or Uvero Alto add calm. If you want value, local restaurants, a walkable town and the freedom to explore, Bávaro — and especially the El Cortecito pocket — is the answer, and it is the only zone where the £22-to-£60 room-only market really exists.

## Punta Cana Hotels FAQs

**Is Punta Cana all-inclusive only, or can I stay room-only?**

Punta Cana is dominated by all-inclusive resorts, but no, it is not the only option — and understanding this is the whole game here. The long white-sand strip along Bavaro and the coconut coast is lined almost entirely with big gated all-inclusive resorts, where one price covers your room, all your meals, drinks, snacks and entertainment for the week. That is what most UK package holidays to Punta Cana book, and for a lot of people it is exactly right. But there is also a smaller, cheaper world of room-only hotels, guesthouses and self-catering aparthotels, mostly clustered in the local beach-town pocket of El Cortecito and Los Corales and a little inland, where you pay for a bed and eat in the town's restaurants. This guide covers both: the all-inclusive resorts in the luxury and mid tiers, and the room-only and aparthotel stays in the budget tier from £22 a night. Which suits you comes down to how you want to spend your days, covered in the next question.

**Is all-inclusive worth it in Punta Cana, or should I book room-only?**

This is the single most important decision for a Punta Cana trip, and the honest answer is that all-inclusive suits most visitors here but not everyone. Punta Cana was built as an all-inclusive destination — there is no historic town to wander, few independent restaurants outside the El Cortecito pocket, and the resorts are spread along the coast rather than clustered around a walkable centre. If you picture your holiday as beach, pool, buffet, swim-up bar and evening show without leaving the property, all-inclusive is genuinely good value and by far the least stressful way to do it, because eating and drinking a la carte adds up fast and the nearest supermarket may be a taxi ride away. If instead you want to be out most days on island trips, in Santo Domingo or exploring, and you are happy to eat in local restaurants, a room-only or self-catering base in El Cortecito works out much cheaper and frees you from paying for meals you will not eat. Do the sums for your actual plans, and always check exactly what a given all-inclusive rate covers, because the range from budget buffet-only to premium top-shelf is enormous.

**Why are Punta Cana's cheap hotels not on the resort beachfront?**

Because the beachfront is a purpose-built all-inclusive strip and there is very little cheap, independent accommodation on it by design. The coconut coast at Bavaro was developed plot by plot as large gated resorts, each with its own stretch of sand, staff, restaurants and security — so the beach you see in the brochures is almost entirely resort land. The budget hotels, guesthouses and aparthotels in this guide instead sit in the small local beach town of El Cortecito and Los Corales, where there is a genuine public beach and a cluster of restaurants and shops, and a little further inland in the everyday town of Veron-Bavaro where resort staff live. That is why £22 to £60 a night buys a real room here while the same money would not get you a lounger inside a resort. The honest trade is that most budget stays are room-only and either a short walk to the public beach or a little inland, rather than a gated all-inclusive with the sand on the doorstep — but El Cortecito's public beach is the same gorgeous coconut-palm coast, just shared rather than private.

**Is there a town or old centre in Punta Cana?**

Not in the way European travellers often expect, and it is worth setting that expectation before you book. Punta Cana is not a historic city with an old quarter, a cathedral square and centuries of streets — it is a modern resort region strung along the beach, named after the nearby cape and airport. The closest thing to a walkable town is the small beach settlement of El Cortecito and neighbouring Los Corales in Bavaro, which has a public beach, a strip of open-air seafood restaurants, bars, dive shops and souvenir stalls, and is where most of the budget and independent hotels sit. Inland, Veron-Bavaro is the ordinary working town where local people live, cheap and authentic but not a tourist sight. If you want genuine colonial history — cobbled streets, forts, the oldest cathedral in the Americas — that is Santo Domingo, a long day trip west, covered further down. Come to Punta Cana for the beach and the resorts, not for a historic centre, and you will not be disappointed.

**Where should I base myself — Bavaro, Cap Cana or Uvero Alto?**

These are the three main zones and they suit different trips. Bavaro is the heart of Punta Cana and the coconut coast — the longest run of resorts, the El Cortecito and Los Corales beach-town pocket with public beach and restaurants, the most choice at every price, and the shortest transfers from the airport at roughly 15 to 25 minutes. It is the natural default, especially for a first visit or anyone on a budget, because it is the only zone with a walkable town and cheap room-only options. Cap Cana is the upmarket, master-planned enclave to the south, built around a marina and the exclusive Juanillo beach, with the glossiest resorts, golf and the Scape Park adventure park on its doorstep — quieter and more polished, but pricier and with little outside the gates. Uvero Alto is the quieter stretch further north, around 40 minutes to an hour from the airport, with a run of large resorts on a wilder, emptier beach — lovely for switching off, but remote, so you will rely on resort facilities and organised trips. For most UK visitors, and everyone watching the budget, Bavaro is the sensible base.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Punta Cana?**

Yes — Punta Cana (PUJ) is one of the Caribbean's busiest airports and there are regular direct flights from the UK, most reliably on TUI and British Airways from London Gatwick, often as scheduled or charter holiday flights, with the exact departure airports and frequency changing by season. A direct flight is the comfortable option on a journey this long and it sidesteps the US-transit paperwork trap covered below, so it is usually worth paying a little more for. If no direct service runs on your dates you will be routed one-stop, most commonly through a US hub such as New York or Miami, or via Madrid on Iberia or Air Europa — the Madrid routing avoids the US entirely, which some travellers prefer. Search your own dates rather than assuming, because the direct charters in particular come and go with the season.

**How long is the flight from the UK to Punta Cana?**

A direct flight from London to Punta Cana takes roughly 9 to 10 hours. A one-stop routing through a US gateway or through Madrid adds a few hours once you count the connection, and the Madrid option in particular can turn it into most of a day. The Dominican Republic runs on Atlantic Standard Time and does not observe daylight saving, so it is around four to five hours behind the UK depending on the season — which, combined with a long westbound daytime flight, means you usually arrive in the afternoon or evening and have an early first night. The flight home is often overnight and feels shorter.

**If I route through the US to reach Punta Cana, do I need an ESTA?**

Yes, and it catches people out every year. If your flight connects through any airport in the United States — even if you never leave the transit area — you must hold an approved US ESTA (or a US visa) before you travel, because the US has no international transit zone and you legally enter the country to change planes. Apply on the official US government site well before you fly, not through a lookalike agency, and budget for the fee. A genuinely direct UK-to-Punta-Cana flight avoids the US completely and needs no ESTA, and so does a one-stop routing through Madrid rather than a US hub — which is one reason some UK travellers prefer the Spanish connection when no direct flight is available.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for the Dominican Republic?**

No — UK passport holders do not need a visa for tourist stays in the Dominican Republic, and can normally stay for up to 30 days on entry (extendable, for a fee, if you overstay). You do need a passport valid for the duration of your stay and an onward or return ticket. The old separate tourist card is now bundled into most airline tickets, covered in the next question, so for the majority of UK holidaymakers arriving by air there is no separate visa or card to buy in advance. Rules and passport-validity requirements do change, so confirm the current position on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly, and check your passport has enough validity well ahead of departure.

**What is the Dominican Republic tourist card, and do I still pay for it?**

The tourist card was historically a US$10 entry fee every visitor paid on arrival. Since 2018 it has been bundled into the airfare for most travellers arriving by air, so you almost certainly pay it as part of your ticket without noticing and do not queue to buy one at the airport — check your booking confirmation and you will usually see it itemised. There is also a separate departure tax, which is likewise normally included in your airfare these days. The practical upshot for a UK holidaymaker flying in on a package or scheduled ticket is that you generally have nothing extra to buy at immigration, but keep an eye on your confirmation, and if you are arriving by an unusual route or by land, check whether the card still needs paying separately.

**What currency does Punta Cana use — pesos or dollars?**

The Dominican Peso (DOP) is the official currency, but Punta Cana is heavily dollarised and US dollars are widely accepted in resorts, on tours and in tourist restaurants — many places even quote prices in dollars. As almost everywhere, though, paying in the local currency usually gets you a better rate than paying in dollars, and change often comes back in pesos at the seller's own rate. Draw pesos from ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than the machines in resort lobbies or the airport, which charge poor rates and high fees, carry small notes for tips and taxis, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, decline and let your own bank do the conversion. British pounds are not useful on the ground, so change or withdraw dollars or pesos rather than sterling.
**What is sargassum and when does it affect Punta Cana?**

Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts across the Atlantic and washes onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and yes, Punta Cana's east-facing coconut coast can get it. It is typically most likely from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach, and plenty of trips see clear sand and turquoise water. When it does land in volume it piles up on the beach and smells of sulphur as it rots, which makes swimming there unpleasant. We are not going to pretend it never happens, because it is the main beach disappointment for visitors expecting an unbroken postcard: the big resorts rake and clear their own sand each morning and some run offshore barriers, but nobody can promise you a flawless sea on a given week. Cap Cana's Juanillo beach and some sheltered spots often fare better than the open Bavaro strip.

**How can I check the sargassum situation before I travel?**

Look at recent dated photos and the newest guest reviews rather than the resort's own marketing shots, and follow the sargassum monitoring pages and local social-media groups that publish beach-by-beach updates for Punta Cana and the wider Dominican coast. If clear water is the priority, travel outside roughly April to August, choose a resort with a strong pool complex so a bad seaweed week does not sink the whole trip, and consider Cap Cana or a sheltered stretch, which often escape the worst. Nobody can guarantee the sea, so build a little flexibility into how you picture your days, and remember the pools, excursions and island trips are unaffected.

**When is hurricane season in Punta Cana?**

Atlantic hurricane season runs roughly June to November, with the highest risk from August to October. The Dominican Republic sits in the hurricane belt, and while direct hits on Punta Cana are less frequent than the forecasts sometimes suggest, the risk is real and it is one of the main reasons late-summer prices are so low. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than a heavy, short afternoon downpour that clears as fast as it arrives, but storms can disrupt flights and excursions, so take travel insurance that specifically covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your dates if you can, and follow the forecasts in the run-up. Punta Cana is a well-drilled resort region that handles storm season every year.

**Why is late summer cheaper in Punta Cana?**

Two things stack up from roughly August to October: it is the peak of hurricane season, and it overlaps with the tail of the sargassum window. Demand drops, so resort rates and flight prices fall, and you can find genuine bargains on all-inclusives that are expensive at Christmas, New Year and Easter. Whether that trade is worth it depends on your appetite for risk and your flexibility — you are gambling a little on the weather and the seaweed in exchange for a much lower price. Families tied to the UK school summer holidays often have little choice, in which case good insurance, a resort with excellent pools, and realistic expectations about the beach are the way to make the most of it. The flip side is that the sea is warmest and the resorts quietest in these months.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Punta Cana?**

No — stick to bottled or filtered water. Resorts almost always provide purified drinking water, either bottled in the room or from dispensers, and reputable restaurants use purified water and ice, so a drink with ice in a proper restaurant or resort is fine. Brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine for most people, but drinking it is not advised, and it is sensible to be a little careful with salads and unpeeled fruit from street stalls when you first arrive. Bring a reusable bottle and refill it from purified dispensers where you can, both to save money and to cut down on plastic.

**How do I get from Punta Cana airport (PUJ) to my hotel?**

Punta Cana International (PUJ) is the region's own airport — famous for its open-air, thatched-roof terminals — and it sits close to the resorts, roughly 15 to 25 minutes from most of Bavaro and Cap Cana and up to an hour to Uvero Alto. If you are on a package holiday your transfer is usually included and you simply find your rep. Booking independently, a pre-arranged private transfer door to door is the easiest option with luggage or a family, and it is worth arranging in advance rather than haggling in the arrivals hall. Official airport taxis have fixed zone fares — agree the price before you set off — and are fine for a couple of people. Ignore anyone approaching you inside the terminal offering transport or timeshare pitches, and walk out to your arranged pickup or the official taxi desk.

**Is Punta Cana safe for tourists?**

Punta Cana is one of the Caribbean's most established resort destinations and the overwhelming majority of visits are trouble free, but it is not risk free and sensible precautions matter. The resort strip is heavily geared around visitors and generally very safe; the ordinary towns inland are normal working places where usual urban common sense applies. Use official taxis or pre-booked transfers rather than unmarked cars, agree fares before setting off, do not carry more cash than you need, use ATMs inside bank branches, keep valuables in the room safe, be sensible on quiet stretches of beach after dark, and only book excursions through your hotel or a reputable operator. Watch your drink and your belongings around the nightlife as you would anywhere. Read the current UK government travel advice for the Dominican Republic before you go, as it carries practical safety guidance that is updated over time.

**How do I get around Punta Cana?**

Punta Cana is spread out and has no single walkable centre, so getting around is different from a city break. Within a resort you barely move; to go beyond it, most visitors use taxis, pre-booked excursions or hotel shuttles. Official taxis have fixed zone fares that are not cheap — always agree the fare before you get in, as they are not metered — and short hops within Bavaro are much less than a run to Cap Cana or the airport. There are cheap local guagua minibuses that run along the main roads and are used by residents, which are an adventure and very cheap but not geared to tourists with luggage. The El Cortecito beach-town pocket is walkable once you are there. If you plan to explore independently — Santo Domingo, inland, several different beaches — a hire car earns its keep, but for a pure resort-and-excursions holiday you will not need one.

**Can I visit Saona Island from Punta Cana?**

Yes — Saona Island is the classic Punta Cana day trip and one of the best. It is a protected island within the Parque Nacional del Este off the south-east coast, reached on a full-day catamaran-and-speedboat excursion, usually departing from Bayahibe about an hour and a half or so down the coast, with hotel pickup included. The day typically mixes a natural swimming-pool sandbar out at sea, snorkelling, a beach lunch and plenty of rum and merengue on the boat — touristy and busy, but the palm-fringed beaches are genuinely stunning and it is a brilliant day out. Book through your hotel or a reputable operator, take sun protection, cash for tips and drinks, and expect an early start and a long, happy day.

**Can I visit Catalina Island from Punta Cana?**

Yes, though it is a longer haul than Saona. Catalina Island lies off the coast near La Romana, further west, and is best known for superb snorkelling and diving on its shallow reefs and its wall dive. A day trip from Punta Cana means a longer road transfer to the boat, so it is a full and early day, best planned if snorkelling or diving is the priority rather than a casual beach afternoon. If you mainly want the palm-beach-and-sandbar experience, Saona is the easier and more famous call from Punta Cana; choose Catalina if the underwater world is what you are after.

**What is Hoyo Azul and Scape Park?**

Hoyo Azul is a spectacular natural cenote — a deep, vivid turquoise freshwater pool at the foot of a limestone cliff — and it is the headline attraction at Scape Park, an adventure park in Cap Cana. Scape Park bundles it with zip lines, cave and cenote swims, jungle trails and other activities, and it is one of the few genuine sights within Punta Cana itself rather than a long excursion away. The freshwater cenotes are, incidentally, completely unaffected by sargassum, so a Scape Park day is a good rain-or-seaweed backup. Book ahead, take water shoes and biodegradable sunscreen, and go early to beat the coach parties.

**Can I visit Santo Domingo from Punta Cana?**

Yes, but treat it as a long full-day trip rather than a casual outing. Santo Domingo, the Dominican capital, is roughly two to two and a half hours west of Punta Cana by road, and its Zona Colonial is the oldest continuously inhabited European city in the Americas — cobbled streets, the first cathedral built in the New World, forts and colonial squares, all a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is the real historical counterpoint to Punta Cana's resort strip, and well worth a day if you want more than the beach. Most people go on an organised coach tour with a guide, which handles the long drive and the highlights; independent travellers can drive or take a comfortable Expreso Bavaro coach. Either way it is an early start and a late return, so pick a day when you are happy to swap the beach for history.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Punta Cana?**

**When is the best time to visit Punta Cana?**

December to April is the driest, sunniest and most reliable stretch, with the lowest hurricane risk and generally the best beach conditions — and it is also the busiest and most expensive, with Christmas, New Year and Easter at peak. May and November are good-value shoulder months when the weather is usually still fine. June to November is hurricane season, and roughly April to August is when sargassum is most likely, which together explain why late summer is cheapest. If settled weather and the clearest water matter most, aim for the winter and early spring; if price matters most, the summer and autumn discounts are real, provided you understand what you are trading and carry good insurance.

**How many days do you need in Punta Cana?**

For a classic Punta Cana beach holiday, seven nights is the standard and works well — enough for several beach-and-pool days plus a couple of excursions like Saona Island and Scape Park, or a long day trip to Santo Domingo, without feeling rushed. A shorter four or five nights is fine if you mainly want to switch off on the beach. Because it is a fly-and-flop destination rather than a touring base with lots of sights, many UK visitors book a full week or ten nights all-inclusive and simply relax. If you want to combine it with touring the rest of the Dominican Republic, add extra nights and consider a hire car or internal transfers.

**Is Punta Cana good for families?**

Very — it is one of the most family-friendly destinations in the Caribbean. The big all-inclusive resorts are built for families, with kids' clubs, water parks, pools, calm swimming areas and evening entertainment all included in one price, which makes budgeting easy and keeps children occupied. The long, gently shelving Bavaro beach suits younger ones, excursions like Saona Island and Scape Park work well with school-age kids, and the short airport transfers help after a long flight. 👪 A family all-inclusive with a good pool complex is the easy, everything-in-one-place option, and a strong pool is also your insurance against a bad sargassum week. Pack plenty of high-factor sun protection for the intense Caribbean sun, and check each resort's specific family or adults-only policy before booking, as several of the resorts here are adults-only.

**Do I need a hire car in Punta Cana?**

Not for a standard resort holiday. If you are staying all-inclusive and doing a couple of organised excursions, you will never need to drive — transfers, taxis and tour operators cover everything, and there is nowhere you need to get to under your own steam. A hire car only earns its keep if you want to explore the wider Dominican Republic independently — Santo Domingo, several different beaches, inland towns — over several days on your own schedule. If you do drive, be aware that Dominican roads and driving standards can be challenging, especially at night, that a scooter or quad is riskier still, and that you should keep your documents and a little cash for the occasional police checkpoint. For most visitors, taxis and excursions are the simpler choice.

**How much should I tip in Punta Cana, and what are the taxes?**

Tipping is customary and appreciated, and it matters to service staff. Restaurants in the Dominican Republic legally add a 10 percent service charge plus an 18 percent ITBIS tax to the bill, so check what has already been added before you tip on top — a little extra for good service is normal and welcome, but you are not obliged to add a full further tip when the service charge is already there. At an all-inclusive resort, tipping is not required but a few dollars or the peso equivalent for housekeeping, bartenders and helpful staff goes a long way and noticeably improves your week. Small tips for tour guides, drivers and porters are expected. You can tip in US dollars or pesos — small notes are ideal, and pesos are always useful.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Punta Cana?**

No. Spanish is the language of the Dominican Republic, but Punta Cana is one of the most internationally visited resort regions in the Caribbean, and English is widely spoken in the resorts, on tours and in tourist restaurants. Away from the resort strip, in the local towns and with taxi drivers, less English is spoken, so a few polite words — hola, por favor, gracias, la cuenta — go a long way and are always appreciated. A translation app on your phone covers the rest. If you are staying all-inclusive you will have no language trouble at all.
