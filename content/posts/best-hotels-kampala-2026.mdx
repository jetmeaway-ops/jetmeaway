  - q: "Do UK travellers need a visa for Uganda?"
    a: "Yes. UK passport holders need a Uganda visa, and the standard route is to apply online in advance through the official Ugandan e-visa portal before you fly. It is not a turn-up-and-pay arrangement you should count on. Allow processing time, print your approval letter, and check the current rules on the official portal and the UK FCDO travel advice page before you book anything, because entry requirements change."
  - q: "What is the East Africa Tourist Visa?"
    a: "It is a single multiple-entry visa covering Uganda, Kenya and Rwanda, valid for 90 days, applied for through the country you enter first. If your trip takes in gorillas in Uganda, Kigali and the Maasai Mara, it is usually cheaper and simpler than three separate visas. If you are only visiting Uganda, the ordinary single-country e-visa is the simpler choice. Confirm current fees and conditions on the official portal."
  - q: "What currency is used in Kampala?"
    a: "The Ugandan Shilling (UGX). Cards work in the bigger hotels, malls and restaurants, but Uganda is far more of a cash economy than the UK — carry shillings for markets, boda fares, small cafés and rural stops. US dollars are genuinely useful for national park entry fees, gorilla permits and safari operators, and they should be clean, undamaged notes. Pound figures in this guide are rough conversions for guidance only, not exact quotes."
  - q: "Are there direct flights from the UK to Uganda?"
    a: "No. There is no direct UK to Uganda service, so you connect — most commonly via the Gulf hubs (Doha, Dubai, Abu Dhabi), via Nairobi with Kenya Airways, via Addis Ababa with Ethiopian, or via Amsterdam with KLM. Total journey time is typically 12 to 17 hours depending on the connection. All flights land at Entebbe International Airport (EBB), not Kampala itself."
  - q: "How far is Entebbe airport from Kampala?"
    a: "Roughly 40 to 50km, and that is the important number to plan around. The Entebbe Expressway has improved the run, but the old road and Kampala's own traffic mean the journey can take anywhere from about 45 minutes to two hours or more depending on the time of day. Arrange your transfer through your hotel, and if you have a very early departure or an evening arrival, consider sleeping in Entebbe rather than the city."
  - q: "Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Uganda?"
    a: "Yes — a yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Uganda, and you can be asked to show it on arrival. Get the vaccination well before you travel, since the certificate needs time to become valid, and keep the card with your passport. Check the current requirement with a travel clinic and the official guidance before you fly."
  - q: "Do I need malaria tablets for Uganda?"
    a: "Malaria is a real risk across Uganda, including in Kampala, and it is not something to improvise. See your GP or a travel clinic several weeks before departure to discuss antimalarial tablets, and pack a DEET repellent, cover up at dusk and use the mosquito net your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice — get professional advice for your own trip."
  - q: "Is Kampala safe for tourists?"
    a: "Kampala is generally welcoming and most visits pass without incident, but petty theft, pickpocketing and bag-snatching are real in crowded places like Owino Market, taxi parks and busy streets after dark. Use registered ride-hailing apps or your hotel car rather than walking at night, do not display phones or jewellery, keep bags closed and in front of you in markets, use the hotel safe and carry only what you need. Read the current UK FCDO travel advice for Uganda before you go."
  - q: "Should I use boda-boda motorbike taxis in Kampala?"
    a: "Honestly, we would not recommend flagging one down. Boda-bodas are everywhere and they are the fastest way through Kampala traffic, but road-accident rates involving them are high, helmets are often missing or unusable, and riders weave aggressively. If you use one at all, use a registered app-based boda service with a proper helmet. For most visitors a car through a ride-hailing app or a hotel driver is the sensible choice."
  - q: "How do I get around Kampala?"
    a: "Registered ride-hailing apps operate in Kampala and are cheap by UK standards — that is what most visitors use. Hotels will also arrange a car and driver by the half-day or day, which is the easiest way to string together the Kasubi Tombs, the mosque and the museum. Shared matatu minibuses are extremely cheap but confusing for a first-timer. Traffic is heavy and unpredictable, so always allow far more time than the distance suggests."
  - q: "When is the best time to visit Kampala and Uganda?"
    a: "The drier seasons: December to February and June to August. Those months also suit gorilla and chimp trekking, because forest trails in Bwindi and Kibale are less punishing when they are not saturated. The rains fall roughly March to May and September to November — travel is perfectly possible then and prices soften, but tracks get muddy and trekking is harder work. Kampala sits near the equator at altitude, so it is warm rather than baking all year."
  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Kampala?"
    a: "No — stick to bottled or properly filtered water, including for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach. Hotels almost always provide bottled water and many have refill stations. Be sensible about ice and raw salads at very cheap street stalls, and carry a bottle with you because Kampala is warm and hilly."
  - q: "Is English spoken in Uganda?"
    a: "Yes. English is an official language of Uganda and is used in government, business, signage and education, so you will have no trouble being understood in hotels, restaurants and with drivers. Luganda is the everyday language in and around Kampala, and a greeting in it goes a long way — but you do not need it to travel comfortably."
  - q: "Should I stay in Kampala or Entebbe?"

Entebbe is also calmer, sits on Lake Victoria, and has the botanical gardens and boats to Ngamba Island on its doorstep."

Book the permit before you book anything else, either directly through the authority or through a reputable operator who will secure it for you. Bwindi is a long drive or a short charter flight from Kampala, so build travel days into the plan."
  - q: "Is Kampala a good base for a safari?"
    a: "It is the logistical starting point for almost every Uganda trip.   - q: "What are the must-do things in Kampala?"

  - q: "What are the Kasubi Tombs?"
    a: "The Kasubi Tombs are the royal burial ground of the Kabakas, the kings of Buganda, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site on a hill in Kampala. The great thatched domed tomb building is one of the most significant pieces of traditional architecture in the region; it was badly damaged by fire in 2010 and has been the subject of a long restoration. It is an active cultural and spiritual site, so dress modestly and follow your guide's instructions."
  - q: "Can you go up the Gaddafi National Mosque?"
    a: "Yes — the Uganda National Mosque, widely known as the Gaddafi National Mosque after the Libyan leader who funded its completion, sits on Old Kampala hill and visitors can climb its minaret for a full 360-degree view across the city's hills. It is the single best viewpoint in Kampala. Modest dress is required, women are given a headscarf and wrap at the entrance, and there is a guided-tour fee."
  - q: "What is there to do at Jinja?"
    a: "Jinja, about two to three hours east of Kampala, is where the Nile leaves Lake Victoria, and it has become Uganda's adventure capital — white-water rafting on the White Nile is the headline, alongside kayaking, quad biking, bungee jumping over the river and gentler sunset boat trips. It makes a comfortable overnight trip from Kampala, and plenty of people do the rafting as a long day."
  - q: "Do they drive on the left in Uganda?"
    a: "Yes, Uganda drives on the left, like the UK. That said, very few visitors self-drive in Kampala — the traffic, the boda-bodas swarming between lanes and the unmarked junctions make a hired driver or a ride-hailing car far easier, and both cost little by UK standards. Self-driving becomes more realistic on the open roads out to the national parks, usually in a 4x4."
  - q: "Is tipping expected in Uganda?"
    a: "Tipping is customary and appreciated for guides, drivers, porters and restaurant staff, and it forms a real part of income in the tourism economy. Small shilling notes are the most useful, and US dollars are fine for safari and trekking guides. Some restaurants add a service charge, so check the bill before adding more."
  - q: "Is Kampala good for nightlife?"
    a: "Kampala has a reputation across East Africa for going out, and it earns it — the bars and clubs of Kololo, Kabalagala and the Industrial Area run very late, live music is everywhere, and the city is genuinely sociable. Go with people who know the city if you can, take a registered car home rather than walking, and watch your drink and your phone, exactly as you would in any big city."
  - q: "What should I know before visiting Owino Market?"
    a: "Owino, officially St Balikuddembe Market, is one of East Africa's largest markets and an overwhelming experience in the best and worst senses — thousands of stalls, narrow lanes and constant noise. It is also the most likely place in Kampala to have your pocket picked. Go in the morning, take a guide or a local friend, carry only small notes in a front pocket, leave the good camera at the hotel and keep your phone away."

## What to See in Kampala

Start with the hill that matters most. The **Kasubi Tombs** are the royal burial ground of the Kabakas, the kings of Buganda, and a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** — a great thatched dome that is one of the most important surviving pieces of traditional architecture in the region. It was badly damaged by fire in 2010 and has been the subject of a long restoration. This is a living cultural and spiritual site rather than a museum piece, so dress modestly, take a guide and follow the protocol you are given.

From there the city's set pieces come quickly. The **Uganda Museum** is the oldest museum in East Africa and covers the country's ethnography, natural history and traditional music. **Namugongo Martyrs Shrine**, on the edge of the city, marks the site where Christian converts were executed in the 1880s and draws enormous pilgrimages each June. The **Gaddafi National Mosque** — officially the Uganda National Mosque, and named for the Libyan leader who funded its completion — sits on Old Kampala hill, and climbing its minaret gives you a full circle of the city's hills and rooftops: the best view in Kampala, and worth the stairs. **Owino Market**, officially St Balikuddembe Market, is the overwhelming counterpoint, thousands of stalls deep and best visited in the morning with a guide and nothing valuable in your pockets. In the evening, **Ndere Cultural Centre** stages dance and drumming from across Uganda's regions in an open-air amphitheatre, and it is the most enjoyable single evening in the city. Quieter, and worth an hour, is the **Bahá'í House of Worship** on Kikaya hill — the Mother Temple of Africa — set in serene gardens above the traffic.

Then head for the water. **Entebbe**, on the Lake Victoria shore about 40 to 50km away, has the **botanical gardens** running down to the lake, the Uganda Wildlife Education Centre, and boats out to the **Ngamba Island Chimpanzee Sanctuary**, where rescued chimps live on a forested island. Back in the city, Kampala's **nightlife** is famous across East Africa — Kololo, Kabalagala and the Industrial Area bars run very late, with live music most nights.

Above all, Kampala is where a Uganda trip begins. From here and from Entebbe you travel to **Bwindi Impenetrable Forest** for **mountain gorilla trekking** — the reason most visitors come to Uganda at all — to **Kibale Forest** for chimpanzees, to **Queen Elizabeth National Park**, to **Murchison Falls** where the entire Nile forces itself through a gorge a few metres wide, and east to **Jinja**, where the Nile leaves Lake Victoria and the **white-water rafting** on the White Nile is world class. Gorilla permits are issued by the **Uganda Wildlife Authority**, are expensive, capped in number each day and sell out months ahead — book the permit before you book anything else. For planning, the **Uganda Tourism Board** is the official starting point, and you should read the current [UK FCDO travel advice for Uganda](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/uganda) before you book, since it covers entry rules, health requirements and region-by-region safety guidance.

## Choosing Where to Stay in Kampala

- **Kololo and Nakasero** — the two central hills, and the smartest, safest-feeling part of the city. The embassies, the best restaurants and most of the top hotels are here. If it is your first trip and you want to walk to dinner, base yourself on one of these.
- **Bugolobi and Naguru** — quieter residential districts to the east, with good value, a more local feel and a short drive into the centre. Comfortable rather than lively.
- **City centre** — the cheapest rooms and the easiest transport links, including the coach and matatu connections out of town. Honestly, though: it is busy, noisy and chaotic, and not somewhere to wander on foot at night. It works perfectly well as a budget base if you take cars in the evening.
- **Munyonyo and the Kigo road** — lakeside calm on the southeastern edge, on the way to Entebbe. Good for a rest day or a last night before a flight.
- **Entebbe** — worth being blunt about: **Entebbe airport (EBB) is around 40 to 50km from Kampala and the road can take one to two hours or more** depending on traffic. Many travellers deliberately stay in Entebbe for an early departure, a late arrival or a safari charter, and it is calmer, greener and right on Lake Victoria. If your itinerary is tight, sleep near the plane.

## Getting Around Kampala — the Honest Version

Kampala traffic is genuinely difficult. Journeys that look like ten minutes on a map routinely take an hour, junctions are unsignalled and improvised, and the **boda-boda motorbike taxis** that swarm between the lanes are the fastest and the riskiest way through it. Road-accident rates involving bodas are high, helmets are frequently missing or unusable, and riders take gaps that a UK visitor would not. Our advice is simple: **do not flag a boda in the street**. Use a **registered ride-hailing app** or a car and driver arranged by your hotel — both are inexpensive by UK standards and remove the fare negotiation entirely. If you do use a boda, use a registered app-based service that provides a proper helmet.

Beyond the road, normal big-city precautions apply and they are enough. Petty theft and bag-snatching happen in crowded places — Owino Market, the taxi parks, busy streets after dark — so keep your phone out of sight, carry bags closed and in front of you, use the hotel safe, draw cash inside bank branches or malls rather than on the street, and take a car rather than walking at night. Ask your hotel's reception which specific areas to avoid and when; they give straight, current answers that beat any guidebook. Pair that with the UK FCDO travel advice for Uganda linked above, which is updated regularly.

## Kampala Hotels FAQs

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Uganda?**
Yes. UK passport holders need a **Uganda visa**, and the standard route is to **apply online in advance via the official Ugandan e-visa portal** before you fly. It is not a turn-up-and-pay arrangement you should count on. Allow processing time, print your approval letter, and check the current rules on the official portal and the UK FCDO travel advice page before booking — entry requirements change.

**What is the East Africa Tourist Visa?**
A single **multiple-entry visa covering Uganda, Kenya and Rwanda**, valid for 90 days and applied for through the country you enter first. If your trip takes in gorillas in Uganda, Kigali and the Maasai Mara, it is usually cheaper and simpler than three separate visas. If you are only visiting Uganda, the ordinary single-country e-visa is simpler. Confirm current fees and conditions on the official portal.

**What currency is used in Kampala?**
The **Ugandan Shilling (UGX)**. Cards work in the bigger hotels, malls and restaurants, but Uganda is far more of a cash economy than the UK — **carry shillings** for markets, fares, small cafés and rural stops. **US dollars are genuinely useful** for national park entry fees, gorilla permits and safari operators, and should be clean, undamaged notes. Pound figures in this guide are rough conversions for guidance only, not exact quotes.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Uganda?**
**No — there is no direct UK to Uganda service.** You connect: most commonly via the **Gulf** hubs (Doha, Dubai, Abu Dhabi), via **Nairobi**, via **Addis Ababa**, or via **Amsterdam**. Total journey time is typically 12 to 17 hours depending on the connection. All flights land at **Entebbe International Airport (EBB)**, which is around **40 to 50km from Kampala**.

**How far is Entebbe airport from Kampala?**
Roughly **40 to 50km**, and that is the number to plan around. The Entebbe Expressway has improved the run, but the old road and Kampala's own traffic mean the journey can take anywhere from about 45 minutes to **two hours or more** depending on the time of day. Arrange your transfer through your hotel, and for a very early departure or a late arrival, consider sleeping in Entebbe instead.

**Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Uganda?**
⚠️ **Yes — a yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Uganda**, and you can be asked to show it on arrival. Get vaccinated well before you travel, since the certificate needs time to become valid, and keep the card with your passport. Check the current requirement with a travel clinic and the official guidance before you fly.

**Do I need malaria tablets for Uganda?**
⚠️ **Malaria is a real risk across Uganda, including in Kampala.** See your GP or a travel clinic several weeks before departure to discuss **antimalarials**, and pack a DEET repellent, cover up at dusk and use the mosquito net your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice — get professional advice for your own trip.

**Is Kampala safe for tourists?**
Kampala is generally welcoming and most visits pass without incident, but **petty theft, pickpocketing and bag-snatching are real** in crowded places like Owino Market, the taxi parks and busy streets after dark. Use registered ride-hailing apps or your hotel car rather than walking at night, do not display phones or jewellery, keep bags closed and in front of you, use the hotel safe and carry only what you need. Read the current UK FCDO travel advice for Uganda before you go.

**Should I use boda-boda motorbike taxis in Kampala?**
Honestly, we would not recommend flagging one down. **Boda-bodas** are everywhere and are the fastest way through Kampala traffic, but road-accident rates involving them are high, helmets are often missing or unusable, and riders weave aggressively. If you use one at all, use a **registered app-based boda service with a proper helmet**. For most visitors a ride-hailing car or a hotel driver is the sensible choice.

**How do I get around Kampala?**
**Registered ride-hailing apps** operate in Kampala and are cheap by UK standards — that is what most visitors use. Hotels will also arrange a car and driver by the half-day or day, which is the easiest way to string together the Kasubi Tombs, the mosque and the museum. Shared matatu minibuses are extremely cheap but confusing for a first-timer. Traffic is heavy and unpredictable, so always allow far more time than the distance suggests.

**When is the best time to visit Kampala and Uganda?**
The drier seasons: **December to February** and **June to August**. Those months also suit gorilla and chimp trekking, because forest trails in Bwindi and Kibale are less punishing when they are not saturated. The rains fall roughly **March to May** and **September to November** — travel is perfectly possible then and prices soften, but tracks get muddy. Kampala sits near the equator at altitude, so it is warm rather than baking all year.

**Can I drink the tap water in Kampala?**
No — stick to **bottled or properly filtered water**, including for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach. Hotels almost always provide bottled water and many have refill stations. Be sensible about ice and raw salads at very cheap street stalls, and carry a bottle with you, because Kampala is warm and hilly.

**Is English spoken in Uganda?**
Yes. **English is an official language of Uganda**, used in government, business, signage and education, so you will have no trouble being understood in hotels, restaurants and with drivers. **Luganda** is the everyday language in and around Kampala, and a greeting in it goes a long way — but you do not need it to travel comfortably.

**Should I stay in Kampala or Entebbe?**
Stay in **Kampala** for the city — markets, food, nightlife, museums and the tombs. Stay in **Entebbe** if you have a dawn flight, a late-night arrival or a safari charter, because **EBB is around 40 to 50km from Kampala and the road can take one to two hours or more**. Entebbe is also calmer, sits on Lake Victoria, and has the botanical gardens and boats to Ngamba Island on its doorstep.

**Which area of Kampala should I stay in?**
**Kololo and Nakasero** are the smartest and safest hilltop areas — embassies, the best restaurants and most of the top hotels. **Bugolobi and Naguru** are quieter residential options with good value and a local feel. The **city centre** has the cheapest rooms and the best transport links, but it is busy, noisy and chaotic — fine as a budget base if you take cars in the evening. **Munyonyo and Kigo** offer lakeside calm on the Entebbe side.

**What are the must-do things in Kampala?**
The **Kasubi Tombs** (UNESCO), the **Uganda Museum**, the **Namugongo Martyrs Shrine**, the minaret of the **Gaddafi National Mosque** for the best view over the city, the sprawl of **Owino (St Balikuddembe) Market**, a dance evening at the **Ndere Cultural Centre**, and the quiet **Bahá'í Temple** on its hill. **What are the Kasubi Tombs?**
The royal burial ground of the **Kabakas, the kings of Buganda**, and a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** on a hill in Kampala. **Can you go up the Gaddafi National Mosque?**

**What is there to do at Jinja?**

Yes, **Uganda drives on the left**, like the UK. **Is tipping expected in Uganda?**
Tipping is customary and appreciated for guides, drivers, porters and restaurant staff, and it forms a real part of income in the tourism economy. Small shilling notes are the most useful, and US dollars are fine for safari and trekking guides. Some restaurants add a service charge, so check the bill before adding more.

**Is Kampala good for nightlife?**
Kampala has a reputation across East Africa for going out, and it earns it — the bars and clubs of **Kololo, Kabalagala and the Industrial Area** run very late, live music is everywhere and the city is genuinely sociable. Go with people who know the city if you can, take a registered car home rather than walking, and watch your drink and your phone, exactly as you would in any big city.

**What should I know before visiting Owino Market?**
**Owino**, officially **St Balikuddembe Market**, is one of East Africa's largest markets and an overwhelming experience in the best and worst senses — thousands of stalls, narrow lanes and constant noise. It is also the most likely place in Kampala to have your pocket picked. Go in the morning, take a guide or a local friend, carry only small notes in a front pocket, leave the good camera at the hotel and keep your phone away.
