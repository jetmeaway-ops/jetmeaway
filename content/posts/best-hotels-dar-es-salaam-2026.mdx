The fast ferry from the port terminal on Sokoine Drive takes roughly two hours to Stone Town and runs several times a day — book a seat in advance in high season and take the earlier sailings, which are usually calmer. Or fly: the hop from Julius Nyerere International to Zanzibar takes about 20 minutes and is inexpensive, though you add airport time at both ends. Take motion sickness tablets if the sea bothers you."

The practical routine most visitors follow: use hotel cars or a trusted ride app rather than flagging street taxis, do not display phones, cameras or jewellery, keep car doors locked in traffic, use the hotel safe, carry only what you need for the day, and avoid walking alone after dark or along quiet stretches of beach.   - q: "What is there to see in Dar es Salaam itself?"
    a: "The National Museum, which holds fossil casts and finds associated with Olduvai Gorge and early human origins; the open-air Village Museum with traditional dwellings from across Tanzania; the enormous Kariakoo Market; the early-morning Kivukoni fish market by the harbour; the waterfront and Coco Beach on the Msasani Peninsula; and boat trips to Bongoyo Island and Mbudya Island for snorkelling and a beach lunch."
  - q: "How do I get to Bongoyo Island?"
    a: "Boats run from the Slipway on the Msasani Peninsula and take around 30 minutes to Bongoyo, a small marine-reserve island with a beach, grilled fish and easy snorkelling.   - q: "Is Kariakoo Market worth visiting?"
    a: "Yes, if you want to see how the city actually works — it is one of East Africa's biggest markets, dense with spices, fabric, produce and hardware, and completely unlike a tourist bazaar.   - q: "Do they drive on the left in Tanzania?"
    a: "Yes, Tanzania drives on the left, like the UK. That said, very few visitors self-drive in Dar — congestion, dala dala minibuses, bajaji tuk-tuks and boda boda motorbikes make a hotel car or a ride app far easier, and a driver by the half-day costs very little by UK standards."
  - q: "How do I get around Dar es Salaam?"
    a: "Ride apps operate in the city and are what most visitors use, along with hotel cars arranged at reception. Bajaji three-wheelers cover short hops cheaply. The DART bus rapid transit runs along the main corridors and is genuinely fast, but it is crowded and not the easiest introduction with luggage. Whatever you use, allow far more time than the distance suggests."
  - q: "Is tipping expected in Tanzania?"
    a: "Tipping is customary for safari guides, drivers, porters and restaurant staff and forms a real part of income in the tourism economy. Small shilling notes are useful day to day, and US dollars are fine for safari guides. Some restaurants add a service charge — check the bill before adding more."

Both are workable; the peninsula is more pleasant, the centre is more practical."

They are not Zanzibar, and it is only fair to say so, but they are a pleasant way to spend a night either side of the ferry without being stuck in traffic in town."

## What to see in Dar es Salaam — and what it is really the gateway to

Start with the honest version. Dar es Salaam is **Tanzania's largest city and its main port**, the commercial engine of the country, and for most visitors it is a hinge rather than a destination. Its two great functions are these: it is **the gateway to Zanzibar**, with fast ferries taking roughly **two hours** from the terminal on Sokoine Drive across to Stone Town, plus short flights that cover the same hop in about twenty minutes; and it is the springboard for the **southern safari circuit** — **Nyerere National Park**, which absorbed the old Selous Game Reserve, and **Mikumi National Park**, both reached by light aircraft or a long road transfer. The famous northern circuit — the **Serengeti, the Ngorongoro Crater and Kilimanjaro** — is a different trip entirely and is usually reached via Kilimanjaro International Airport and Arusha instead. Booking Dar and expecting the Serengeti next morning is the classic Tanzania planning mistake.

Once you accept that, the city itself is more rewarding than its reputation suggests. The **National Museum** on Shaaban Robert Street holds the material connected to **Olduvai Gorge** and the Leakey excavations — casts and finds from the deep human past that make the abstract phrase "cradle of mankind" suddenly concrete. The open-air **Village Museum** on Bagamoyo Road assembles traditional dwellings from tribes across Tanzania in one leafy compound, with dancing and craft demonstrations on some afternoons. **Kariakoo Market** is the city at full volume — spices, kanga fabric, produce, hardware, noise — and one of the biggest markets in East Africa. Get up early instead for the **Kivukoni fish market** by the harbour, where the night's catch comes ashore and is auctioned in a crowd; it is chaotic, pungent and the single most photogenic hour in Dar.

Then there is the water, which is what makes the city bearable. The **waterfront** along Ocean Road runs past the old German-era hospital and colonial buildings towards the peninsula, and **Coco Beach** on Msasani is where the city goes at the weekend for grilled maize, sugarcane juice and sunset. From the Slipway, boats leave for **Bongoyo Island**, a small marine-reserve island about half an hour offshore with a beach, snorkelling and grilled fish for lunch — the best half-day in Dar by a distance. **Mbudya Island** does the same job from the northern beach hotels. And the **Msasani Peninsula** itself holds the restaurants: Indian, Italian, Ethiopian, Swahili seafood, and the coffee shops that expats and Tanzanian professionals treat as offices.

For official, current information on visas, entry rules and safety, the two sources worth checking before you book are the UK government's [Tanzania travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/tanzania) and the [Tanzania Tourist Board](https://www.tanzaniatourism.go.tz/). Rules and requirements do change, and both are updated far more often than any travel guide.

## Choosing where to stay in Dar es Salaam

**Msasani Peninsula and Oyster Bay** are where most leisure visitors should aim. This is the sea-facing, embassy-and-expat side of the city: the smartest hotels, the best restaurants, the Slipway waterfront, Coco Beach and a noticeably calmer evening atmosphere. Sea Cliff Hotel, Hotel Slipway and the Oyster Bay Protea all sit here, and so do budget options like Q-Bar. You will still take taxis, but you can walk to dinner.

The **city centre and Kivukoni** are the practical choice. This is where the Zanzibar ferry terminal is, where the National Museum and the harbour front are, where the business towers stand, and where the cheapest rooms in this guide cluster. Golden Tulip, CBD Hotel, Holiday Inn, Four Points and the Hyatt Regency are all here. It is busy, hot and not somewhere to wander after dark, but if your trip is a night, a ferry and a flight, nothing beats it for convenience.

**Masaki** — effectively the smart end of the peninsula — is the upmarket dining quarter, and worth targeting if good food matters more to you than sightseeing. The **northern beaches** at Kunduchi, Mbezi and Jangwani give you resort pools and sea frontage, with the trade-off of a long drive into town. And staying near **Julius Nyerere International Airport (DAR)** genuinely makes sense for an early flight or a safari departure: Transit Airport B&B and Airport Transit Lodges exist for exactly this. Be honest about the traffic — the run between the peninsula and the airport can take well over an hour at peak, occasionally far more, and no amount of optimism shortens it.

## Honest practicalities and safety in Dar es Salaam

It is **hot and humid all year**, at sea level, on the equatorial coast. Working **air conditioning is the single thing to verify before you book** — more important than the star rating, the pool or the breakfast. Pack light cotton and linen, something modest that covers shoulders and knees for markets, mosques and the older parts of town, and a rain layer if you are travelling in the March to May long rains or around the November short rains. The dry, comfortable windows are **June to October** and **December to February**.

On safety: Dar is a big working port city with a genuine petty-crime and bag-snatching problem, and it deserves neither panic nor pretence. Use **hotel cars or a trusted ride app** rather than flagging a taxi in the street, agree or check the fare in advance, and keep car doors locked and windows up in slow traffic. **Do not display phones, cameras or jewellery** — most incidents reported by visitors are opportunistic grabs, and simply not having anything visible removes the opportunity. Carry only what you need for the day, leave passports and spare cash in the hotel safe, use ATMs inside banks or malls rather than on the street, and **take normal city precautions after dark** — do not walk alone, especially on beaches or quiet stretches of the waterfront. Ask your hotel which specific streets to avoid; reception staff will tell you straight.

Health matters more here than in a highland capital. **Malaria is a real risk in Dar and along the whole Tanzanian coast, including Zanzibar** — see your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly to discuss antimalarials, and pack repellent. A **yellow fever certificate may be required depending on your routing**, particularly if you are arriving from or transiting a yellow-fever country, so check before you travel and carry it with your passport if you need one. Drink bottled or filtered water. Tanzania **drives on the left**, like the UK, though very few visitors self-drive here. And carry some cash: the **Tanzanian Shilling (TZS)** for daily spending, plus **newer US dollar notes** for park fees, safari operators and some hotel rates — old or damaged dollar bills are routinely refused.

## Dar es Salaam Hotels FAQs

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Tanzania?**
Yes. UK passport holders need a Tanzania visa, and the recommended route is the **e-visa applied for online in advance** of travel. A visa on arrival has existed at Julius Nyerere International Airport, but the e-visa is the reliable option and saves you queuing after a long overnight flight. Rules, fees and processing times change, so always check the current requirements on the official Tanzanian immigration site and the UK FCDO travel advice page before you book.

**What currency is used in Dar es Salaam?**
The **Tanzanian Shilling (TZS)**. Cards work in hotels, malls and larger restaurants, but cash is king in markets and small guesthouses. **US dollars are widely used** for national park fees, safari operators and some hotel rates — bring newer notes, as older or damaged dollar bills are frequently refused. The pound figures in this guide are rough conversions for guidance only, not exact quotes.

**Are there direct flights from London to Dar es Salaam?**
No — and it is worth being clear about it. There is **no direct UK service** to Dar es Salaam. Most travellers connect through the Gulf with Qatar Airways via Doha, Emirates via Dubai or Etihad via Abu Dhabi, through **Amsterdam with KLM**, or via **Nairobi** with Kenya Airways. Total journey time is usually somewhere between 12 and 16 hours depending on the connection. The airport code is **DAR**.

**Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Tanzania?**
It depends on your route. A **yellow fever certificate may be required** if you are arriving from or transiting a country with yellow fever transmission risk — which catches out plenty of people connecting through African hubs. Requirements change and are enforced at the border, so check with a travel clinic and the official guidance well before departure and carry the certificate with your passport if you need one.

**Is malaria a risk in Dar es Salaam?**
Yes. **Malaria is a real risk in Dar es Salaam and along the whole Tanzanian coast**, including Zanzibar, all year round. See your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly to discuss antimalarial tablets, and pack a DEET repellent, cover up at dusk and use the mosquito net or air conditioning your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice.

**How do I get from Dar es Salaam to Zanzibar?**
Two ways. The **fast ferry** from the port terminal on Sokoine Drive takes **roughly two hours** to Stone Town and runs several times a day — book a seat in advance in high season and take the earlier sailings, which are usually calmer. Or **fly**: the hop from Julius Nyerere International to Zanzibar takes about 20 minutes and is inexpensive, though you add airport time at both ends. Take motion sickness tablets if the sea bothers you.

**Is Dar es Salaam safe for tourists?**
It is a large, busy port city with a real petty-crime and bag-snatching risk, and it is fair to plan around that rather than ignore it. The practical routine most visitors follow: use hotel cars or a trusted ride app rather than flagging street taxis, do not display phones, cameras or jewellery, keep car doors locked in traffic, use the hotel safe, carry only what you need for the day, and avoid walking alone after dark or along quiet stretches of beach. Ask your hotel which specific streets to avoid, and check the UK FCDO advice for Tanzania before you go.

**How many days do you need in Dar es Salaam?**
**One to two days** is enough for most people. Dar is a working port and commercial capital rather than a sightseeing city, and the great majority of visitors use it as a gateway — a night either side of the Zanzibar ferry, or before flying out to a safari camp. A single full day covers the National Museum, the Village Museum, Kariakoo Market and the waterfront. Add a second day if you want a boat trip to Bongoyo Island.

**Which area of Dar es Salaam should I stay in?**
**Msasani Peninsula and Oyster Bay** for the smartest hotels, best restaurants and a calmer, sea-facing feel; the **city centre and Kivukoni** for business, the ferry terminal, the National Museum and the cheapest rooms; **Masaki** for upmarket dining and boutique stays; and **near Julius Nyerere airport** if you have a dawn safari flight or a late arrival. Traffic is heavy, so pick the area closest to what you are actually doing.

**How long does it take to get to the airport in Dar es Salaam?**
Be honest with yourself about this one: at peak times the run between the Msasani Peninsula and **Julius Nyerere International Airport (DAR)** can take **well over an hour**, sometimes considerably more. Off-peak it might be 30 to 40 minutes. If you have an early flight or a safari departure, an airport-side hotel the night before is money well spent — several are listed above.

**When is the best time to visit Dar es Salaam?**
The dry seasons: **June to October**, and **December to February**. The **long rains** fall roughly **March to May** and can be genuinely heavy, and the **short rains** come around **November**. June to October is also the classic southern safari season for Nyerere and Mikumi, which is why so many Dar itineraries land in that window.

**Is Dar es Salaam hot?**
Yes, and humid with it, **all year round**. This is a tropical coastal city at sea level, not a highland capital like Nairobi — expect sticky heat and short, intense downpours in the rainy months. **Air conditioning is not a luxury here**, it is the thing to check before you book. Pack light cotton and linen, and something modest with shoulders and knees covered for markets and mosques.

**Do I need to speak Swahili in Dar es Salaam?**
No. Swahili is the national language and is what you will hear everywhere, but **English is widely used in hotels, business, tour operators and restaurants**, and is one of Tanzania's official working languages. A few words go a very long way socially: *jambo* for hello, *karibu* for welcome, *asante* for thank you, and *pole pole* for slowly.

**Can I drink the tap water in Dar es Salaam?**
Stick to **bottled or properly filtered water**. Most hotels provide bottled water or a filtered dispenser, and many now have refill stations to cut plastic. Use bottled water for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach, and be sensible about ice and raw salads at very cheap street stalls.

**Is Dar es Salaam a good base for a safari?**
It is the natural springboard for Tanzania's **southern circuit** — **Nyerere National Park**, which took in the old Selous Game Reserve, and **Mikumi National Park**, both reachable by light aircraft or a long road transfer. It is **not** the base for the northern circuit: the **Serengeti, Ngorongoro Crater and Kilimanjaro** are usually reached via Kilimanjaro International Airport and Arusha instead, which is a different flight and a different trip.

**What is there to see in Dar es Salaam itself?**
The **National Museum**, which holds fossil casts and finds associated with **Olduvai Gorge** and early human origins; the open-air **Village Museum** with traditional dwellings from across Tanzania; the enormous **Kariakoo Market**; the early-morning **Kivukoni fish market** by the harbour; the waterfront and **Coco Beach** on the Msasani Peninsula; and boat trips to **Bongoyo Island** and **Mbudya Island** for snorkelling and a beach lunch.

**How do I get to Bongoyo Island?**
Boats run from the **Slipway** on the Msasani Peninsula and take around 30 minutes to Bongoyo, a small marine-reserve island with a beach, grilled fish and easy snorkelling. **Mbudya Island** is reached from the beach hotels further north on the Kunduchi and Mbezi stretch. Both make a good half-day if you have a spare morning before a ferry or a flight.

**Is Kariakoo Market worth visiting?**
Yes, if you want to see how the city actually works — it is one of East Africa's biggest markets, dense with spices, fabric, produce and hardware, and completely unlike a tourist bazaar. Go in the morning, take a guide or a driver who knows it, carry very little, and keep your phone in your pocket. It is overwhelming in the best and the most tiring way.

**Do they drive on the left in Tanzania?**
Yes, **Tanzania drives on the left**, like the UK. That said, very few visitors self-drive in Dar — congestion, *dala dala* minibuses, *bajaji* tuk-tuks and *boda boda* motorbikes make a hotel car or a ride app far easier, and a driver by the half-day costs very little by UK standards.

**How do I get around Dar es Salaam?**
Ride apps operate in the city and are what most visitors use, along with hotel cars arranged at reception. *Bajaji* three-wheelers cover short hops cheaply. The **DART** bus rapid transit runs along the main corridors and is genuinely fast, but it is crowded and not the easiest introduction with luggage. Whatever you use, allow far more time than the distance suggests.

**Is tipping expected in Tanzania?**
Tipping is customary for safari guides, drivers, porters and restaurant staff and forms a real part of income in the tourism economy. Small shilling notes are useful day to day, and US dollars are fine for safari guides. Some restaurants add a service charge — check the bill before adding more.

**Should I stay on the Msasani Peninsula or in the city centre?**
**Msasani and Oyster Bay** if you want the sea, the good restaurants, hotel pools and a calmer evening. **The city centre and Kivukoni** if you are here to work, need the ferry terminal for Zanzibar, or want the cheapest rooms and easiest onward transport. Both are workable; the peninsula is more pleasant, the centre is more practical.

**Are there beach hotels in Dar es Salaam?**
Yes — the northern beaches at **Kunduchi, Mbezi and Jangwani** have resort-style hotels with pools and sea frontage, and there are places on the southern side at **Kigamboni** across the harbour.
