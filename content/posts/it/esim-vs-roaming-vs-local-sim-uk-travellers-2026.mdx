## 1. The hidden cost of just leaving roaming on

The default — switch the phone on, ignore the warning popup, get on with your holiday — is also the most expensive choice for the vast majority of trips. Inside the EU it's tolerable: EE charges **£2.47 a day** to use your UK plan in 47 European destinations on plans taken out from July 2021, capped at 50GB fair-use. Three charges **£2 a day** unless you're on an Inclusive Roaming plan. O2 still includes EU roaming free on most consumer plans up to a 25GB cap, and Vodafone's policy depends on the tier you signed up for.

Step outside the EU and the maths gets ugly fast. EE's standard roaming pass for the USA is **£5 a day**, or £25 for a 7-day pass; in Thailand it's the same £5/day or £25/week, and pay-as-you-go calls run at **£1.66 per minute**. A two-week trip to Phuket on your existing UK SIM with no roaming pass can easily land a £100+ bill. Anyone who's opened their phone on the runway in Bangkok and seen "Welcome to AIS, your network" will know the cold panic that follows.

The traveller's instinct of "I'll just turn it off" doesn't hold either — apps update in the background, iCloud syncs photos, Maps caches new tiles. One forgotten setting and the bill speaks for itself.

There's also a psychological cost that doesn't show up on the bill itself: **usage anxiety.** Travellers on a per-day or per-MB roaming plan tend to under-use their phone abroad — turning off Maps navigation to save data, avoiding a video call home, not looking up a restaurant review before walking in — because every megabyte feels like it's ticking a meter. That's a real cost even when the final invoice looks manageable, and it's one of the quieter reasons a flat-fee eSIM or a genuinely inclusive roaming plan tends to make for a noticeably more relaxed trip than watching a data counter all week.

Reading your own contract matters more than any general rule in this guide. UK mobile contracts vary enormously by signup date, tier and even by which retention deal you negotiated last time you called to threaten leaving. The EE Full Works plan, Vodafone's Xtra Airtime add-on, and Three's Inclusive Roaming option can each turn "roaming is expensive" into "roaming is free" for the destinations they cover — the mistake is assuming your specific plan behaves like the default one, when in the UK's competitive post-Brexit market there's real variation between two people on the "same" network.

## How Travel eSIMs Work for UK Travellers in 2026

If you've never used a travel eSIM, the best mental model is: it's a second SIM card that lives entirely in software. Your phone keeps your UK SIM active for calls and texts (so your banking codes and 2FA messages still land), and the travel eSIM piggybacks on a foreign network just for data.

The buying experience now takes about five minutes. You pick a plan in the [Airalo](/esim) or Yesim app, pay with Apple Pay, scan a QR code, and the eSIM installs. Activation is automatic the moment your plane lands and your phone connects to the destination network. **No SIM swap, no losing your UK number, no scrabbling for a paperclip in arrivals.** A subtle but real win: you also keep the same WhatsApp account because WhatsApp is tied to your phone number, not the SIM you happen to be using.

The trade-offs are honest. Travel eSIMs are data-only in most cases — you can't make a normal phone call from a basic travel eSIM plan, though WhatsApp, FaceTime and iMessage all work fine over the data connection. Local SMS to non-WhatsApp numbers (a hotel reception, a taxi driver) usually needs your UK SIM, which means roaming charges if you don't disable that line first, or a premium eSIM plan with a bundled local number.

**The full install-to-activation sequence, step by step:**

1. **Buy on WiFi before you fly.** Open the Airalo or Yesim app or website while still on UK WiFi, pick your destination and data size, and pay. Both accept Apple Pay, Google Pay and standard cards.
2. **Scan the QR code.** The app (or an emailed confirmation) shows a QR code. On iPhone: Settings → Cellular → Add eSIM → Use QR Code. On Android: Settings → Network & Internet → SIMs → Add eSIM. The whole scan-to-install process takes under 90 seconds.
3. **Label the lines.** Give your UK SIM and the new eSIM clear labels ("UK — EE" and "Spain Trip") so you can tell at a glance which line is doing what — this matters when you're setting data routing in the next step.
4. **Set data routing.** In cellular settings, set your default line for data to the travel eSIM, and keep your UK SIM as the line for calls and SMS (or turn its data off entirely to guarantee no accidental roaming charges).
5. **Fly.** The eSIM sits dormant — no cost, no active connection — until your phone detects the destination network after landing, at which point it connects automatically.
6. **Track usage in-app.** Both Airalo and Yesim show live data balance in their apps whenever you're online, so a quick check on day 2 or 3 catches any unexpected burn rate before it becomes a problem.

## 5. When a local SIM still wins

Travel eSIMs have eaten most of the local-SIM market, but there are still scenarios where walking into a Movistar or AIS shop on day one is genuinely the better play.

- **Long stays (one month or more).** A 30-day local prepaid SIM in Spain or Thailand undercuts almost any travel eSIM on per-GB cost. Movistar's "Movistar Prepago" Spanish SIM gives 25GB+ for around €15. Thailand's AIS one-month tourist SIM packs unlimited 4G for roughly £20.
- **Heavy hotspot / tethering use.** Some travel eSIMs cap or throttle hotspot data after a few GB. Local SIMs almost always allow full-speed tethering up to your plan's allowance.
- **You need to call local numbers.** Booking a restaurant, ringing a hotel reception, calling a taxi driver who hasn't heard of WhatsApp — only a SIM with a real local phone number does this without WhatsApp's blessing. Most travel eSIM plans are data-only.
- **Backpacker-style multi-month trips through one country.** Buying a local SIM, topping it up monthly, and using it as your only line is the cheapest way to spend three months in Vietnam.

The friction is real, though. Thailand requires passport registration at point of sale (3 SIMs per person per brand, NBTC rules in force from August 2025). Spain's ID requirements are softer but still require a passport scan. The USA is famously hostile to short-term prepaid SIMs at airport kiosks — Mint Mobile or T-Mobile prepaid online plus a friendly delivery address is the usual workaround.

**A practical middle path many long-stay travellers miss:** buy a short eSIM (7–14 days) to cover arrival and the first days of settling in, then switch to a local SIM once you've had time to find a proper shop away from the airport-kiosk markup and compare local carriers on the ground. This avoids the two worst outcomes — paying tourist-kiosk prices at arrivals under time pressure, or landing with zero connectivity while you hunt for a SIM shop and can't even look up directions to one.

**Where to actually buy a local SIM without the tourist markup.** Airport kiosks in almost every country charge 2–4x the price of the same SIM bought at a city-centre carrier shop or, in some countries, a supermarket or convenience store — 7-Eleven sells AIS and TrueMove SIMs in Thailand at close to list price, for instance. If you're not in a rush the moment you land, a short taxi or transit ride to a proper carrier store (Movistar, Vodafone, Orange, Vodacom, Telcel — whichever operates locally) both saves money and usually gets you a staff member who can set the SIM up correctly, rather than a kiosk optimised for a five-minute tourist transaction.

## iPhone, Android and the throttle question

Travel eSIMs only work if your phone supports eSIM. The good news: virtually every iPhone sold in the UK from late 2018 onwards (iPhone XS, XR and newer) supports dual SIM with a physical SIM and an eSIM running side by side. The XS Max, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16 lines all qualify. Older iPhones (X, 8, SE 1st gen) do not.

On Android the picture is more fragmented. **Google Pixel 4 and later, Samsung Galaxy S20 and later, and the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold/Flip range** all support eSIM. Most other current flagships do too, but it's genuinely worth checking before you buy a plan — older mid-range Androids, Chinese-market imports and certain carrier-locked devices still don't have eSIM hardware. Airalo and Yesim both maintain compatibility checkers on their sites.

A second gotcha: **5G and throttling**. Most travel eSIMs do support 5G in 2026 — Airalo, Yesim and Holafly all advertise it — but the host network in your destination decides whether your eSIM gets prioritised. In practice you'll often see 4G/LTE speeds in places where a local SIM would happily pull 5G. "Unlimited" plans almost always throttle after a daily fair-use threshold (Airalo's unlimited plans typically slow after 3GB/day, Holafly after a higher but undisclosed cap). For Netflix on a beach this is fine; for tethering a working day's video calls it might bite.

**A third gotcha, easy to miss: dual-eSIM vs dual-physical-SIM hardware limits.** Some iPhone and Android models support two active eSIMs simultaneously alongside a physical SIM; others support only one eSIM active at a time even if multiple are installed, requiring a manual switch in settings between, say, your travel eSIM and a backup. Before relying on a "switch between two eSIMs mid-trip" strategy (covered in the dual-eSIM section of our companion [student eSIM guide](/blog/best-student-esims-uk-2026)), check your specific phone model's simultaneous-eSIM limit — the difference between "both active at once" and "one active, one needs manual switching" changes how useful a backup eSIM actually is in practice.

**Checking your own phone before you buy anything.** Both Airalo and Yesim publish compatibility checkers on their websites — search your exact model (not just "iPhone 14" but the specific variant, since US-market and some carrier-locked phones sold in the UK secondhand market occasionally ship without eSIM enabled even on an otherwise-compatible model). It takes thirty seconds and avoids the genuinely bad outcome of buying a plan for a phone that can't install it — most providers won't refund an eSIM once the QR code has been generated and scanned, even unsuccessfully, so check first.

<a id="uk-network-roaming"></a>

## 7. UK network roaming charges 2026 — the real comparison

If you'd rather skip eSIMs and just understand what your existing UK SIM costs abroad, here's where the four big networks land in 2026:

- **EE.** £2.47/day in the EU on plans taken from 7 July 2021, with a 50GB fair-use cap. Outside the EU, daily passes from £5 (Thailand, much of Asia) to £7 (USA peak), or £25 weekly. EE Full Works plan includes Zone 1 worldwide roaming.
- **Three.** £2/day for EU roaming unless you're on an Inclusive Roaming plan. Republic of Ireland is fully inclusive on every Three plan with no fair-use cap.
- **O2.** EU roaming free by default on most consumer and business plans, capped at 25GB fair-use. Outside the EU, the O2 Travel Inclusive Zone covers many destinations on certain plans; otherwise per-day fees apply.
- **Vodafone.** Tier-dependent. Some plans include EU roaming, others charge a daily fee (typically £2.49). Vodafone's Xtra Airtime plans bundle 51 destinations including the USA, Australia and South Africa — the strongest "just leave it on" option of the four if you've got the right tariff.

The pattern: if you're going to the EU on any modern plan, roaming is fine. Anywhere else, the daily fees outpace a travel eSIM within a day or two.

**Checking your own tariff — the two-minute exercise that changes this whole guide's advice for you personally.** Log into your network's app (My EE, My Vodafone, My O2, My Three) and search "roaming" in the help section, or dial the network's roaming-check short code (each network publishes one). You're looking for three answers: is EU roaming included and to what fair-use cap, does the plan include any non-EU destinations for free, and what's the daily or weekly rate for anywhere else. Screenshot the answer before you travel — network websites are not always reliable to load on international WiFi if a dispute comes up later, and a screenshot from home settles any argument about what you were told your plan covered.

**Why the four networks diverge so much post-Brexit.** Before Brexit, "roam like at home" was an EU regulation, not a competitive choice — every UK network was legally required to offer free EU roaming with no meaningful differentiation. Once that requirement lapsed in 2021, each network made a different commercial bet: EE and Vodafone largely reintroduced daily fees (recovering margin), Three kept some roaming free as a customer-acquisition play before later tightening it, and O2 doubled down on free EU roaming as a headline differentiator specifically because its rivals had reintroduced charges. The result is a genuinely fragmented market in 2026 where "what does my phone cost abroad" no longer has one UK-wide answer — it depends entirely on which network and which specific plan you're on, which is exactly why the roaming-check step above matters more now than it did before 2021.

## 9. The 30-day round-the-world data budget

Multi-stop trips are where the cost gap becomes hardest to ignore, because roaming fees compound with every border crossing while an eSIM strategy can be planned once before you leave. Here's a realistic 30-day round-the-world itinerary — a common UK gap-year or career-break route — costed both ways.

**Sample itinerary:** UK → Thailand (8 days) → Vietnam (6 days) → Australia (10 days) → USA, transiting home (6 days).

**Roaming the whole way on EE**, assuming the standard £5/day non-EU pass applied continuously for all 30 days: **£150** (30 × £5), plus the practical problem that EE's pass structure is sold in weekly blocks, so a 30-day continuous trip actually needs to be bought as roughly 4–5 weekly passes, pushing the real total closer to **£175–£200** once you account for passes that don't perfectly divide into your day count.

**eSIM strategy, bought per-leg before departure or at each stop's start:**

| Leg | Plan | Approx. cost |
|---|---|---|
| Thailand, 8 days | Airalo Thailand 10GB/30d | ~£11 |
| Vietnam, 6 days | Airalo Vietnam 5GB/30d | ~£7 |
| Australia, 10 days | Airalo Australia 10GB/30d | ~£15 |
| USA, 6 days | Airalo USA 5GB/30d | ~£12 |
| **Total, 30 days** | | **~£45** |

That's roughly a quarter of the roaming cost for the same trip, and every plan can be bought and installed in advance from a UK WiFi connection before you ever board the first flight — no scrambling for a kiosk at Suvarnabhumi or LAX. The trade-off worth naming honestly: buying four separate country eSIMs means four separate QR-code installs and four separate top-up trackers, which is more admin than one continuous roaming pass. For travellers who'd rather pay a premium for simplicity, Airalo's "Discover Global" regional bundle (100+ countries on one profile) or a Yesim multi-region plan collapses this into one purchase, typically landing **£70–£110** for a comparable 30-day, ~30GB total allowance — still well under half the roaming cost, with none of the per-country admin.

The general rule for any multi-country trip: **price the per-country eSIM route first**, since it's almost always cheapest, and only step up to a regional bundle if the admin overhead of switching profiles at each border genuinely bothers you enough to pay for the convenience.

**Building your own round-the-world budget.** The four-country example above is one common shape, but the method generalises to any itinerary: list each country and the number of days there, check whether a regional bundle covers more than one leg (Southeast Asia legs are frequently covered by a single Airalo "Asialink" style bundle rather than needing separate Thailand and Vietnam purchases), price the per-country route as your baseline, then compare the single regional-bundle price against that baseline to see whether the convenience premium is worth paying for your specific trip length and number of border crossings. As a rule of thumb, once a trip crosses more than 4-5 countries, a regional or global bundle usually starts winning on both price and admin simplicity over buying individually per country.

**What this looks like for a longer 90-day career-break trip.** Extending the same logic to a 90-day trip (a common shape for a UK sabbatical or extended career break) roughly triples the per-leg eSIM costs shown above if the itinerary and data usage stay proportional, landing in the region of £130–£160 for pure per-country eSIM purchases across a similar four-to-six-country route — still a fraction of what 90 days of continuous non-EU roaming passes would cost on any UK network. For trips this long, though, it's worth revisiting the "local SIM still wins" section above for any single country where you're spending three or more consecutive weeks — the crossover point where a local monthly SIM contract undercuts even a well-priced eSIM typically arrives somewhere around the three-to-four-week mark in any one country.

<a id="families-multi-device"></a>

## 10. Families and multi-device trips

An eSIM plan is per-device, not per-person or per-family — there's no "family plan" the way UK home broadband or mobile contracts sometimes work. That changes the maths for a family of three or four travelling together, and it's worth working through honestly rather than assuming per-person costs simply multiply.

**Option A — every phone gets its own eSIM.** For a family of four on a 7-day EU trip, four separate Airalo 5GB/30-day eSIMs run roughly £13–£16 each (depending on destination pricing), so **£52–£64 total**. Compare that against four people roaming on EE at £2.47/day each: 4 × £2.47 × 7 = **£69.16** for the week. The eSIM route wins, but not by the dramatic multiple you'd see on a single-traveller comparison, because you're paying the per-device cost four times over.

**Option B — one bigger eSIM, hotspotted to the rest of the family.** Buy one larger-data eSIM (say, 20GB/30 days on the primary parent's phone, roughly £20–£25 in Europe) and hotspot the other three devices off it. This is usually the cheapest option for a family trip and the one JetMeAway recommends by default, with two caveats: hotspotting drains the host phone's battery fast (worth carrying a power bank), and tethered data on some "unlimited" eSIM tiers is throttled or capped separately from direct-device data, so check the specific plan's tethering allowance before assuming 20GB really means 20GB shared four ways.

**Option C — kids stay off data entirely.** For families with younger children who don't need independent connectivity, skipping a device eSIM for them and relying on the parents' phones (for photos, translation, maps when the family is together) is the simplest and cheapest option of all — there's no need to buy a plan for a device that's mostly used for games on hotel WiFi.

**Multi-device business or group trips** (colleagues travelling together, a group of friends) follow the same logic as Option A: each device needs its own plan, so budget per-device rather than per-trip, and compare that per-device total against a group roaming cost before assuming eSIMs automatically win — for very short trips (2–3 days) in the EU, a modern UK plan's included EU roaming can still be cheaper than buying and managing multiple eSIM profiles for a whole group.

**A worked family example for a longer trip — two weeks in Thailand, family of four.** Roaming on EE for four people at £5/day each for 14 days: 4 × £5 × 14 = **£280** — genuinely painful. Four separate Airalo Thailand 10GB/30-day eSIMs at roughly £11 each: **£44 total**. One 20GB Airalo Thailand eSIM on a parent's phone at roughly £22, hotspotted to the other three devices for light use (maps, WhatsApp, occasional browsing, not everyone streaming simultaneously): **£22 total**, though this depends on the family's actual combined data appetite staying under the plan's cap and on tolerating one phone's battery draining fast enough to need a power bank. The spread between these three options — £280 down to potentially £22 — is the single largest cost gap in this entire guide, and it's exactly the scenario where doing the two minutes of maths before the trip pays for itself many times over.

**Tablets and second devices for entertainment.** A tablet loaded with downloaded shows, books and offline maps before you fly needs no data connection at all for most of a family trip, and is usually the cheapest way to keep children entertained on transport days without adding another eSIM to the family's device count. Reserve the family's paid data allowance for the phones that actually need live connectivity — navigation, translation, checking in with people back home — rather than defaulting to connecting every screen.

<a id="tethering-rules"></a>

## 11. Tethering and hotspot rules by provider

Whether your eSIM data plan can be used to get a laptop or tablet online — not just the phone itself — matters enormously for anyone working remotely, and the rules differ meaningfully by provider and even by plan tier within the same provider.

- **Airalo.** Most standard data plans allow tethering with no separate fee, and the data simply comes out of your existing allowance. On fixed-GB plans (1GB, 5GB, 10GB etc.) tethering is unrestricted up to the plan's total data cap. On Airalo's unlimited plans, expect a fair-use throttle after a daily threshold (commonly around 3GB/day) that applies to all usage, tethered or not.
- **Yesim.** Tethering is generally supported across plan tiers, and Yesim's unlimited plans are positioned partly around dual-network reliability (useful for a stable video call) rather than raw hotspot speed. As with Airalo, treat "unlimited" as "unlimited until a fair-use cap kicks in," and check the specific plan's fine print if a full working day of video calls depends on it.
- **Holafly.** Historically the most restrictive of the three on tethering — some of Holafly's cheaper unlimited tiers have limited or blocked hotspot use entirely, reserving full tethering for higher-priced plans. If a laptop workday is the whole point of the trip, confirm Holafly's current tethering policy for the specific plan before buying, since this is the one area where its "truly unlimited" pitch doesn't automatically extend to hotspot use.

**The practical rule for remote workers:** if your trip genuinely depends on tethering a laptop for a full working day, buy a fixed-GB plan sized generously (10GB+ for a week of moderate use, more for video-call-heavy days) rather than relying on an unlimited plan's fair-use throttle holding up under sustained hotspot load — a throttled connection mid-video-call is a worse outcome than paying for headroom you might not fully use.

**Realistic data burn rates for tethered work**, so you're sizing the plan rather than guessing: a video call (Zoom, Teams, Google Meet) at standard quality uses roughly 500MB–1GB per hour; the same call on "high definition" can push 1.5–2GB per hour; sending and receiving email with attachments is negligible by comparison, typically under 100MB for a full working day; and uploading or downloading large files (a client deck, a video export) can consume several GB in a single transfer. A working day built around three hours of video calls plus general email and browsing realistically needs 2.5–4GB — multiply that by the number of working days in the trip to size a fixed-GB eSIM plan with a sensible buffer, or budget for Holafly-style unlimited if the day-to-day mix is too unpredictable to plan around.

**A cheaper alternative worth considering before buying a big tethering-capable plan:** hotel and coworking-space WiFi. Most business-grade UK travel is to cities with reliable hotel and café WiFi, and the honest move for a genuinely heavy work day is to work from a location with fixed WiFi and reserve the eSIM's tethered data for the gaps in between (transport, a coffee shop without WiFi, a delayed flight). This alone can cut the required eSIM data size by more than half compared with assuming every working hour needs to run over cellular tethering.

## 12. A quick decision framework

If the fifteen destinations, the round-the-world budget and the family maths above feel like more detail than you need for one specific trip, here's the condensed version — a short set of questions that gets most UK travellers to the right answer in under a minute.

**Is the destination in the EU and is your UK plan on free or cheap EU roaming (O2 by default, or another network if you've checked your specific tariff)?** If yes, stop here — just travel, your phone already works, and nothing in this guide changes your outcome. If your EU roaming isn't free (EE, most Three and Vodafone tiers), move to the next question.

**Is the trip under 10 days and is your data need predictable (maps, messaging, some browsing, not heavy video or tethering)?** If yes, buy a fixed-GB Airalo eSIM sized to roughly 0.5–1GB per day of light use, or 1.5–3GB per day if you'll be navigating constantly or sharing photos and videos home. This is the cheapest correct answer for the largest number of trips in this guide.

**Is your data need genuinely unpredictable, or does the trip involve heavy video calls, content uploading, or tethering a laptop for work?** If yes, buy Holafly's unlimited plan (or Airalo/Yesim's unlimited tier if the fair-use throttle level is acceptable for your use) and stop counting gigabytes — the certainty is worth the premium at this usage level.

**Is the trip one month or longer in a single country?** If yes, buy a short eSIM to cover the first week while you settle in, then switch to a local prepaid or contract SIM once you can shop around away from the airport — this consistently beats every eSIM option on pure cost for stays this long.

**Are you travelling with a family or group of three or more?** If yes, default to one larger eSIM on the phone most likely to stay near everyone, hotspotted to the rest, unless your group's combined data need or a battery/tethering-limit concern makes per-device plans the safer bet — work the actual numbers using the family section above rather than assuming either option is automatically cheaper for your specific group size and destination.

**Do you need to receive calls or SMS from a local number (a delivery driver, a restaurant booking, a hotel)?** If yes, either keep your UK SIM active for that purpose alongside a data-only eSIM, or buy a premium eSIM plan (some Yesim tiers) that bundles a local number — a pure data-only eSIM alone won't cover this need.

For **EU short breaks** on most modern UK contracts, just leave your phone alone — the daily roaming fee or built-in allowance will cover you for less hassle than an eSIM. Travelling as a **family or group**, budget per-device rather than per-trip, and default to one larger eSIM hotspotted across the group unless tethering limits make separate plans the safer bet.
