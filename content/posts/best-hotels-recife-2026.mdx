A word about what Recife is, because it surprises people. This is a city of around 1.6 million on the Atlantic coast of Pernambuco, built where two rivers meet the sea and spread across islands and bridges — hence the "Brazilian Venice" nickname. It has a restored colonial old town, two of Brazil's most unusual museums, a Carnival that many Brazilians rate above Rio's for authenticity, and a long seafront strip at Boa Viagem where almost all the hotels are. Brazil's currency is the **Brazilian Real (BRL, R$)**; the pound figures here are rough conversions of live rates, not fixed quotes.

**One thing you must know before you book a beach hotel here:** Boa Viagem has a genuine, well-documented **shark risk**. Attacks have happened along this stretch over a period of decades, official warning signs are posted, and swimming beyond the natural reef line is restricted and strongly discouraged. What people do instead is swim inside the reef, in the shallow natural pools that appear at low tide. That is the safe option, and it is a perfectly good one — but it is not the same as a beach you swim out from, and no honest guide to Recife hotels should bury it. Obey the flags and the signage, always.

There is **no direct flight from the UK to Recife**. The two realistic routes are a connection in Lisbon, from where TAP flies on to Recife (REC), or a connection through São Paulo Guarulhos and then a domestic flight of roughly three hours north. Allow a full travel day in each direction.

## What to See and Do in Recife

Start with **Recife Antigo**, the restored old-port district on the island where the city was founded. **Rua do Bom Jesus** is its showpiece — a street of painted colonial facades that holds the **Kahal Zur Israel synagogue**, the oldest in the Americas, built by the Dutch-era Jewish community in the seventeenth century and now a small, moving museum. At the water's edge, **Marco Zero** is the square from which distances in the city are measured, and it gives one of Brazil's better sunset views back over the bridges and the old port.

Then the two Brennand museums, which are unlike anything else in Brazil. The **Instituto Ricardo Brennand** is a purpose-built castle-style museum in landscaped gardens, holding one of the world's largest private collections of arms and armour alongside Dutch-colonial paintings of Pernambuco. A few miles away, the **Oficina Cerâmica Francisco Brennand** is an old family ceramics factory turned into a sprawling workshop and sculpture garden filled with the artist's own strange, monumental work. Either one is a half-day; both in a day is a lot.

**Boa Viagem** is the city's seafront: a long promenade, a wide beach, joggers at dawn and kiosks all afternoon. Read the safety section below before you plan a swim.

The best day out, though, is **Olinda** — the UNESCO-listed colonial hill town just 20 to 30 minutes north. Steep cobbled streets, painted houses, baroque churches, artists' workshops and viewpoints back across the Recife skyline. Go in the morning while it is cooler and wear shoes you can climb in. About an hour south, **Porto de Galinhas** is the other classic excursion: at low tide, traditional **jangada** sailing rafts ferry visitors out to natural reef pools full of fish, which is as good as shallow-water snorkelling gets in Brazil. Check the tide table before you go, because the pools only exist at low water.

If your dates allow it, **Carnival in Recife and Olinda** is one of Brazil's biggest and most authentic, and it happens in the street rather than in a stadium. The region's own **frevo** — fast, acrobatic, danced with tiny umbrellas — and the drum-heavy **maracatu** are the sounds of it, and the Galo da Madrugada parade in Recife is among the largest carnival blocks in the world. Book many months ahead. Year-round, the **Capibaribe river** and the tangle of bridges that stitch the city's islands together are what earn Recife its "Brazilian Venice" nickname.

Before you book, read the UK government's current [foreign travel advice for Brazil](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/brazil), which covers entry requirements, safety and health, and check it again in the week before you fly. For events, opening hours and regional information, Brazil's official tourism site [Visit Brasil](https://www.visitbrasil.com/) is the reliable starting point.

## Boa Viagem, Sharks and Staying Safe: the Honest Version

This is the section most guides skip, and it matters more than any hotel recommendation here.

**There is a genuine, well-documented shark risk at Boa Viagem beach.** Attacks have occurred along this stretch of the Pernambuco coast over a period of decades — enough of them that the authorities post permanent warning signs along the seafront and run monitoring programmes. Swimming beyond the natural reef line is restricted and strongly discouraged. The factors usually cited are the deep-water shipping channel close to shore, habitat changes around the Suape port development to the south, and the reef itself.

What this means in practice is simple. **Do not swim out.** The safe option, and the one locals and visitors use, is the shallow **natural pools that form inside the reef at low tide** — warm, calm, waist-deep water where children can play safely. Check the tide, stay inside the reef, obey the flags and the signage without exception, and follow the lifeguards. Avoid the water at dusk and at high tide. The beach itself is genuinely lovely and the promenade is one of the best things about the city — you are simply booking a beach to walk and sit on, plus reef-pool swimming at the right hours, rather than an open-water swimming beach.

The rest is standard Brazilian city sense, which applies here as it does in Rio or São Paulo. Use **Uber or a licensed taxi after dark** rather than walking, particularly between districts. Do not flash a phone, a camera or jewellery on the street. Carry the cash you need and leave the rest in the hotel safe. Avoid the beach at night. Stay to the busy, lit parts of Boa Viagem and Recife Antigo in the evening. Recife requires a bit more care than a European city, and none of this should put you off — it should just be part of your planning.

## Where to Stay in Recife: Choosing Your Base

**Boa Viagem** is the answer for most visitors. It is the long seafront district in the south of the city, it holds the overwhelming majority of the hotels in this guide at every price level, and it has the restaurants, the shopping malls and the promenade. It also feels the most comfortable in the evening. The trade-off is the swimming, covered above.

**Recife Antigo** is the choice for culture and character: the restored old port with Rua do Bom Jesus, the synagogue, Marco Zero and a strong weekend bar and live-music scene. There are far fewer hotels here — the Novotel Recife Marina is the main option in this guide — and it is quieter midweek, but it is the more interesting place to wake up.

**Piedade and Candeias**, just south of Boa Viagem in the neighbouring municipality of Jaboatão dos Guararapes, are where the beachfront gets cheaper. Several of the best-value properties in this guide sit there, including the cheapest 4★. You will Uber into the city more often.

**The airport district around Imbiribeira** deserves a mention that it would not get in most cities, because **Recife's airport is unusually close to the beach — roughly 10 to 15 minutes from most Boa Viagem hotels**. That means an airport hotel here is not a compromise base, and it is the obvious pick if you have a dawn departure or a late-night arrival.

**Boa Vista and Ilha do Leite**, in the centre, are the cheapest and most central options for anyone whose trip is about the museums and the old town rather than the sand. Brazil drives on the right, but you do not need a car in Recife — Uber is cheap and the city is compact.

**Can you swim at Boa Viagem beach?**

Attacks have occurred here over a period of decades, official warning signs are posted along the seafront, and swimming beyond the natural reef line is restricted and strongly discouraged. What people do instead is swim inside the reef, in the shallow natural pools that form at low tide, which is the safe option. Always obey the flags and the signage, do not swim at high tide or at dusk, and take the lifeguards' instructions seriously. The beach is beautiful and busy and perfectly enjoyable — it just is not a beach you swim out from.

**Why is there a shark risk at Boa Viagem?**
The commonly cited factors are the deep-water channel dredged close to shore, the loss of habitat further south around the Suape port development, and the reef line that changes how sharks move along this stretch of coast. The result is that a normal-looking city beach carries a real hazard beyond the reef. Local authorities post warning signs and run monitoring programmes. Treat the reef pools at low tide as the swimming option and stay out of the deeper water.

**Is Recife safe for tourists?**
Recife is a big north-eastern Brazilian city and it requires normal Brazilian city precautions rather than fear. Use Uber or a licensed taxi after dark instead of walking, keep your phone out of sight on the street, do not carry more cash or jewellery than you need, avoid quiet areas and beaches at night, and leave valuables in the hotel safe. Boa Viagem and Recife Antigo's restored streets are the areas most used to visitors. Read the UK government's travel advice for Brazil before you go.

**Which area should I stay in in Recife?**
Boa Viagem for most visitors — it is the long beachfront strip in the south of the city and it holds the great majority of the hotels in this guide, plus the restaurants, shopping and the safest-feeling evening streets. Recife Antigo, the restored old-port district, for culture, historic character and nightlife. Around Imbiribeira and the airport for early flights or the cheapest rooms. Piedade, just south of Boa Viagem in Jaboatão, for beachfront at lower prices.

**Is Boa Viagem a good place to stay?**
Yes, for most first-time visitors. It is Recife's seafront district: a long promenade, a wide beach, a dense strip of hotels at every price level, plenty of restaurants and shopping malls, and a ten to fifteen minute drive from the airport. The one thing to understand before you book is the shark risk — you are booking a beach view and a promenade rather than a swimming beach, and the safe swimming is inside the reef at low tide.

**Is Recife Antigo worth staying in?**
It is the most characterful part of the city. Recife Antigo is the restored old-port quarter on the island where the city began, with the colourful Rua do Bom Jesus, the Kahal Zur Israel synagogue — the oldest in the Americas — the Marco Zero square and a lively bar and music scene at weekends. There are fewer hotels there than on Boa Viagem, and it is quieter midweek, but for culture and atmosphere it is the more interesting base.

**How far is Recife airport from Boa Viagem?**
Very close, which is one of the underrated things about the city. Recife Guararapes International (REC) sits in the south of the city and reaches most Boa Viagem hotels in around 10 to 15 minutes by car. Uber works well and is the simplest option; official taxis wait outside arrivals; and there is a metro station at the airport connecting to the city rail line. If you have a dawn departure, the airport-area hotels around Imbiribeira are worth considering.

**Is Olinda worth a day trip from Recife?**
Absolutely — it is the single best day out from the city. Olinda is a UNESCO-listed colonial hill town about 20 to 30 minutes north of Recife, with steep cobbled streets, painted houses, baroque churches, artists' workshops and viewpoints back over Recife's skyline. Go in the morning while it is cooler, wear proper shoes for the hills, and allow at least half a day. Many visitors end up wishing they had stayed a night there.

**Is Porto de Galinhas worth a day trip?**
Yes, and it is the region's most popular beach excursion. Porto de Galinhas lies about an hour south of Recife and is famous for its natural tide pools, reached at low tide on traditional jangada sailing rafts, where you can snorkel among fish in clear shallow water. Go on a low-tide day — the pools only exist then — and expect crowds at weekends and holidays.

**What is Carnival like in Recife and Olinda?**
It is one of Brazil's biggest and most authentic Carnivals, and it is a street Carnival rather than a stadium one. Recife and Olinda celebrate together with frevo and maracatu — the fast, acrobatic frevo dance with its little umbrellas is the region's own tradition — and the Galo da Madrugada parade in Recife is one of the largest carnival blocks in the world. Hotels sell out months ahead and prices rise sharply, so book very early if that is your trip.

**When is the best time to visit Recife?**
Recife sits close to the equator and is warm all year, so the real variable is rain. The driest and sunniest stretch runs roughly from September to February, which is the best window for a beach trip. The wettest months are roughly April to July, when heavy tropical downpours are common — usually short and dramatic rather than all-day, but frequent enough to affect a short holiday. Carnival, in February or March, is peak season for prices whatever the weather.

**Does it rain a lot in Recife?**
In the wet season, yes. Recife's rainy period runs approximately April to July and can bring heavy downpours; the city has had serious flooding events in that window in the past. Outside those months rain is much less frequent and the sunshine is reliable. Temperatures stay warm year-round — think high twenties Celsius most days — so you are packing for heat and humidity whenever you come.

**How many days do you need in Recife?**
Three to four nights is the sweet spot. That gives you a day for Recife Antigo and the museums, a day in Olinda, a day at Porto de Galinhas or on the beach, and a slower half-day for the Brennand institutes. Two nights is enough for the city itself but forces you to choose between Olinda and Porto de Galinhas. Add a fifth night if you want to head further along the coast.

**Do you need to speak Portuguese in Recife?**
Brazilians speak Portuguese, not Spanish, and outside the bigger hotels English is not widely spoken in Recife. A handful of phrases goes a long way: bom dia, boa tarde, por favor, obrigado if you are a man or obrigada if you are a woman, and a conta por favor for the bill. Download an offline translation app for menus and signs — it is genuinely useful here.

**Can I drink the tap water in Recife?**
Stick to bottled or filtered water. The mains supply is treated but building tanks and older pipework mean quality varies, and most visitors and plenty of residents drink bottled. It is cheap and sold everywhere, and many hotels have filtered dispensers, so carry a refillable bottle. In this heat you will drink more than you expect — hydration matters more here than the water quality does.

**What is there to see in Recife itself?**
Recife Antigo, the restored old-port district, is the heart of it: Rua do Bom Jesus with its painted facades, the Kahal Zur Israel synagogue — the oldest in the Americas — and the Marco Zero square on the waterfront. Add the Instituto Ricardo Brennand, a castle-style museum with an outstanding armour and art collection, and the Oficina Cerâmica Francisco Brennand, an extraordinary ceramics workshop and sculpture garden. The Capibaribe river and its many bridges are what earn Recife its Brazilian Venice nickname.

**What are the Brennand museums?**
Two separate places, both founded by members of the same Recife family and both worth your time. The Instituto Ricardo Brennand is a purpose-built castle-style museum set in gardens, holding one of the world's largest private collections of arms and armour along with Dutch-colonial paintings. The Oficina Cerâmica Francisco Brennand is an old ceramics factory turned into a sprawling sculpture garden filled with the artist's own work. Allow half a day for either.

**Why is Recife called the Brazilian Venice?**
Because the city is built where the Capibaribe and Beberibe rivers meet the sea, spread across islands and peninsulas and stitched together by a great many bridges. The nickname is a stretch if you are expecting canals and gondolas, but the waterways genuinely shape the city, and the view of the bridges and the old-port skyline from Marco Zero at sunset is one of Recife's best sights.

**Do I need a car in Recife?**
Not for the city. Recife's sights are close together, Uber is cheap and plentiful, and the airport is minutes from Boa Viagem. A car only makes sense if you plan several trips down the coast to Porto de Galinhas and beyond, and even then organised day trips or Uber often work out simpler given city traffic and parking. Brazil drives on the right.

**Is Recife good for families?**
It works well if you plan around the water. The reef pools at low tide are excellent for small children — shallow, warm and calm — and the Boa Viagem promenade is easy with a pushchair. Many hotels here have pools, which matters given the swimming restrictions, and several of the budget flats give you a kitchen. Porto de Galinhas is the standout family day out.
