Poznań (roughly *POZ-nahn*) is the capital of the Wielkopolska region and, in the most literal sense, where Poland started. The centrepiece is the **Old Market Square (Stary Rynek)**, one of the finest in the country, ringed by painted merchant houses and dominated by the Renaissance **Town Hall** — where, every day at noon, two **mechanical billy goats emerge above the clock and butt their heads together twelve times**. It is a two-minute spectacle, it is free, and the square fills with people craning their necks for it. A short walk east lies **Ostrów Tumski**, Cathedral Island, the cradle of the Polish state and home to Poland's oldest cathedral and the tombs of its first rulers. Elsewhere in the centre you get the vast **Imperial Castle (Zamek)** built for Kaiser Wilhelm II and now a busy arts venue, the **Poznań Croissant Museum** celebrating the EU-protected **rogal świętomarciński** (St Martin's croissant), and **Stary Browar**, a nineteenth-century brewery converted into a shopping-and-arts complex that regularly wins design awards. East of town, **Lake Malta** offers a regatta course, a year-round toboggan run, the Nowe Zoo and thermal baths. And the **Enigma Cipher Centre** tells a story British visitors rarely know: three Poznań mathematicians first cracked the German Enigma cipher in the 1930s and passed their work to Britain before the war, feeding directly into Bletchley Park. Poznań is also Poland's big **trade-fair city**, which is where our one serious booking warning comes from.

## Poznań Hotels FAQs

**What is the cheapest hotel in Poznań?**
On recent live searches the lowest real, bookable rate in this guide is Old Town Mystery- from around £35 a night, followed by Moon Hostel Poznań and Apartamenty Pomarańczarnia from about £45 and Hotel Gaja from around £46. A £35 floor in a genuine European city centre is remarkable, and it is one of the main reasons Poznań works so well as a cheap UK city break. Prices are set in Polish złoty, not euros, which is a big part of why your money stretches here.

**How much does a budget hotel in Poznań cost per night in 2026?**
Realistically about £35 to £79 a night for the cheaper tier on ordinary dates. The floor sits near £35, the reliable three-star band runs roughly £46 to £65 (Hotel Gaja, Park Inn by Radisson, Palazzo Rosso Old Town, Hotel Altus Poznań Old Town, Hotel Korel), and the upper budget stretch of familiar names like Hotel Topaz, Hampton by Hilton Poznan Old Town and Ibis Poznan Stare Miasto tops out around £76 to £79. The one big exception is trade-fair weeks, when rates across the whole city can multiply.

**Does Poznań use the euro?**
No — and this catches a lot of UK visitors out. Poland is in the European Union but has kept its own currency, the Polish złoty (zł / PLN). Do not turn up with euros expecting to spend them: shops, restaurants and most hotels price in złoty, and the few places that accept euros give you a poor rate. Pay by card wherever you can (contactless is near universal), or draw złoty from a bank ATM and always decline the machine's own currency conversion. Prices in this guide are converted to pounds for easy comparison.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Poland?**
For a normal holiday, no. British passport holders can visit Poland without a visa for short stays as a tourist — up to 90 days in any 180-day period across the Schengen area as a whole, so time spent in other Schengen countries counts towards the same allowance. Your passport needs to meet the usual validity and issue-date rules. The EU is rolling out its new EES biometric border checks and the ETIAS travel authorisation, which will change what UK travellers need at the border, so check the current official requirements shortly before you fly.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Poznań?**

**How do I get from Poznań Airport to the city centre?**
Poznań–Ławica (POZ) is only about 7 km west of the centre, so the transfer is short and cheap. **Why do Poznań hotel prices sometimes double or triple?**
Trade fairs. Poznań is Poland's biggest trade-fair city, and the Poznań International Fair grounds (Międzynarodowe Targi Poznańskie, MTP) sit right beside the main railway station in the middle of town. When a major fair is on, thousands of exhibitors and buyers arrive at once and hotel rates across the entire city can multiply several times over — the same room that costs £50 on a quiet Tuesday can be several times that on a fair week. Always check the MTP event calendar before you fix your dates. Shifting a trip by one week is the single biggest money-saver in this city.

**What are the goats on Poznań's town hall?**
Poznań's most-loved daily ritual. Every day at noon, two mechanical billy goats emerge above the clock on the Renaissance Town Hall in the Old Market Square and butt their heads together twelve times while a bugler plays. The tradition dates back to the sixteenth century and a story about two goats that escaped a cook and started fighting on the tower. It only lasts a couple of minutes, it is completely free, and the square fills up with people looking upward — it is worth timing your first morning around it.

**Is Poznań worth visiting compared with Kraków or Warsaw?**
It is, as long as you know what you are getting. Kraków is the show-stopper and Warsaw is the capital, and both are busier, better known and more expensive. Poznań is smaller, cheaper and far less touristed — you get a genuinely beautiful Renaissance market square, the birthplace of the Polish state on Cathedral Island, a good food and bar scene and almost no queues. It is not a week-long destination, but as a low-cost two-day city break, or as a stop between Berlin and Warsaw, it is excellent and most British visitors have never considered it.

**How many days do you need in Poznań?**
Two days is the sweet spot, or two nights with a lazy third morning. Day one covers the Old Market Square, the goats at noon, the Town Hall, the Croissant Museum and the bars and restaurants around the square. Day two covers Ostrów Tumski and the cathedral, the Imperial Castle, the Enigma Cipher Centre and Stary Browar, with an afternoon at Lake Malta if the weather is good. The centre is small and walkable, so you genuinely can see the city properly in a weekend without rushing.

**Which area should I stay in Poznań?**
Four areas cover almost every trip. The Old Town around Stary Rynek is best for first-timers — sights, restaurants and walkability in one place. Jeżyce, just west of the centre, is the hip local neighbourhood with independent cafés, bars and a proper market, and it is cheaper than the square. Around Poznań Główny station is the value and transport pick, handy for trains and the airport bus. And the trade-fair grounds (MTP) area suits business travellers who need to be at the halls. If you are here for sightseeing, pick the Old Town or Jeżyce.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Poznań?**
The neighbourhoods just outside the historic core. Around Poznań Główny station, out towards Jeżyce and Grunwald, and over by Lake Malta and the eastern suburbs, rates run noticeably below the Old Market Square while trams and buses put you in the centre in ten to fifteen minutes. Several of the cheapest beds in this guide sit in exactly those bands from around £35 to £50. Poznań is small enough that being a few tram stops out barely costs you time, and the saving over two or three nights is real.

**Is Poznań safe for tourists?**
Very. Poznań is a prosperous university and business city with low levels of crime against visitors, and the areas in this guide are ordinary residential, student or commercial districts that are perfectly fine to stay in. Normal city precautions apply — watch your bag in busy spots around the square and the main station, take licensed taxis or an app, and expect the usual late-night boisterousness in the bar streets at weekends. Nothing about Poznań requires special caution beyond what you would do in any UK city.

**Do you need to speak Polish in Poznań?**
No. English is widely spoken by younger Poles, hotel staff, and in cafés, restaurants and museums in the centre, and menus in tourist areas are usually bilingual. You may hit a language wall in an older neighbourhood shop or with an older taxi driver, but a translation app covers it. Learning dzień dobry (good day), proszę (please) and dziękuję (thank you) goes down very well and takes five minutes. Signage at the airport and the main station is bilingual too.

**Do you tip in Poland?**
Yes, but modestly. Around 10% is the normal tip in a restaurant or a bar with table service if you were happy with the service, and rounding up is fine for a taxi or a coffee. Tipping is not automatic and service is not usually added to the bill, so check before you add. One local quirk worth knowing: if you hand over cash and say thank you as you pay, it can be taken to mean keep the change — so state the amount you want to pay if you are expecting change back.

**Can you drink the tap water in Poznań?**
Yes. Tap water across Poland, including Poznań, is treated to EU standards and is safe to drink. Some locals prefer bottled or filtered water on taste grounds because older buildings can have ageing pipework, but there is no health reason to avoid it. Bringing a refillable bottle saves money and plastic over a weekend, and restaurants will normally serve tap water on request even though bottled is what they will offer you first.

**When is the best time to visit Poznań?**
May to September is the sweet spot — warm weather, long evenings, café tables all over the Old Market Square, and Lake Malta at its best for the toboggan run and the water sports. Spring and early autumn are cheaper and quieter. The single most characterful date in the calendar is 11 November, St Martin's Day, when the city celebrates its protected St Martin's croissant with a street festival, a parade and croissants everywhere. Winter is cold and grey but the Christmas market on the square is pretty and cheap.

**What is a rogal świętomarciński (St Martin's croissant)?**
It is Poznań's own pastry and a genuinely protected regional product — a rich, heavy croissant filled with white poppy seed paste, nuts, raisins and candied fruit, glazed and topped with more nuts. Only certified bakers in the Poznań region are allowed to make and sell the real thing. They are eaten all year but the tradition peaks on 11 November, St Martin's Day, when the city gets through hundreds of tonnes of them. The Poznań Croissant Museum on the Old Market Square runs entertaining live demonstrations, in English at set times.

**What is Ostrów Tumski in Poznań?**
Ostrów Tumski, or Cathedral Island, is where Poland began. This small island in the Warta was the seat of the first Polish rulers, and it holds Poland's oldest cathedral — the Archcathedral Basilica of St Peter and St Paul — with the tombs and memorials of the earliest Piast dynasty. It is a five to ten minute walk or a short tram ride from the Old Market Square, and the Porta Posnania heritage centre next to it explains the whole story clearly in English. For anyone interested in how Poland came to exist, it is the most important spot in the country.

**What is the Enigma Cipher Centre in Poznań?**
It is a modern, hands-on museum telling a story most British visitors have never heard: that three young Polish mathematicians from Poznań University — Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski — first broke the German Enigma cipher in the 1930s, and handed their methods and reconstructed machines to British and French intelligence shortly before the war. That head start fed directly into the work at Bletchley Park. The Centrum Szyfrów Enigma is interactive, well presented in English and genuinely fascinating if you know the Bletchley side of the story.

**What is Lake Malta?**
Lake Malta (Jezioro Maltańskie) is an artificial lake just east of the centre and Poznań's outdoor playground. It has an international-standard regatta course used for rowing and canoeing championships, a year-round toboggan and dry-ski slope on the bank, the large Nowe Zoo, a thermal water park and a little narrow-gauge tourist railway running along the shore. It is a short tram ride from the Old Town and it is where locals go to run, cycle and swim in summer — a good half-day if you are travelling with children.
