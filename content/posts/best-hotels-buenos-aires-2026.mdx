  - q: "When is the best time to visit Buenos Aires?"

The loveliest windows are spring, October and November, when the jacaranda trees flower across Recoleta and Palermo, and autumn, March to May, when the weather is mild and the city is fully back at work. Those two shoulder seasons also tend to be the best value."

  - q: "Which neighbourhood should I stay in in Buenos Aires?"

  - q: "Is Palermo a good place to stay?"
    a: "For most visitors, yes — it is the barrio people mean when they say Buenos Aires feels like a European city with better weather. Palermo Soho is low-rise, tree-lined and packed with restaurants, wine bars and independent shops around Plaza Serrano; Palermo Hollywood next door is where the production companies and the late-night restaurants are. The huge Bosques de Palermo parks, the rose garden and the MALBA art museum are all within walking or short-ride distance. The trade-off is that you are a Subte ride or a longer walk from the historic centre and Plaza de Mayo."
  - q: "Is Recoleta a good base?"
    a: "Recoleta is the most classically elegant part of the city: wide avenues, French-influenced apartment blocks, embassies, cafés and the Recoleta Cemetery with Eva Perón's grave. It is generally considered one of the safest and most pleasant areas to walk in the evening, it is well placed between Palermo and the centre, and it has a good spread of hotels from grand 5★ names down to small budget guesthouses. It is quieter than Palermo at night, which is a plus or a minus depending on what you came for."
  - q: "Is San Telmo a good place to stay and is it safe?"
    a: "San Telmo is the oldest and most atmospheric barrio — cobbled streets, colonial buildings, antique shops, tango in the squares and the big Sunday market along Defensa. It is also the best value for character, with guesthouses and small hotels well below Palermo prices. It is a working, lived-in neighbourhood rather than a polished one, so apply normal city-centre precautions, keep valuables out of sight and take a ride rather than walking long distances late at night. Most visitors have a very good time there."
  - q: "Is Puerto Madero worth staying in?"
    a: "Puerto Madero is the redeveloped dockland strip along the river, and it is where most of the city's modern glass-tower hotels are. It is safe, quiet, spacious and pleasant for an evening walk along the water past the Puente de la Mujer footbridge, with the Costanera Sur nature reserve at the far end. The honest catch is that it is a bit corporate and has less street life than the older barrios — good for a comfortable, low-hassle stay, less good if you want to step out of the door into a neighbourhood."
  - q: "Is the Microcentro a good place to stay?"
    a: "It depends what you want. The Microcentro — the grid around Florida street, Avenida Corrientes and Plaza de Mayo — has the densest cluster of cheap hotels in the city, the best Subte connections, and everything historic within walking distance. The catch is that it is an office district: it is busy and noisy by day and empties out in the evening, which makes some streets feel bleak after dark. If your priority is the lowest price and the shortest commute to the sights, it works. If you want to eat and drink where you sleep, stay in Palermo or San Telmo."
  - q: "Where should I stay in Buenos Aires on a budget?"

Palermo is the priciest of the popular areas, though even there mid-range rates are low by western European standards."
  - q: "How do I see real tango in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "There are two versions and they are not the same thing. Dinner-and-show tango houses are polished, professional and aimed at visitors — they are genuinely good entertainment and worth doing once. Milongas are the social dance halls where locals actually go: you pay a small entry, sit at a table, and watch or dance in a room with its own etiquette and codes. Several run beginner classes before the dancing starts, which is the friendliest way in. You will also see street tango in Plaza Dorrego in San Telmo on a Sunday and around Caminito in La Boca — that is performance for tips, but it is free and it is fun."
  - q: "What time do people eat dinner in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Late. Argentines commonly sit down to dinner at 9pm or 10pm and later still at weekends, and many kitchens do not get busy until well after that. Restaurants that open at 8pm will be almost empty. Plan for a big late lunch, a coffee and a medialuna in the afternoon, and dinner at a properly Argentine hour — or book the earliest slot at a restaurant used to visitors. Sunday lunch, by contrast, is a long and important meal."
  - q: "What should I eat in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Beef, first of all. A parrilla is a grill restaurant, and the ritual is a shared cut cooked over wood or charcoal, chimichurri on the side, and a bottle of Malbec from Mendoza. Beyond steak, look for empanadas, milanesa, provoleta, and the Italian-Argentine cooking that came with a century of immigration — the pizza and pasta here are their own tradition, not an imitation. For breakfast, medialunas with coffee. For anything sweet, dulce de leche, in and on everything. Vegetarians are far better served than the city's reputation suggests, especially in Palermo."
  - q: "Do I need to speak Spanish in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "It helps a lot. English is spoken at the bigger hotels and in tourist-facing restaurants, but far less widely than in western Europe and hardly at all with taxi drivers, in neighbourhood cafés or at ticket windows. Argentine Spanish also sounds different from the Spanish taught in UK schools — vos instead of tú, and a distinctive sh sound where you expect a y. Basic courtesy phrases and an offline translation app will carry you through comfortably, and any attempt at Spanish is received warmly."
  - q: "Is the Subte metro useful, and what about taxis?"
    a: "Yes. The Subte is cheap, fast and covers the routes visitors actually use, running out from the centre along several lines — Line A, opened in 1913, was the first underground railway in Latin America and is worth riding for its own sake. You need a rechargeable travel card, sold and topped up at stations, and the same card works on the buses. It stops running late in the evening, so for nights out most people switch to a radio taxi or an app-based ride, which is also the safer default after dark."
  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Tap water in the city is generally treated and considered drinkable, and many residents drink it straight from the tap. Bottled water is nonetheless very common and cheap, and plenty of visitors stick to it out of habit or because they prefer the taste. If you have a sensitive stomach or you are travelling outside the city into rural Argentina, bottled or filtered is the cautious choice."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Three to four full days covers the city properly: one for the historic centre, Plaza de Mayo, the Casa Rosada and the Ateneo Grand Splendid, one for Recoleta and its cemetery plus the MALBA and the Palermo parks, one for San Telmo and La Boca, and a fourth for tango, a football match or simply eating. Add a fifth if you want a day trip to the Tigre delta or across the river to Colonia in Uruguay. Buenos Aires also rewards doing less — long lunches and café afternoons are the point, not a failure of planning."
  - q: "Is La Boca worth visiting and is it safe?"
    a: "Caminito, the short painted street of corrugated-iron houses in La Boca, is genuinely worth seeing, and La Bombonera stadium is a few blocks away. The honest advice, which locals give as readily as guidebooks do, is to go in daylight, stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito and the stadium, and take a taxi or app ride in and out rather than wandering in on foot from surrounding blocks. Treated that way it is a straightforward and enjoyable half-day."
  - q: "Can I do a day trip from Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Two obvious ones. Tigre, on the Paraná delta about an hour north by train, is a maze of river channels, stilt houses and boat services, and makes an easy half or full day out of the city. Colonia del Sacramento, across the Río de la Plata in Uruguay, is a UNESCO-listed colonial old town reachable by ferry in around an hour on the fast services — remember it is a different country, so take your passport and check entry requirements before you go."
  - q: "Can I visit the Teatro Colón?"
    a: "Yes, and you should. The Teatro Colón is widely rated among the finest opera houses in the world for its acoustics, and it runs guided tours through the horseshoe auditorium, the foyers and the Golden Hall during the day as well as a full opera, ballet and concert programme. Tours are popular and slots are timed, so book ahead rather than turning up. If your dates allow, seeing an actual performance — even from a cheap upper seat — is one of the best-value cultural nights in the city."
  - q: "Can I see a football match in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Buenos Aires is one of the great football cities, and La Bombonera in La Boca and the Monumental in Núñez are its two most famous grounds. Tickets for ordinary league fixtures can be hard for visitors to buy directly because of membership systems, so many people go through an organised hospitality or tour operator, which also handles transport to and from the ground. Stadium tours are available at both when there is no match on and are a good fallback."

Argentine hotel breakfast is usually a modest continental spread — coffee, medialunas, bread, jam, fruit and sometimes eggs — rather than a full buffet, and it is included at many mid-range and even budget properties. At the cheapest central hotels and some apartment-style stays it is either extra or absent. ## What to See in Buenos Aires

Start with the **Recoleta Cemetery**, a walled city of marble mausoleums and cypress alleys in the middle of the smartest barrio, where **Eva Perón's grave** draws a steady queue and the rest of Argentine history is filed away in the surrounding streets of tombs. It is the single most atmospheric hour in the city, and the neighbourhood around it — cafés, galleries, the weekend craft fair on the green — is worth the rest of the afternoon.

**La Boca** and the painted **Caminito** street are the postcard image of Buenos Aires: corrugated-iron houses in primary colours, tango dancers performing for tips, and **La Bombonera**, Boca Juniors' famously steep stadium, a few blocks away. Be honest with yourself about it: go in daylight, stay on the tourist streets around Caminito and the ground, and take a taxi or app ride in and out rather than walking in from surrounding blocks. Done that way it is a good, cheerful half-day.

**San Telmo** is the oldest barrio and the best one to wander: cobbled streets, antique shops, faded grand doorways, and on Sundays a long **antiques market** down Defensa street with tango danced in Plaza Dorrego. Just north, the **Plaza de Mayo** is the political heart of the country, with the pink **Casa Rosada** on one side, the colonial Cabildo on another, and the Metropolitan Cathedral on a third.

The **Teatro Colón** is the cultural set piece — one of the world's great auditoriums, ranked with Vienna and Milan for its acoustics, open for daytime guided tours as well as a full opera, ballet and concert season. Nearby on Avenida Santa Fe, the **Ateneo Grand Splendid** bookshop occupies a converted 1919 theatre, with shelves in the boxes and a café on the stage; it regularly appears on lists of the most beautiful bookshops in the world, and it is free to walk in.

**Puerto Madero**'s waterfront is the modern face of the city: converted brick warehouses, glass towers, the white **Puente de la Mujer** footbridge and the Costanera Sur ecological reserve at the far end, which is the best place in the city for a long flat walk. Out in Palermo, the **MALBA** museum holds the strongest collection of twentieth-century Latin American art in the country, and the **Bosques de Palermo** parks — lakes, a rose garden, a Japanese garden — are where Buenos Aires goes to run, cycle and drink mate on a Sunday.

Then there is football, which is closer to religion here: **La Bombonera** in La Boca and the **Monumental** in Núñez are two of the great grounds of the world game, both offering stadium tours between matches. And above all there is **tango** — polished dinner shows for visitors, and milongas, the social dance halls where locals actually go, several of which run beginner classes before the dancing starts. Pair it with the other national obsession, the **steak-and-Malbec** culture of the parrilla, and you have the two things people fly 7,000 miles for.

## Where to Stay in Buenos Aires: Choosing Your Base

**Palermo** — specifically Palermo Soho and Palermo Hollywood — is the usual first choice and rarely the wrong one. Low-rise, tree-lined and dense with restaurants, wine bars, boutiques and late-night places, with the big parks and MALBA on the doorstep. It is the most fun place to sleep in the city; the trade-off is that the historic centre is a Subte ride away.

**Recoleta** is the elegant option: wide avenues, French-influenced apartment blocks, embassies, the cemetery, and streets that feel comfortable to walk in the evening. It sits neatly between Palermo and the centre, and it has a surprising number of small budget guesthouses hidden between the grand blocks. Quieter than Palermo at night, which suits some people exactly.

**Puerto Madero** is where the modern waterfront hotels are — glass towers, river views, safe and calm, and pleasant for an evening walk along the docks. The honest catch is that it feels a little corporate and has less street life than the older barrios, so you will travel for dinner rather than step out into it.

**San Telmo** gives you the most character per pound: cobbles, antiques, colonial buildings, tango in the squares, the Sunday market, and prices well below Palermo. It is a lived-in, working neighbourhood rather than a polished one, so apply the usual city precautions and take a ride rather than walking long distances late.

**The Microcentro** is the cheapest and best-connected base — the biggest cluster of budget hotels, every Subte line, and Plaza de Mayo, the Obelisco and the Teatro Colón within walking distance. The honest downside is that it is an office district: lively by day, and noticeably quiet and less pleasant at night once the offices empty. Perfect for a short, sightseeing-heavy trip on a tight budget; less good for a week.

## Money in Argentina — What UK Visitors Actually Need to Know

This is the one section of this guide we would ask you not to skim, because Argentina does not behave like most destinations.

Argentina has lived through **years of high inflation**, and at various points it has operated **more than one exchange rate at the same time**. A restaurant price, a taxi fare or a hotel rate quoted in an article — including the pound figures in this guide, which are conversions of live rates pulled while writing — should be read as **indicative only**, not as a quote you can hold anyone to.

## Staying Safe in Buenos Aires — Honest, Practical Advice

Buenos Aires deserves a straight answer rather than either scaremongering or a shrug. Millions of people visit every year without trouble, and by the standards of large Latin American cities it is a comfortable place to walk around. It does, however, have a real petty-crime problem, and visitors who look wealthy and distracted are the obvious targets.

The precautions are the ordinary big-city ones and they work. Use **app-based rides or radio taxis at night** rather than walking unfamiliar streets, especially between neighbourhoods. Keep your **phone out of sight** instead of navigating with it in your hand at a junction — phone snatching is the most common problem tourists report. Carry the card and the cash you need for the day and leave the rest, plus your passport, in the hotel safe. Do not leave a bag on the back of a chair or on the floor of a café. Take particular care around **Retiro** station and the streets behind it, and in **La Boca**, where the standing advice is to visit in daylight and stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito and La Bombonera.

Before you book, read the UK government's current [foreign travel advice for Argentina](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/argentina), which covers safety, entry requirements, health and money, and check it again in the week before you fly. For attractions, festivals, opening hours and travel around the rest of the country, Argentina's official tourism site [Visit Argentina](https://www.argentina.travel/) is the reliable starting point.

The Argentine Peso, written ARS or $. Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds so UK readers can compare like for like, but you will be charged in pesos on the ground. Argentina has had high inflation for years and has at times operated more than one exchange rate, so the pound-to-peso relationship moves far faster than it does for most destinations. Treat any peso price you see online — here or anywhere else — as indicative only, and check the current rate shortly before you travel.

**Why do prices in Argentina change so fast?**
Argentina has lived through sustained high inflation and, at various points, more than one official exchange rate running at the same time. The practical effect for a visitor is that peso prices are revised often, and menus, taxi fares and hotel rates that were accurate a few months ago may not be now. **Which Buenos Aires airport should I fly into?**
Buenos Aires has two. Ezeiza (EZE), properly Ministro Pistarini International Airport, handles long-haul and most international flights and sits roughly 35km south-west of the city — allow around 45 minutes to an hour and a half depending on traffic. Aeroparque (AEP), the Jorge Newbery airfield, sits right on the river a short ride from the centre and handles mostly domestic and regional flights, including some services to Uruguay, Brazil and Chile. If you are connecting between the two, leave generous time — they are on opposite sides of the city.

**How do I get from Ezeiza airport into Buenos Aires?**
The straightforward options are a pre-booked remise or radio taxi from an official desk in the arrivals hall, an app-based ride, or the airport shuttle bus that runs to the city centre. Take the time to use an official counter rather than accepting an offer from someone approaching you in the terminal — that is standard advice at any big international airport and it applies here. Agree or confirm the fare before you set off, and have a small amount of local cash with you in case a driver cannot take cards.

**When is the best time to visit Buenos Aires?**
Argentina is in the southern hemisphere, so the seasons are reversed. Summer runs December to February and is hot and humid, with the city emptying out in January as locals head for the coast. Winter, June to August, is cool and grey but rarely severe. The loveliest windows are spring, October and November, when the jacaranda trees flower across Recoleta and Palermo, and autumn, March to May, when the weather is mild and the city is fully back at work. Those two shoulder seasons also tend to be the best value.

**Is Buenos Aires safe for tourists?**
Buenos Aires is visited by millions of people a year and most trips pass without incident, but it is a big city with a real petty-crime problem, and the sensible precautions are worth taking seriously. Keep your phone out of sight rather than navigating with it in your hand, carry only the card and cash you need, and use app-based rides or radio taxis after dark instead of walking unfamiliar streets. Be particularly alert around Retiro station and its surroundings, and in La Boca, where the advice is to visit in daylight and stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito. Check the UK government's current travel advice for Argentina before you go.

**Which neighbourhood should I stay in in Buenos Aires?**
For a first visit, Palermo or Recoleta. Palermo — especially Palermo Soho and Palermo Hollywood — has the restaurants, the boutiques, the parks and the nightlife, and is the usual first choice. Recoleta is more elegant and residential, with leafy streets, the famous cemetery and an easy walk to the centre. Puerto Madero suits travellers who want modern waterfront hotels and quiet evenings, San Telmo suits those who want bohemian character and lower prices, and the Microcentro is the cheapest and best connected but noticeably quiet and less pleasant after office hours.

**Is Palermo a good place to stay?**
For most visitors, yes — it is the barrio people mean when they say Buenos Aires feels like a European city with better weather. Palermo Soho is low-rise, tree-lined and packed with restaurants, wine bars and independent shops around Plaza Serrano; Palermo Hollywood next door is where the production companies and the late-night restaurants are. The huge Bosques de Palermo parks, the rose garden and the MALBA art museum are all within walking or short-ride distance. The trade-off is that you are a Subte ride or a longer walk from the historic centre and Plaza de Mayo.

**Is Recoleta a good base?**
Recoleta is the most classically elegant part of the city: wide avenues, French-influenced apartment blocks, embassies, cafés and the Recoleta Cemetery with Eva Perón's grave. It is generally considered one of the safest and most pleasant areas to walk in the evening, it is well placed between Palermo and the centre, and it has a good spread of hotels from grand 5★ names down to small budget guesthouses. It is quieter than Palermo at night, which is a plus or a minus depending on what you came for.

**Is San Telmo a good place to stay and is it safe?**
San Telmo is the oldest and most atmospheric barrio — cobbled streets, colonial buildings, antique shops, tango in the squares and the big Sunday market along Defensa. It is also the best value for character, with guesthouses and small hotels well below Palermo prices. It is a working, lived-in neighbourhood rather than a polished one, so apply normal city-centre precautions, keep valuables out of sight and take a ride rather than walking long distances late at night. Most visitors have a very good time there.

**Is Puerto Madero worth staying in?**
Puerto Madero is the redeveloped dockland strip along the river, and it is where most of the city's modern glass-tower hotels are. It is safe, quiet, spacious and pleasant for an evening walk along the water past the Puente de la Mujer footbridge, with the Costanera Sur nature reserve at the far end. The honest catch is that it is a bit corporate and has less street life than the older barrios — good for a comfortable, low-hassle stay, less good if you want to step out of the door into a neighbourhood.

**Is the Microcentro a good place to stay?**
It depends what you want. The Microcentro — the grid around Florida street, Avenida Corrientes and Plaza de Mayo — has the densest cluster of cheap hotels in the city, the best Subte connections, and everything historic within walking distance. The catch is that it is an office district: it is busy and noisy by day and empties out in the evening, which makes some streets feel bleak after dark. If your priority is the lowest price and the shortest commute to the sights, it works. If you want to eat and drink where you sleep, stay in Palermo or San Telmo.

**Where should I stay in Buenos Aires on a budget?**
The Microcentro, Congreso, Monserrat and San Telmo give the best combination of low prices and walkable access to the sights — this is where £21 to £45 a night buys a real hotel room rather than a compromise. Recoleta also has a surprising number of small budget guesthouses tucked between the grand blocks, which is unusual for a neighbourhood that expensive. Palermo is the priciest of the popular areas, though even there mid-range rates are low by western European standards.

**How do I see real tango in Buenos Aires?**
There are two versions and they are not the same thing. Dinner-and-show tango houses are polished, professional and aimed at visitors — they are genuinely good entertainment and worth doing once. Milongas are the social dance halls where locals actually go: you pay a small entry, sit at a table, and watch or dance in a room with its own etiquette and codes. Several run beginner classes before the dancing starts, which is the friendliest way in. You will also see street tango in Plaza Dorrego in San Telmo on a Sunday and around Caminito in La Boca — that is performance for tips, but it is free and it is fun.

**What time do people eat dinner in Buenos Aires?**
Late. Argentines commonly sit down to dinner at 9pm or 10pm and later still at weekends, and many kitchens do not get busy until well after that. Restaurants that open at 8pm will be almost empty. Plan for a big late lunch, a coffee and a medialuna in the afternoon, and dinner at a properly Argentine hour — or book the earliest slot at a restaurant used to visitors. Sunday lunch, by contrast, is a long and important meal.

**What should I eat in Buenos Aires?**
Beef, first of all. A parrilla is a grill restaurant, and the ritual is a shared cut cooked over wood or charcoal, chimichurri on the side, and a bottle of Malbec from Mendoza. Beyond steak, look for empanadas, milanesa, provoleta, and the Italian-Argentine cooking that came with a century of immigration — the pizza and pasta here are their own tradition, not an imitation. For breakfast, medialunas with coffee. For anything sweet, dulce de leche, in and on everything. Vegetarians are far better served than the city's reputation suggests, especially in Palermo.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Buenos Aires?**
It helps a lot. English is spoken at the bigger hotels and in tourist-facing restaurants, but far less widely than in western Europe and hardly at all with taxi drivers, in neighbourhood cafés or at ticket windows. Argentine Spanish also sounds different from the Spanish taught in UK schools — vos instead of tú, and a distinctive sh sound where you expect a y. Basic courtesy phrases and an offline translation app will carry you through comfortably, and any attempt at Spanish is received warmly.

**Is the Subte metro useful, and what about taxis?**
Yes. The Subte is cheap, fast and covers the routes visitors actually use, running out from the centre along several lines — Line A, opened in 1913, was the first underground railway in Latin America and is worth riding for its own sake. You need a rechargeable travel card, sold and topped up at stations, and the same card works on the buses. It stops running late in the evening, so for nights out most people switch to a radio taxi or an app-based ride, which is also the safer default after dark.

**Can I drink the tap water in Buenos Aires?**
Tap water in the city is generally treated and considered drinkable, and many residents drink it straight from the tap. Bottled water is nonetheless very common and cheap, and plenty of visitors stick to it out of habit or because they prefer the taste. If you have a sensitive stomach or you are travelling outside the city into rural Argentina, bottled or filtered is the cautious choice.

**How many days do you need in Buenos Aires?**
Three to four full days covers the city properly: one for the historic centre, Plaza de Mayo, the Casa Rosada and the Ateneo Grand Splendid, one for Recoleta and its cemetery plus the MALBA and the Palermo parks, one for San Telmo and La Boca, and a fourth for tango, a football match or simply eating. Add a fifth if you want a day trip to the Tigre delta or across the river to Colonia in Uruguay. Buenos Aires also rewards doing less — long lunches and café afternoons are the point, not a failure of planning.

**Is La Boca worth visiting and is it safe?**
Caminito, the short painted street of corrugated-iron houses in La Boca, is genuinely worth seeing, and La Bombonera stadium is a few blocks away. The honest advice, which locals give as readily as guidebooks do, is to go in daylight, stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito and the stadium, and take a taxi or app ride in and out rather than wandering in on foot from surrounding blocks. Treated that way it is a straightforward and enjoyable half-day.

**Can I do a day trip from Buenos Aires?**
Two obvious ones. Tigre, on the Paraná delta about an hour north by train, is a maze of river channels, stilt houses and boat services, and makes an easy half or full day out of the city. Colonia del Sacramento, across the Río de la Plata in Uruguay, is a UNESCO-listed colonial old town reachable by ferry in around an hour on the fast services — remember it is a different country, so take your passport and check entry requirements before you go.

**Can I visit the Teatro Colón?**
Yes, and you should. The Teatro Colón is widely rated among the finest opera houses in the world for its acoustics, and it runs guided tours through the horseshoe auditorium, the foyers and the Golden Hall during the day as well as a full opera, ballet and concert programme. Tours are popular and slots are timed, so book ahead rather than turning up. If your dates allow, seeing an actual performance — even from a cheap upper seat — is one of the best-value cultural nights in the city.

**Can I see a football match in Buenos Aires?**
Buenos Aires is one of the great football cities, and La Bombonera in La Boca and the Monumental in Núñez are its two most famous grounds. Tickets for ordinary league fixtures can be hard for visitors to buy directly because of membership systems, so many people go through an organised hospitality or tour operator, which also handles transport to and from the ground. Stadium tours are available at both when there is no match on and are a good fallback.

**Are hotel breakfasts included in Buenos Aires?**
Often, but not always. Argentine hotel breakfast is usually a modest continental spread — coffee, medialunas, bread, jam, fruit and sometimes eggs — rather than a full buffet, and it is included at many mid-range and even budget properties. At the cheapest central hotels and some apartment-style stays it is either extra or absent. Check what is included on the room page before you book; with cafés on almost every corner, skipping it and going out for coffee and medialunas is no hardship.
