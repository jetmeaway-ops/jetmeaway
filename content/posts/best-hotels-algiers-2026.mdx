## What to See in Algiers

The city's centrepiece is the **Casbah of Algiers**, a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the most extraordinary urban landscapes in the Mediterranean — an Ottoman-era citadel quarter stacked up the hillside above the bay in a maze of stepped lanes, arched passages, old palaces, mosques and courtyard houses. It is not a restored tourist quarter. People live in it, parts of it are fragile and in poor repair, and the lanes are unmarked. Go in daylight, dress modestly, and go with a **local guide** — you will be shown things you would never find, and residents respond very differently to a visitor who arrives with a neighbour.

Look at the city from the water or the heights and you understand its nickname: **"Algiers the White" (Alger la Blanche)**, from the tiers of whitewashed buildings rising straight out of the bay. The best places to see it are the terrace at the **Martyrs' Memorial (Maqam Echahid)** — the vast concrete monument of three joined palm fronds commemorating the war of independence, with a museum beneath it — and the cliff-top basilica of **Notre Dame d'Afrique**, north-west of the centre, whose inscription asking Our Lady of Africa to pray for Muslims and Christians alike has made it a symbol of the city's layered history.

For museums, the **Bardo National Museum of Prehistory and Ethnography** occupies an Ottoman villa and covers Algeria's deep past, and the **National Museum of Fine Arts** holds one of the largest art collections in Africa, looking out over the **Jardin d'Essai** — the great 19th-century botanical garden below it, with avenues of palms and dragon trees that are worth an afternoon on their own. Downtown, the **Grande Poste** is the landmark of French colonial Algiers, a white neo-Moorish palace of a post office at the head of the arcaded seafront boulevards, and the surrounding streets around **Didouche Mourad** are the best walking in the modern city.

The essential day trip is **Tipaza**, a second UNESCO site about an hour west along the coast, where a Roman theatre, basilicas and villas sit directly above the Mediterranean — arguably a more beautiful setting than any Roman site in Italy, and usually near-empty. Most visitors go with a driver for the day.

## Choosing Where to Stay in Algiers

- **City centre — Grande Poste and Didouche Mourad** — the default for a first visit. Walkable to the Casbah, the seafront arcades, the cafés and the museums, on the tram and metro lines, and where the cheapest rooms in this guide sit. Busy and noisy, which is the trade-off.
- **Hydra and El Biar** — the leafy heights above the bay, home to embassies and the city's smarter residential streets. Quieter, greener, better air and views, with taxis down into town in minutes. Where to stay if the centre feels too much.
- **Bab Ezzouar and the airport strip** — the business district east of the city beside Houari Boumediene Airport, and where the modern chain hotels are. Practical for work trips, early flights and late arrivals; nothing to do on foot.
- **The seafront and western coast — Chéraga, Zeralda, Sidi Fredj** — sea air, space and beach access, plus a head start on the drive to Tipaza. You commute into the city for everything historic.

## Planning a Trip to Algeria — the Honest Version

Algeria is a **much less-visited destination than Morocco or Tunisia**, and that is both the appeal and the warning. You will not queue for anything, you will rarely see another foreign tourist, and people are genuinely pleased you came — but nothing is set up for you either, and the trip takes real preparation.

**The visa is the big one.** UK visitors need a **visa arranged in advance through an Algerian consulate or embassy**. There is no quick e-visa route for most travellers, and applications typically require supporting documents — a confirmed hotel booking, an itinerary, proof of funds, and in many cases a letter of invitation from a host or a licensed tour operator. It takes time. **Start weeks ahead**, get your hotel confirmation early precisely because you may need it for the application, and confirm the current requirements directly with the consulate rather than relying on a blog post, ours included.

**Carry cash.** Algeria runs on the **Algerian Dinar (DZD)** and card acceptance is limited outside the big international hotels; foreign cards are often refused at ATMs. Budget in cash, change money through official channels, and ask your hotel how they want to be paid before you arrive. Every pound figure in this guide is a rough conversion, not a quote.

**Expect Arabic and French.** Arabic is the official language, Tamazight (Berber) is also official, and French is everywhere in signage, menus and business. **English is not widely spoken** — outside the airport chain hotels you should not count on it. School French goes a long way; an offline translation app goes further.

Finally, and importantly: **UK FCDO advice for Algeria is region-specific**, with advisories against travel to certain border regions and parts of the south. That affects travel insurance validity as well as safety, and it changes. Read the current [UK FCDO travel advice for Algeria](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/algeria) before you book anything, and check it again before you fly.

**Is Algiers safe for tourists?**

**When is the best time to visit Algiers?**
**Spring (April to June) and autumn (September to November)** — warm, bright Mediterranean weather without the summer heat, and much better for the Casbah's steep lanes. **Can I use my card in Algeria?**
Assume not, outside the big international hotels. Carry cash, keep small notes for taxis and entrance fees, and confirm your hotel's payment method in advance. Travellers who arrive with only a card have a bad first day.
