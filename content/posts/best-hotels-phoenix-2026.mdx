If you can genuinely handle the heat, plan your days around early mornings, indoor attractions and a hotel with a good pool, and treat the car as air-conditioned transport between air-conditioned buildings, then July and August in Phoenix are one of the best value luxury deals in the United States. ## The Best-Rated Hotels in Phoenix

These are the sixteen best-rated properties in this guide — six five-star landmarks and ten strong four-stars — and the striking thing about the list is how little the star rating tells you about the price. A five-star here can be £110 a night in August and £400 in March; a four-star can be £58 or £562 depending entirely on what else is happening in the city that week. Phoenix is a **resort town wrapped around a business city**, so rates are driven by conventions, spring training baseball, golf tournaments and the winter snowbird season far more than by the room itself. Read every one of these with your own dates in hand.

## What to Do in Phoenix

- **Desert Botanical Garden** — in Papago Park, and the single best introduction to the Sonoran Desert you can get in a couple of hours. Cactus gardens, wildflower trails in spring, and evening opening in the hot months so you can walk it after sunset.
- **Camelback Mountain and Piestewa Peak** — the two great city hikes, both short, both brutally steep, both giving you the whole Valley from the top. Go at first light, take far more water than you think you need, and in summer do not go at all after mid-morning.
- **South Mountain Park and Preserve** — one of the largest municipal parks in the United States, with miles of trails and a road up to Dobbins Lookout for the best free view of the city at night.
- **The Heard Museum** — a world-class collection of Native American art and history in central Phoenix, and the most rewarding indoor hour in the city when it is 44C outside.
- **The Musical Instrument Museum** — genuinely one of a kind: instruments from every country on earth with headphones that play each one as you approach. It surprises everyone, including people who did not want to go.
- **Roosevelt Row and downtown** — the arts district north of the centre, with murals, galleries, breweries and independent restaurants, plus **Chase Field** for Arizona Diamondbacks baseball and the **Footprint Center** for Phoenix Suns basketball, both walkable from downtown hotels.
- **Old Town Scottsdale** — twenty to thirty minutes east, and the Valley's best walkable evening: galleries, patios, cocktail bars and steakhouses in a low-rise grid. Also the base for **Taliesin West**, Frank Lloyd Wright's desert home and architecture school.
- **Papago Park and Hole-in-the-Rock** — red sandstone buttes right in the middle of the city, with an easy scramble up to a natural window that frames the sunset. Ten minutes from the airport and free.
- **The pool** — not a joke. From June to September a hotel with a serious pool is not a luxury in Phoenix, it is the itinerary. This is why the resorts in this guide discount so hard in summer and why they are still worth booking then.

## Where to Base Yourself in Phoenix

Phoenix is not one place, it is a **valley of about twenty joined-up cities**, and picking the wrong corner of it can add an hour of driving to every day. Here is the honest breakdown.

**Downtown Phoenix.** The compact core: the convention centre, Chase Field, the Footprint Center, Arizona State University's downtown campus, and the Roosevelt Row arts district just north. It is the only part of the metro where a car is genuinely optional, because the **Valley Metro light rail** runs through it and connects to the airport rail station in one direction and Tempe and Mesa in the other. Book here for sport, museums, nightlife and a no-car trip. Do not book here expecting resort facilities.

**The Biltmore and Camelback corridor.** The smart end of the city, running roughly along Camelback Road between 24th Street and Scottsdale: the Arizona Biltmore, Royal Palms, The Camby, the best restaurants in Phoenix proper, and Camelback Mountain itself rising out of the middle of it. This is where most visitors on a mid to high budget should stay, and — as entry 48 shows — there is exactly one cheap way in.

**North Phoenix and the I-17.** Where the budget tier lives: Bell Road, Deer Valley, the motorway corridor. Nothing to walk to, everything easy to drive to, and crucially it is the **launchpad for Sedona, Flagstaff and the Grand Canyon**. If your trip is really an Arizona road trip that starts in Phoenix, sleeping here on the first and last night saves you an hour of city traffic.

**The airport corridor.** Five miles east of downtown, dense with cheap chains, and by far the most sensible place for a first night after a long flight or a last night before an early one. The Sky Train and light rail mean you can even do a short stay here without a car.

**South Phoenix.** Quieter and more spread out, home to the Arizona Grand and The Legacy Golf Resort and backing onto South Mountain Park. Good for golf, families and hiking; a fair drive from the nightlife.

**Scottsdale.** A separate city immediately east, twenty to thirty minutes from downtown Phoenix, and the Valley's resort-and-spa heartland. Old Town is the best walkable evening in the metro. It is also expensive and has almost no budget accommodation, which is why this guide sends you to sleep in Phoenix and drive over for dinner.

**Tempe and the east Valley.** Tempe is fifteen minutes south-east, built around Arizona State University, cheap to eat in and lively at night, and it is on the light rail. ## Honest Practicalities for Phoenix

**The heat is the whole story, and it is not a joke.** From roughly June to September, daytime highs sit at or above 40C, with 43C to 46C routine in July. Resorts that are unaffordable in February and March are startlingly cheap in July and August, and the cheap end gets cheaper still. If you can structure your days around early mornings, indoor attractions, the pool and the drive up to cooler Sedona or Flagstaff, a summer trip here buys a standard of hotel that would be out of reach almost anywhere else. Just go in knowing exactly what you are trading.

**Driving.** You will almost certainly want a car, and Phoenix is one of the easiest American cities to drive in: wide roads, a grid layout, cheap fuel and generally free parking outside downtown. Americans drive on the right, a UK photocard licence is normally sufficient alongside your passport, and the insurance sold at the rental desk is expensive — check what your own policy or credit card already covers. Watch for right turns on a red light, which are legal unless a sign says otherwise, and for the sheer distances: forty minutes across the Valley is an ordinary journey here.

**Safety and sense.** Greater Phoenix is a large American city with ordinary big-city rules. ## Phoenix Hotels FAQs

**Which area of Phoenix should I stay in?**

Phoenix is not a compact European city — it is a sprawling desert metropolis spread across the Valley of the Sun, and where you sleep changes your holiday completely. Downtown Phoenix suits sport, museums, nightlife and anyone arriving without a car, because it sits on the light rail line and is only about five miles from Sky Harbor airport. The Biltmore and Camelback corridor is the smart, leafy end of the city, with the best restaurants and the classic resorts. North Phoenix, along the Interstate 17 and Bell Road, is where the cheapest chain motels cluster and where you want to be if you are driving to Sedona or the Grand Canyon. The airport strip is the most convenient for a one-night stopover. Old Town Scottsdale, twenty to thirty minutes east, is the polished resort-and-restaurant district, and Tempe, fifteen minutes south-east, is the student town with the cheapest food and the liveliest weeknights. For a first visit with a hire car, the Camelback corridor is the easy answer; for a first visit without one, stay downtown.

**Is Scottsdale part of Phoenix, and should I stay there instead?**

Scottsdale is a separate city, not a district of Phoenix, but it shares the same metropolitan sprawl and most visitors treat the two as one destination. It sits immediately east of Phoenix, about twenty to thirty minutes from downtown by car depending on traffic, and it is where a large share of the Valley's resorts, spas, golf courses and expensive restaurants are. It is greener, tidier and considerably more expensive than most of Phoenix, and it has very little genuinely cheap accommodation. The hotels in this guide are Phoenix properties, which is exactly why the budget tier here starts at around £40 a night rather than three times that. A sensible compromise for many trips is to sleep in Phoenix, on the Camelback corridor or out in north Phoenix, and drive into Old Town Scottsdale for the evenings.

**What is Old Town Scottsdale?**

Old Town is Scottsdale's walkable centre — a grid of low-rise blocks with art galleries, western-themed shopfronts, cocktail bars, patio restaurants and the Valley's densest concentration of nightlife. It is the one part of Greater Phoenix that genuinely works on foot for an evening, which is why so many visitors base themselves nearby. It is also where the Scottsdale arts district, the seasonal farmers market and a run of well-known steakhouses and taco bars sit. If you are staying in Phoenix rather than Scottsdale, budget for a taxi or rideshare each way, because it is a twenty to thirty minute drive from most of the hotels in this guide and there is no direct rail link.

**Should I stay in Tempe?**

Tempe is worth considering, particularly if you want cheap food and a walkable evening. It sits between Phoenix and Mesa, about fifteen minutes south-east of downtown Phoenix, and it is dominated by Arizona State University, which means an enormous student population, Mill Avenue full of bars and restaurants at student prices, and Tempe Town Lake for running and paddling. Critically, it is on the Valley Metro light rail line, so you can reach downtown Phoenix and the airport rail station without a car. It is livelier and younger than most of Phoenix, which is a selling point or a warning depending on who you are.

**How hot does Phoenix actually get in summer?**

Hotter than most British visitors are prepared for. From roughly June to September daytime highs sit at or above 40C, and stretches of 43C to 46C are routine in July. In 2023 the city recorded 31 consecutive days at or above 110F, which is 43C, and overnight lows in the worst of it often fail to drop below 30C, so there is no cool evening to recover in. This is dry desert heat rather than humid heat, which makes it more bearable in the shade than the numbers suggest, but it is genuinely dangerous if you treat it casually. Outdoor plans in summer happen before about nine in the morning or after sunset, and everything in between happens indoors, in air conditioning, or in a pool.

**Why are Phoenix hotels so cheap in July and August?**

Because almost nobody wants to be there. Phoenix runs on an inverted seasonal calendar compared with most holiday destinations: winter is peak and summer is the bargain basement. When daytime temperatures sit above 43C, the resorts that charge a fortune in February discount hard to fill rooms, and the same is true right down the market. If you can genuinely handle the heat, plan your days around early mornings, indoor attractions and a hotel with a good pool, and treat the car as air-conditioned transport between air-conditioned buildings, then July and August in Phoenix are one of the best value luxury deals in the United States. If you cannot, do not book it — no discount is worth a miserable week.

**When is the best time to visit Phoenix?**

For comfort, roughly November to April, when daytime highs sit in the pleasant high teens to mid twenties Celsius and the sun is reliable — this is why the city fills with North American snowbirds every winter and why hotel prices peak then. March is the busiest and most expensive month of all, because it combines perfect weather with baseball spring training and a run of major golf and motoring events. October and early November, plus late April and May, are the sweet spot: still warm, noticeably cheaper, and free of the extreme heat. June to September is the cheap, brutally hot season, and July to September also brings the monsoon, with dramatic afternoon thunderstorms and occasional dust storms.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Phoenix?**

Yes, though the schedule is not as dense as for Los Angeles or Las Vegas. British Airways has operated London Heathrow to Phoenix Sky Harbor non-stop, and American Airlines has also served the route, but frequencies change with the season and airlines adjust long-haul schedules regularly, so check live availability for your own dates rather than assuming. If there is no direct service when you want to travel, the one-stop options are extensive: American through Dallas Fort Worth, Chicago or Charlotte, Delta through Atlanta, Detroit or Minneapolis, and United through Newark, Houston or Chicago. A connection usually costs less and adds three to five hours to the day.

**How long is the flight from the UK to Phoenix?**

Around ten to eleven hours non-stop westbound from London, and a little less coming home, since the jet stream helps on the return. With a single connection through an American hub, expect roughly thirteen to sixteen hours door to door depending on the layover. Arizona does not observe daylight saving time, which means Phoenix is eight hours behind the UK during British Summer Time and seven hours behind in winter — an unusual detail that catches people out when they book connecting flights or ring home.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for the USA?**

Most UK passport holders travel under the Visa Waiver Program, which requires an approved ESTA rather than a full visa. You apply online before you fly, pay a fee, and approval is usually quick but is neither guaranteed nor instant in every case, so do it as soon as you have booked rather than the night before you travel. An ESTA is normally valid for two years or until your passport expires, covers stays of up to 90 days, and does not apply to everyone — certain travel histories and dual nationalities require a full visa instead. Check the current rules on the UK government travel advice pages for the United States before you book anything.

**How do I get from Sky Harbor airport (PHX) into Phoenix?**

Sky Harbor is unusually close to the city it serves — about five miles east of downtown Phoenix, which is a ten to fifteen minute drive in normal traffic. The free PHX Sky Train connects all terminals to the Rental Car Center and to the 44th Street station on the Valley Metro light rail, from where trains run into downtown Phoenix and out through Tempe to Mesa for a couple of dollars. Taxis and rideshare pick up from designated areas at each terminal and are quick and reasonably priced for the distance. If you are hiring a car, the Rental Car Center is a separate building reached by the Sky Train, so allow an extra twenty minutes on top of your arrival time.

**Do I need a hire car in Phoenix?**

For most trips, yes. Greater Phoenix covers a vast area of low-rise sprawl, the attractions are spread from South Mountain to Desert Ridge and out to Scottsdale, and in summer walking any distance between them is not a realistic option. A car also unlocks the reason many people come at all — Sedona, the Grand Canyon, the Superstition Mountains and the saguaro country south of the city. Petrol is far cheaper than in the UK, roads are wide and easy, and hotel parking outside downtown is often free. The exceptions are a short city break based downtown or in Tempe using the light rail, or a summer trip where you intend to stay by the pool. If you do hire, remember Americans drive on the right, that the insurance sold at the desk is expensive, and that a UK photocard licence plus your passport is normally all you need.

**Is there public transport in Phoenix?**

There is, and it is better than the city's reputation suggests, but it only covers one corridor properly. The Valley Metro light rail runs roughly north to south through central Phoenix and then east through Tempe to Mesa, linking downtown, midtown, the airport rail station, Arizona State University and a string of neighbourhoods, and it is cheap and easy to use. There is also an extensive bus network. What none of it does well is reach Scottsdale, north Phoenix, the resorts or the trailheads, which is where most visitors want to go. Treat the light rail as genuinely useful if your hotel sits on it, and as irrelevant if it does not.

**What are resort fees and will I have to pay them?**

Resort fees, sometimes called destination or amenity fees, are a mandatory daily charge that many American hotels add on top of the room rate, usually collected at the property rather than at the time of booking. They are common at Phoenix and Scottsdale resorts and can add a meaningful amount per night, nominally covering things like wifi, pool towels, fitness rooms and local calls whether you use them or not. Parking is often charged separately again. Budget motels and most limited-service chains do not charge them. Always read the fee section of the room description before you book, and factor the fee plus parking into any comparison between a resort and a city hotel — it can close a surprisingly large gap.

**What currency does Phoenix use and should I pay in dollars or pounds?**

The US Dollar. Cards are accepted almost everywhere and contactless is normal, so you need very little cash beyond small notes for tips. If a card machine or a hotel offers to bill you in pounds rather than dollars, decline it — that is dynamic currency conversion and the rate is almost always worse than letting your own bank do the conversion. Watch for the American habit of quoting prices before tax: the number on the menu or the shelf edge is not the number on the bill.

**How much should I tip in Phoenix?**

Tipping is not optional in the United States in the way it is in Britain, because service staff wages assume it. Around 18 to 20 percent is standard in restaurants with table service, and card terminals will usually suggest amounts. At a bar, a dollar or two per drink. For housekeeping, a few dollars per night left in the room. For taxis and rideshare, roughly 15 to 20 percent. A dollar or two per bag for porters, and the same for valet parking. Check whether a service charge has already been added for larger groups so that you do not pay twice.

**Is Phoenix safe for tourists?**

Greater Phoenix is a large American city and the usual big-city rules apply rather than anything exotic. The tourist areas — downtown, the Biltmore and Camelback corridor, Old Town Scottsdale, Tempe and the resort districts — are busy and generally trouble free. As anywhere in the United States, quality varies sharply street by street rather than district by district, so at the very cheap end of the market read the most recent reviews carefully and check exactly where the property sits on a map. Do not leave anything visible in a parked car, particularly at trailheads. The single biggest genuine risk to a visitor here is not crime, it is the heat: people are hospitalised every summer for underestimating a midday hike. Read the current UK government travel advice for the United States before you go.

**Can I do the Grand Canyon as a day trip from Phoenix?**

You can, but be honest with yourself about what it involves. The South Rim is roughly 230 miles north of Phoenix, about three and a half to four hours each way by car, so a self-drive day trip means eight hours of driving for perhaps four hours at the canyon. Coach tours do exactly that and leave before dawn. It is far better as an overnight: drive up, stay in Tusayan, Williams or Flagstaff, see the rim at sunset and again at sunrise, and come back the next day. Many people combine it with Sedona on the way, which turns it into a very good two or three day loop.

**How far is Sedona from Phoenix?**

About 115 miles north, which is a two hour drive up the Interstate 17 and then across on the 179 — one of the easiest and most rewarding day trips in the American south-west. Sedona sits among red sandstone buttes at around 1,300 metres, so it is meaningfully cooler than Phoenix, which makes it a genuine escape in summer as well as a beautiful one in winter. Cathedral Rock, Bell Rock, the Chapel of the Holy Cross and Oak Creek Canyon are the standard stops. Go on a weekday if you can, because the town and its car parks fill badly at weekends.

**What else is worth a day trip from Phoenix?**

Plenty, and this is one of the strongest arguments for hiring a car. The Superstition Mountains and the Apache Trail are an hour east and give you classic desert scenery without the drive to Sedona. Montezuma Castle, a cliff dwelling beside the motorway near Camp Verde, is an easy stop on the way north. Jerome, a former mining town clinging to a hillside, and Prescott, with its old western square, pair well together. Tucson and the giant saguaro forests of Saguaro National Park are around two hours south. And Page, for Antelope Canyon and Horseshoe Bend, is four and a half hours or so north and really needs an overnight.

**What is there to do in Phoenix itself?**

More than its reputation as a stopover suggests. The Desert Botanical Garden in Papago Park is the single best introduction to the Sonoran Desert. The Heard Museum holds one of the finest collections of Native American art anywhere. The Musical Instrument Museum is genuinely world class and surprises almost everyone who goes. Camelback Mountain and Piestewa Peak give you hard, hot, spectacular city hikes, and South Mountain Park is one of the largest municipal parks in the United States. Downtown has Chase Field for baseball, the Footprint Center for basketball, and the Roosevelt Row arts district for murals, galleries and bars. Add Taliesin West, Frank Lloyd Wright's desert home and school over in Scottsdale, and you have several full days without ever leaving the Valley.

**Is Phoenix good for families?**

Yes, with the right base and the right month. The big resorts here are built for families in a way European hotels rarely are — water parks, lazy rivers, kids clubs and multiple pools are standard rather than exceptional, and the Arizona Grand Resort in particular is close to being a water park with rooms attached. The city has the Arizona Science Center, the Phoenix Zoo, an aquarium out in Scottsdale and the Botanical Garden, and the day trips are exactly the sort of scenery children remember. The caveats are the heat, which rules out midday outdoor plans for a large part of the year, and the driving, which means car seats and a lot of time in the car.

**Is Phoenix good for golf?**

It is one of the great golf destinations in the world, with well over 150 courses across the Valley and reliable sunshine for most of the year. Scottsdale has the famous names, and Phoenix itself has plenty of resort and municipal courses, including the courses attached to several hotels in this guide. Green fees follow the same inverted calendar as the hotels: highest from January to April, and heavily discounted in the summer heat when tee times start at dawn. Book early for the winter months, and particularly around the big professional tournament that fills Scottsdale every February.

**What is spring training and how does it affect hotel prices?**

Every February and March, fifteen Major League Baseball teams hold their pre-season training in the Phoenix area, in a set of stadiums collectively called the Cactus League, and tens of thousands of American fans follow them out. It is a big, cheerful, very American event and the games are relaxed and affordable to attend. It is also a large part of the reason March is the most expensive month to book a hotel in this city. If you are travelling on a budget and do not care about baseball, avoid late February and March entirely; if you love it, book your accommodation months ahead.

**How many days do you need in Phoenix?**

Three or four nights covers the city itself comfortably — a desert garden and museum day, a mountain hike, an evening in Old Town Scottsdale and a day by the pool. Five to seven turns it into a proper trip with Sedona and an overnight at the Grand Canyon built in. Two nights is enough only if Phoenix is a stopover on a wider American south-west route, which is how a great many UK visitors use it, pairing it with Las Vegas, Los Angeles or a national parks loop.

**What should I pack for Phoenix?**

Light, loose, covering clothes rather than as little as possible, because the sun is fierce and shade is scarce. A wide-brimmed hat, proper sunglasses, high-factor sunscreen and a large refillable water bottle are essentials rather than nice-to-haves. Bring trainers or walking shoes with grip if you intend to climb Camelback or Piestewa Peak, because both are steeper and rockier than they look in photographs. Pack a fleece or light jacket for winter evenings, which get genuinely cool once the sun drops, and for the Grand Canyon and Sedona, which are much higher and colder than Phoenix.
