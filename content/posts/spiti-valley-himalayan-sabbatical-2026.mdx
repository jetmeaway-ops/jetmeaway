  - q: "How serious is altitude sickness in Spiti, and what should UK travellers do?"
    a: "Very serious. Spiti's main hub **Kaza sits at 3,800m**, and you'll be sleeping at 4,200m in **Kibber** and 4,400m at **Komic**. Above 3,500m, **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)** is a real risk for anyone who flies in from sea-level London and rushes the climb. Symptoms: persistent headache, nausea, breathlessness at rest, disturbed sleep. The protocol that works: enter via Shimla and Kinnaur (a 2-day slow drive that lets your body acclimatise), spend the **first 48 hours in Kaza doing absolutely nothing**, drink 4 litres of water daily, and carry **Diamox (acetazolamide)** prescribed by your UK GP before departure. If symptoms get severe — confusion, ataxia, blue lips — descend immediately. The mountain is not a place to be brave."
  - q: "When is Spiti Valley open, and what's the best window to fly out?"
    a: "Spiti has **two completely different travel seasons**. The summer window — **May to early October** — is when the Manali-to-Kaza road via Rohtang and Kunzum Pass is open and the full loop is drivable. This is when 90% of UK travellers go. The winter window — **December to March** — is for serious adventurers only: roads close, temperatures drop to -25°C, the only access is via the Shimla-Kinnaur road in a 4x4. The sweet spot for first-timers is **June through mid-September** — clear skies, monasteries running full prayer schedules, all 14 days of the loop drivable. Avoid **mid-July to mid-August** (monsoon landslides on the Kinnaur road) and any week with heavy rain forecasts in Himachal."
  - q: "Do I need a permit to visit Spiti Valley?"
    a: "**Indian nationals don't.** UK passport holders technically need an **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** for the Kinnaur-Spiti circuit, particularly for the stretch from Reckong Peo to Kaza which passes near the Tibet border. Your hotel or driver in Shimla or Reckong Peo can arrange this in a single morning — it costs around ₹250 (~£2.50) and is valid for 7 days, extendable. Don't skip it. The Sumdo and Kaza checkposts will turn you back without one. **The Himalayan Brothers** in Kaza routinely handle permits for foreign guests as part of the booking — confirm this in writing when you reserve."
  - q: "What exactly is the Inner Line Permit and how is it different for Indian vs foreign nationals?"
    a: "The ILP is a colonial-era permit system, still enforced, that regulates travel near India's international borders. For **Indian nationals**, most of the Spiti loop requires no permit at all — only the stretch beyond Kaza toward the Tibet border (Kaurik, Chango) needs a simple ILP obtainable on the spot. For **foreign nationals (including UK passport holders)**, the requirement is stricter: an ILP is needed for the Kinnaur-Spiti stretch from Recong Peo onward, must be applied for as a group of a minimum of two people (solo foreign travellers typically need to pair with another traveller or book through an agency that handles this), and is checked at both the Sumdo and Jangi checkposts. Processing takes under an hour at the SDM office in Recong Peo or Kaza with passport copies, visa copies and passport photos — but arrive before the office's early afternoon cutoff, as weekend and holiday processing is unreliable."
  - q: "How do I actually get to Spiti from London?"
    a: "It's a 5-leg journey, and that's by design — the slow climb is what saves you from altitude sickness. **Leg 1**: London Heathrow → Delhi (DEL) on BA or Air India, ~9 hours direct. **Leg 2**: Delhi → Shimla by overnight Kalka Mail train + the iconic Kalka-Shimla narrow-gauge toy train (UNESCO heritage), or a 7-hour private car. **Leg 3**: Shimla → Reckong Peo by 4x4 (~10 hours, mountain roads). **Leg 4**: Reckong Peo → Tabo → Kaza (~6 hours, your altitude gradually rising). **Leg 5**: Exit via Kaza → Manali across the **Kunzum + Rohtang passes** (~12 hours of mountain drama), then Manali → Delhi by overnight Volvo bus. Total London-to-Kaza-and-back: 16 days minimum. **Never** hire a self-drive car for this loop — the roads are genuinely dangerous and your travel insurance won't cover a self-drive accident in Spiti."
  - q: "Should I enter via Manali or via Shimla-Kinnaur?"
    a: "**Shimla-Kinnaur is the medically correct answer for a first-timer.** It's a gradual, staged climb — Shimla (2,200m) to Reckong Peo (2,300m) to Tabo (3,280m) to Kaza (3,800m) over 3-4 days — that lets your body acclimatise in stages rather than in one brutal jump. The **Manali route**, by contrast, crosses Rohtang Pass (3,978m) and Kunzum Pass (4,590m) in a single day from Manali (2,050m), a near-3,000m altitude gain in hours, which is exactly the kind of rapid ascent that triggers AMS. The smart 2026 pattern UK travellers use: **enter via Shimla-Kinnaur (slow, safe), exit via Manali (fast, dramatic, and you're already acclimatised so the altitude swing matters less on the way down)**. Doing it the other way around — Manali in, Shimla out — is where most altitude sickness cases originate."
  - q: "What's the WiFi situation, and can I work from Spiti?"
    a: "Mostly no. **Kaza** has patchy 4G via Jio and Airtel, and a handful of café-and-hotel WiFi connections that work for messaging and email. Outside Kaza — in **Kibber, Langza, Komic, Hikkim, Tabo, Dhankar** — you are in a near-total **dead zone**. This is the point. The Spiti sabbatical is genuinely a digital-detox window, and most UK travellers come back saying that was the most valuable part of the trip. If you absolutely need to work, base yourself in Kaza for the full 14 days, choose **The Himalayan Brothers** or **Spiti Heritage Hotel** for their reliable in-room WiFi, and accept that any video call is a 50/50 gamble."

  - q: "Do I need to book a homestay in advance, or can I turn up in Kibber and Langza?"
    a: "Book ahead, especially July-September. There are fewer than 400 rooms across the entire Spiti Valley, and homestays in Kibber, Langza and Komic typically run 3-6 rooms per family. During peak season a walk-in traveller can genuinely be turned away with nowhere else to sleep in the village. The Spiti homestay network is informal — no big booking platform lists most of them individually — so the reliable route is to have your Kaza-based hotel (The Himalayan Brothers or Spiti Heritage Hotel) pre-arrange the homestay leg as part of your booking, which is standard practice and costs nothing extra. Confirm homestay nights in writing at least 4-6 weeks before you travel."
  - q: "What is Key Monastery and why is it the centrepiece of the trip?"
    a: "Key (also spelled Ki or Kye) Monastery is a roughly 1,000-year-old Tibetan Buddhist fortress-monastery built directly into a conical hill above the Spiti River, home to around 250 resident lamas from the Gelugpa (Yellow Hat) sect. It's the largest and oldest monastery in the Spiti Valley, and its architecture — whitewashed walls stacked in irregular tiers up the hillside — is the single most photographed structure in the region. The real reason to prioritise it: the **6:30am morning prayer session**, when the monastery's inner sanctum fills with chanting, horns and cymbals, and visitors are generally welcome to sit quietly at the back. A tour-bus afternoon visit sees an empty shell of a building; the morning prayer session is the actual experience."
  - q: "What is Kaza and how much time should I spend there?"
    a: "Kaza (3,800m) is Spiti's de facto capital — the only town with a proper bazaar, a hospital, ATMs (unreliable), fuel, and the widest range of accommodation and restaurants in the valley. Plan for at least 5-6 nights in or around Kaza across the trip: 2-3 nights on arrival to acclimatise, then use it as your base for day trips to Key, Kibber, Langza and Komic, before a final 2 nights before the exit drive. Kaza is not scenic in itself — it's a functional mountain town — but it's the only place in Spiti where you can reliably get a hot shower, a phone signal and a proper meal."
  - q: "What is Kibber, and why do people rave about the homestay there?"
    a: "Kibber sits at 4,200m and was, until relatively recently, considered one of the highest permanently inhabited villages in the world with a motorable road. It's a working agro-pastoral village — barley terraces, yak and dzo herds, and a genuine wildlife corridor for the elusive snow leopard in winter. The homestay experience here — a family's spare room, shared meals of thukpa and tsampa, conversations by a wood stove — is consistently what UK travellers cite as the most memorable part of the entire Spiti trip, more than any monastery. Budget 2-3 nights; one night barely scratches the surface."
  - q: "What is Langza, and what are the fossils everyone talks about?"
    a: "Langza (4,400m) is a small village dominated by a massive golden Buddha statue overlooking a valley of ochre-and-white hills. Those hills were, roughly 100-160 million years ago, the seabed of the Tethys Sea — the ocean that existed before the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates collided and pushed the Himalayas up. As a result, Langza's hillsides are scattered with genuine marine fossils, mostly ammonites, easily found on a short walk. Local children sell fossils in the village square, but picking up loose fossils yourself (not buying) is technically removing material from a protected geological area — admire, photograph, and leave them where they lie."
  - q: "What is Hikkim, and is the 'world's highest post office' claim actually true?"
    a: "Hikkim (4,440m) hosts what is widely marketed as the world's highest post office — a small building where you can buy a postcard, get it hand-stamped, and post it home. The 'highest in the world' claim has been debated by rival claimants in Bolivia and elsewhere over the years, but Hikkim's post office is unquestionably among the highest functioning post offices anywhere, and it remains one of the most-loved, most-photographed stops on the entire Spiti circuit. Postcards typically take 2-6 weeks to reach the UK. It's a cliché. Do it anyway."
  - q: "What is Komic, and is it worth the detour?"
    a: "Komic (4,587m/4,513m depending on the survey) is regularly billed as the highest motorable village in the world with a functioning monastery. It's a tiny settlement — a handful of houses, a small 14th-century monastery, and views across the barren cold-desert plateau that feel genuinely lunar. It's a short 15-20 minute drive from Hikkim and Langza, so most itineraries combine all three villages in a single day trip from Kaza or Kibber. Worth the detour: yes, specifically for the sense of scale — standing at nearly 4,600m and seeing nothing but rock, sky and prayer flags resets your sense of what 'remote' means."
  - q: "What is Tabo, and why do people call it the 'Ajanta of the Himalayas'?"
    a: "Tabo Monastery, founded in 996 CE, is one of the oldest continuously operating Buddhist monasteries in India and the Himalayas, predating most of the famous monasteries in Ladakh and Tibet. Its mud-and-timber prayer halls house wall paintings, stucco sculptures and manuscripts over 1,000 years old, still remarkably preserved in Spiti's dry cold-desert climate — hence the comparison to the Ajanta Caves' ancient Buddhist murals in Maharashtra. Tabo sits at a relatively gentle 3,280m, lower than Kaza, which makes it the smart first overnight stop on the Shimla-Kinnaur approach — you gain altitude gradually while visiting one of the most important Buddhist heritage sites in the country."
  - q: "What is Dhankar Monastery, and why is it structurally at risk?"
    a: "Dhankar, once the historical capital of the Spiti kingdom, is a monastery perched on a dramatic knife-edge ridge between the confluence of the Spiti and Pin rivers, roughly 300m above the valley floor. The site's precarious position — genuinely spectacular to look at — is also its liability: erosion, seismic activity and the sheer age of the mud-brick structure have put it on conservation watch lists, and portions have periodically been closed to visitors for safety. Visit it while it's still open to the public; a newer monastery has been built nearby as the ageing structure's long-term future is uncertain."
  - q: "What is Chandratal Lake, and can UK travellers visit it easily?"
    a: "Chandratal ('Moon Lake') is a high-altitude glacial lake at 4,300m near the Kunzum Pass, on the Kaza-to-Manali exit route, famous for its crescent shape and colour-shifting turquoise-to-deep-blue water through the day. It's one of the most photographed spots on the entire Spiti loop and is straightforward to visit if you're already driving the Kaza-Manali exit — most itineraries build in a 1-2 hour stop or a night of basic camping near the lake (permanent structures are banned within the protected area, so accommodation is seasonal tented camps only, typically open June to early October). The final stretch to the lake itself is a roughly 2km walk from the road-head car park, at altitude, so pace yourself."
  - q: "What is a Spiti homestay circuit and how does it differ from staying in hotels?"
    a: "The homestay circuit refers to spending nights in family-run village homes — most commonly in Kibber, Langza, Komic, Demul or Dhankar village — rather than hotels in Kaza. You get a basic private or shared room, home-cooked meals (thukpa, tsampa, momos, butter tea) taken with the family, and often an evening of conversation that's the emotional core of many travellers' trips. It differs from a hotel stay in every practical way: no en-suite bathroom in most cases (shared, sometimes outdoor, facilities), no WiFi, solar-only power, and the pace of the household rather than a service schedule. Most UK travellers do a mixed circuit — hotel nights in Kaza bookending several homestay nights in the villages — rather than committing the whole trip to one or the other."
  - q: "What should I eat in Spiti, and is the food safe for UK travellers?"
    a: "Spitian food is simple, hearty, high-carbohydrate cold-climate cuisine: thukpa (noodle soup), momos (steamed dumplings), tsampa (roasted barley flour, often mixed with butter tea), thenthuk (hand-pulled noodle soup) and butter tea (salted, an acquired taste most first-timers need a few days to warm to). It's almost entirely vegetarian in homestays and small villages — meat is rare and expensive to transport at altitude, so don't expect much beyond Kaza's bazaar restaurants. Food safety is generally good if you stick to hot, freshly cooked meals and avoid raw salads or untreated water; homestay food, cooked to order in front of you, is typically safer than bazaar street food. Carry water purification tablets or a filter bottle as backup."

The genuine risks in Spiti are environmental, not criminal: altitude sickness, road accidents on mountain switchbacks, and being caught out by weather or a closed pass. Solo women travellers do the loop regularly and safely; the main practical friction is the foreign-national Inner Line Permit, which in some interpretations requires a minimum of two travellers on the same permit application — check current rules with your driver or agency, and be prepared to pair up with another solo traveller at the Reckong Peo checkpost if needed."

Self-drive hire cars exist in Manali, but most UK travel insurance policies explicitly exclude cover for self-drive accidents on mountain roads like these, and breakdown recovery in remote Spiti can take a full day or more. ## 1. The altitude reset: life above the clouds

In Spiti you are living at the same altitude as the **Matterhorn's summit**. Kaza, the main town, sits at 3,800m. Kibber village is at 4,200m. Komic — one of the world's highest motorable villages — is at 4,587m. The post office at Hikkim is the highest in the world at 4,440m.

**The slow rule.** You cannot rush Spiti. The first 48 hours are a mandatory do-nothing window for acclimatisation. Sleep, drink water, walk slowly, eat soup. Anyone who arrives in Kaza on a 7-day loop and tries to hit Key Monastery on Day 1 ends up vomiting at 4,000m. Build in the buffer.

**The vibe.** Tibetan Buddhist culture, lunar landscapes scoured by 200,000 years of glacial wind, prayer flags strung between mud-brick chortens, the world's highest inhabited villages. This is a **cold desert**, where the air is dry enough that your skin cracks within 24 hours and the silence is so profound it's physical. You hear your own heartbeat at night.

**Why "cold desert" and not "mountains"?** Spiti sits in the rain shadow of the Himalayan main range — the Pir Panjal and Great Himalayan ranges to the south and west intercept the monsoon before it reaches the valley, so Spiti receives a fraction of the rainfall that soaks Shimla or Manali just a few hundred kilometres away. The result is a genuinely arid, high-altitude desert landscape: bare ochre and grey rock, minimal vegetation outside the irrigated river-valley floor, and a stark clarity of light that photographers chase for years without quite capturing. It's this rain-shadow geography, not just the altitude, that makes Spiti visually and psychologically unlike anywhere else in the Indian Himalayas.

## 3. Village by village: what each stop actually delivers

A Spiti loop is really a chain of very different micro-destinations, each worth a specific amount of your limited time. Here's what to prioritise.

**Key Monastery.** The 1,000-year-old fortress-style monastery, hand-built into the cliff, home to around 250 lamas. Visit during **morning prayers** (6:30am) — the chanting fills the inner sanctum and the acoustic experience is genuinely life-altering. Don't visit on a tour bus afternoon slot; the building is a shell without the ritual happening inside it.

**Kaza.** The valley's only proper town — bazaar, ATMs (unreliable), a small hospital, and the widest choice of restaurants and hotels. Not scenic in itself, but the essential logistics hub: this is where you acclimatise, resupply, arrange permits, and return to for a hot shower between homestay legs.

**Kibber.** At 4,200m, one of the highest villages in the world with a motorable road — barley terraces, yak and dzo herds, and (in winter) a snow leopard corridor. The homestay circuit's emotional centre. Budget 2-3 nights minimum; one night is a taster, not the experience.

**Langza.** A massive 1,000-year-old golden Buddha overlooking a valley filled with **prehistoric marine fossils** — you are standing on what was once the bottom of the Tethys Sea, 100+ million years ago. Local children sell ammonite fossils at the village square. Don't pick up loose fossils yourself: it's a protected geological site, and it's meant to stay in place for the next visitor.

**Hikkim.** Home to the world's highest post office at 4,440m. Send a postcard home — it's a cliché, and it's worth it. Expect a 2-6 week delivery time to the UK. The ATM here is the valley's most famous running joke: budget on it being "out of service."

**Komic.** The world's highest motorable village with a functioning monastery, at 4,587m. A short 15-20 minute drive from Hikkim and Langza, so most itineraries stack all three into a single day. The sense of scale here — nothing but rock, sky and prayer flags at nearly 4,600m — is the trip's most disorienting, memorable moment.

**Tabo Monastery.** The "Ajanta of the Himalayas" — founded in 996 CE, with 1,000-year-old wall paintings inside earthen-walled prayer halls, among the oldest continuously operating Buddhist monasteries in the Himalayas. Tabo sits at 3,280m, lower than Kaza, and is the smartest place to spend your first night in Spiti proper on the Shimla-Kinnaur approach.

**Dhankar Monastery.** Perched on a knife-edge ridge between the Spiti and Pin rivers — the literal definition of *precarity and peace*. The old monastery is structurally fragile (on conservation watch lists) so visit before it's further restricted; a newer monastery has been built nearby as the old structure's long-term future is uncertain.

**Chandratal Lake.** A crescent-shaped glacial lake at 4,300m on the Kaza-Manali exit route, its water shifting from turquoise to deep blue through the day. Accommodation near the lake is seasonal tented camps only (permanent structures are banned in the protected area), open roughly June to early October. The final approach is a 2km walk from the road-head car park — pace yourself at that altitude.

## 4. Manali vs Shimla: the "Transit Torture" — and why it's worth it

There are two ways into Spiti. The **loop** — one direction in, the other out — is the only sensible way to do it.

**The entry — Shimla → Kinnaur → Kaza.** The legendary "world's most treacherous road," a 2-3 day drive that allows your body to acclimatise slowly. You climb from Shimla (2,200m) through Reckong Peo (2,300m) to Tabo (3,280m) over two nights, then push to Kaza (3,800m) on Day 3 or 4. This direction is medically the safer entry, because the altitude gain happens in stages rather than in a single day's brutal jump.

**The exit — Kaza → Manali across Kunzum + Rohtang passes.** High intensity, high drama, and the quickest way back to civilisation. Crosses **Kunzum La (4,590m)** and **Rohtang Pass (3,978m)** in one long day. Closes in early October when the snow returns, and can close temporarily even in summer after heavy rain or a landslide.

**Why not enter via Manali?** Because the Manali route crosses Rohtang and Kunzum in a single day from a starting altitude of just 2,050m — nearly a 3,000m gain in a matter of hours. That's exactly the kind of rapid ascent that triggers Acute Mountain Sickness. Doing the loop Manali-in, Shimla-out is where the majority of AMS cases on this route originate. Save the Manali crossing for your exit, when you're already acclimatised and the altitude swing matters far less on the way down.

**The pro move.** Your hotel in Kaza (The Himalayan Brothers) can pre-arrange a private 4x4 and a driver who knows these roads like the back of his hand. Drivers from Manali charge less but typically don't know the Kinnaur side; drivers from Shimla don't know the Manali pass. Hire **a Spiti-resident driver** — the difference in confidence on hairpin bends is worth every rupee. **Never** hire a self-drive car for Spiti; most UK travel insurance policies explicitly exclude self-drive mountain-road accidents, and breakdown recovery in remote Spiti can take a full day.

<a id="permits"></a>

## 5. Permits, explained properly: Indian nationals vs foreign nationals

This is the section most guides get vague about, and it matters. The **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** system regulates travel near India's international borders, and Spiti's proximity to Tibet means parts of the loop are covered.

**If you're an Indian national**, most of the standard Spiti loop — Kaza, Kibber, Langza, Komic, Hikkim, Tabo, Dhankar — requires no permit at all. Only the stretch beyond Kaza toward the actual border villages (Kaurik, Chango) needs a simple, on-the-spot ILP.

**If you're a UK passport holder (or any foreign national)**, the requirement is stricter and covers more of the route. You'll need an ILP for the Kinnaur-Spiti stretch starting from Reckong Peo, checked at both the **Sumdo** and **Jangi** checkposts. Practical details that catch people out:

- **Group minimum.** Foreign nationals typically need to apply as a group of at least two travellers on the same permit — a genuinely solo foreign traveller may need to pair with another traveller at the checkpost, or book through an agency that handles this as part of the package.
- **Where to apply.** The SDM (Sub-Divisional Magistrate) office in **Reckong Peo** or **Kaza** processes ILPs, usually same-day if you arrive before the early-afternoon cutoff. Weekend and public-holiday processing is unreliable — don't plan your permit run for a Sunday.
- **What you need.** Passport (original + photocopy), visa copy, passport-sized photos (carry 2-4 spares), and the ₹250 (~£2.50) fee. Valid 7 days, extendable at the same office.
- **Who handles it for you.** **The Himalayan Brothers** in Kaza and most established Kaza-based hotels routinely arrange permits for foreign guests as part of the booking — confirm this in writing when you reserve, and don't assume it's automatic.

Don't skip this. The Sumdo and Kaza checkposts will turn you back without a valid ILP, and there's no way to sort it out on the spot at the checkpost itself — it has to be arranged in Reckong Peo or Kaza in advance.

## 6. Spiti Survival Checklist for UK travellers

The non-negotiables. Print this list. Pack against it.

1. **AMS (Acute Mountain Sickness) is real.** Carry **Diamox** prescribed by your UK GP before flying. Drink 4 litres of water a day. Skip alcohol entirely for the first 72 hours.
2. **The Dead Zone.** Outside Kaza, mobile signal vanishes. This is a 100% **digital detox** — embrace it. Tell your office before you leave.
3. **Cash is mandatory.** The ATM in Hikkim (the world's highest) is famously "out of service" most of the time, and Kaza's ATMs aren't much more reliable. Withdraw all rupees you'll need for the entire trip in **Shimla or Manali** before entering the valley.
4. **Solar power reality.** Most homestays run on solar. Charging your laptop is a midday luxury, not a midnight right. Carry a 20,000mAh power bank.
5. **Sun + altitude = chemical-grade burn.** UV at 4,000m is double sea-level intensity. SPF 50, lip balm with SPF, polarised sunglasses are non-negotiable. The light *bounces* off the cold desert.
6. **Dress in layers.** Mornings are -2°C, midday sun is 22°C, nights are -8°C — even in peak summer. Down jacket, fleece, thermal base, windproof shell. Sturdy walking boots (not trainers).
7. **The "buffer day" trick.** Build a flexible day into the itinerary. If altitude hits hard at Dhankar, you stay an extra night. Package tours don't allow this. **Book direct, not on a fixed-date tour.**
8. **Insurance that actually covers you.** Standard UK travel insurance caps altitude cover around 2,500-3,000m. Buy a policy explicitly covering trekking and travel to 5,000m with helicopter evacuation included — World Nomads, SafetyWing and Battleface all offer suitable High Altitude policies.
9. **Book homestays ahead in peak season.** Kibber, Langza and Komic have single-digit room counts per family. Confirm 4-6 weeks out via your Kaza hotel, not on arrival.
10. **Never self-drive.** Hire a local driver who knows the current road conditions. This is the single highest-leverage safety decision on the entire trip.

## Who Spiti actually suits — and who should wait

Spiti isn't a fit for every UK traveller, and it's worth being honest about that before you book flights. It suits people who are comfortable with genuine physical discomfort in exchange for genuine reward — cold rooms, long drives, patchy hot water, and days with nothing scheduled. It suits people recovering from burnout who actually want silence, not a resort with a "digital detox" marketing angle bolted onto a beach holiday. It suits couples and small groups who travel well together under stress, since a 12-hour mountain transit day tests a relationship faster than most trips do. And it suits people who can tolerate real uncertainty — a landslide closing the road for a day, a homestay's hot water failing, a pass closing early because of weather — without treating it as a holiday ruined rather than a mountain doing what mountains do.

It's a harder sell for anyone with a cardiac or respiratory condition that hasn't been cleared for high-altitude travel by a UK doctor, anyone who needs daily reliable connectivity for work or family reasons, anyone travelling with very young children (see the FAQ on this below), and anyone whose idea of "off-grid" is a hotel with slow WiFi rather than a village with none. If any of that describes you, McLeod Ganj — covered in the cross-link at the end of this guide — delivers a genuinely similar sabbatical mindset at a far gentler 1,800m, with reliable phone signal and a 45-minute drive from a domestic airport rather than a multi-day mountain transit.

## Gear and logistics deep-dive

The survival checklist above covers the non-negotiables. Here's the fuller picture for anyone assembling a kit list from scratch in the UK before departure.

**Clothing system.** Think in layers you can add and remove through a single day that might swing 30°C. Base layer: a moisture-wicking thermal top and leggings (merino wool holds up better than synthetic over multiple unwashed days). Mid layer: a heavyweight fleece or a synthetic-fill insulated jacket. Outer layer: a genuine windproof and waterproof shell — Spiti's cold desert means dry-cold most days, but wind chill at altitude is brutal and a sudden squall at a pass is not rare. Down layer: pack a proper down jacket for evenings and early mornings, particularly if you're doing homestay nights where heating is a wood stove rather than central heating. Legs: at least one pair of proper trekking trousers, plus thermal layers for underneath on the coldest mornings. Feet: broken-in hiking boots with ankle support — monastery courtyards, homestay paths and the Komic-Langza-Hikkim stretch all involve uneven, sometimes icy or muddy ground, and trainers won't cut it.

**Medical kit.** Diamox (acetazolamide), prescribed by your UK GP specifically for altitude before you fly — start the recommended dose a day before you climb above 3,000m, not after symptoms appear. Paracetamol and ibuprofen for altitude headaches. Oral rehydration salts. Any personal prescription medication in more than sufficient quantity, since pharmacies beyond Kaza are effectively non-existent and even Kaza's pharmacy has limited stock. A basic blister kit and antiseptic. Sun protection is medical-grade essential here, not optional: SPF 50+ reapplied through the day, a high-SPF lip balm, and proper wraparound polarised sunglasses — snow blindness and severe sunburn are genuine, common risks at this altitude and this UV intensity.

**Power and connectivity.** A 20,000mAh (or larger) power bank is close to mandatory — homestay solar systems prioritise lighting over device charging, and even Kaza's grid power can be intermittent. A universal UK-to-India adaptor (India uses Type C/D/M sockets). Download offline maps (Google Maps offline regions, or a dedicated app like Maps.me) for the entire route before you lose signal outside Kaza — you will lose signal outside Kaza. If you're bringing a laptop for genuine emergency-only use, treat charging it as an opportunistic task you do whenever power is available, not a scheduled routine.

**Money and documents.** Cash, cash, and more cash — see the ATM warning above. Carry photocopies of your passport, visa and Inner Line Permit separately from the originals. A small, quiet daypack for day trips to Komic, Langza and Hikkim, since your main luggage generally stays in the vehicle or at your Kaza base. A physical notebook or journal is worth the negligible weight — many travellers report the digital-detox window produces the kind of reflective writing they haven't done in years.

**What not to bring.** Anything you'd be devastated to lose or damage — the roads are rough, homestay storage is basic, and dust is constant. Skip the fancy outfit for dinner; nobody in Spiti is dressing for anything beyond staying warm. Skip excess luggage generally: a duffel bag or a single mid-size backpack per person is easier to manage across five transit legs than a hard suitcase.

## 7. The 14-day Spiti shape

A realistic itinerary for a UK traveller flying London → Delhi → Shimla → Kaza:

- **Day 1**: London → Delhi.
- **Day 2**: Delhi → Shimla (Kalka-Shimla toy train, UNESCO heritage).
- **Day 3**: Shimla → Reckong Peo (long mountain drive). Sort out your Inner Line Permit here if you haven't already.
- **Day 4**: Reckong Peo → Tabo. First high-altitude night at 3,280m. Visit Tabo Monastery's 1,000-year-old murals.
- **Day 5**: Tabo → Dhankar → Kaza. Settle at **The Himalayan Brothers**.
- **Day 6**: Acclimatisation day in Kaza. Walk the bazaar slowly. Sleep early. Do nothing on purpose.
- **Day 7**: Day trip to **Key Monastery** (morning prayers, 6:30am) + Kibber village.
- **Day 8**: Move to **Kibber Homestay** at 4,200m. Eat Thukpa. Sleep deep.
- **Day 9**: **Komic** (4,587m) + **Hikkim** post office. Send a postcard home.
- **Day 10**: **Langza Buddha** + fossil walk. Return to Kaza.
- **Day 11**: Pin Valley day trip (snow leopards in winter, ibex in summer).
- **Day 12**: Kaza → Manali across Kunzum + Rohtang passes, with a stop at **Chandratal Lake**. Long day.
- **Day 13**: Manali → Delhi by overnight Volvo bus.
- **Day 14**: Delhi → London. Arrive home a different person.
