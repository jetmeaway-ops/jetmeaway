  - q: "Why is Medellín called the City of Eternal Spring?"
    a: "Because it barely has seasons. The result is daytime temperatures in the low-to-mid twenties Celsius almost every day of the year, with evenings cool enough for a light layer. It is the single best thing about the city and the reason so many people who come for a week end up staying a month."

  - q: "What does no dar papaya mean?"

  - q: "Is it appropriate to visit Comuna 13, and how should I do it?"
    a: "Yes, and thousands of people do every week — but how you do it matters. Comuna 13 was one of the most dangerous neighbourhoods in the city during the worst years of Colombia's conflict, and the people who live there lived through it. What you are visiting today is what the community built afterwards: a set of outdoor escalators installed in 2011 that turned a punishing hillside climb into a few minutes, murals and street art made by local artists, music, food stalls and viewpoints over the valley.   - q: "What is the Metrocable and is it worth riding?"
    a: "The Metrocable is a network of cable-car gondola lines that plug the steep hillside barrios directly into the metro system, and it is the single most-studied piece of urban transport in Latin America. It was built as public transport rather than as an attraction — it cut journey times for residents of the hillside comunas from an hour of walking and buses to minutes — and it is included in the ordinary metro fare on the commuter lines. Ride it: the views over the valley are extraordinary and it explains more about how Medellín changed than any museum. Line L up to Parque Arví, the forest park above the city, is a separate and more expensive ticket, and worth it for the cloud-forest walks at the top."

Two rail lines run the length of the valley, the Metrocable gondolas climb the hillsides, a tram serves the eastern centre and dedicated bus lines fill the gaps. Buy a rechargeable Cívica card to skip the ticket queues. The one thing it does not do well is El Poblado nightlife, which sits high above the station on a steep hill — take a ride for that."

Next to it stands El Peñol, a 200-metre granite monolith with a staircase built into a fissure in the rock — somewhere around 700 steps, and the exact count is famously disputed — with lake and island views from the top that are the single best photograph in Antioquia. It is a genuine local tradition rather than a tourist production. Hotel rooms in El Poblado and Laureles sell out and prices rise, so book well ahead if your dates fall in August."
  - q: "Is Medellín good for digital nomads and long stays?"

  - q: "Do I need to speak Spanish in Medellín?"
    a: "Some, yes. Paisa Spanish — the accent of Medellín and Antioquia — is clear and often described as one of the easiest in Latin America to understand, which helps. Learn the courtesies, carry an offline translation app, and expect warmth in return for any attempt at all."
  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Medellín?"
    a: "Tap water in Medellín is generally considered safe to drink and is treated to a standard locals rely on daily — the city water supply has a good reputation, and many residents and long-stay visitors drink it straight from the tap. Bottled water is cheap and universally available if you would rather not, and it is the cautious choice if you have a sensitive stomach or you are travelling on into smaller rural towns where standards vary."
  - q: "When is the best time to visit Medellín?"
    a: "Any time — the eternal spring is not marketing, and average temperatures barely move across the year. What does change is rainfall: December to February and July to August are typically the drier stretches, while April to May and September to November see more afternoon downpours. Rain here usually means a heavy hour rather than a lost day. August brings the Feria de las Flores, which is the most exciting time to be in the city and the most expensive; December brings the Alumbrados, the enormous Christmas light installations along the river, which are free and genuinely spectacular."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Medellín?"
    a: "Three to four full days covers the city properly: one for the Centro, Plaza Botero and the Museo de Antioquia, one for the Metrocable and a community-led Comuna 13 tour, one for the Jardín Botánico, Parque Explora and Pueblito Paisa, and a fourth for Guatapé.   - q: "What should I eat in Medellín?"
    a: "Start with the bandeja paisa, the regional plate that arrives as beans, rice, minced beef, chicharrón, chorizo, fried plantain, avocado, an arepa and a fried egg — it is enormous, it is the local badge of identity, and one between two is a reasonable strategy. Beyond that: arepas at every corner, buñuelos, sancocho stew, fresh fruit juices in flavours you will not recognise, and coffee that is far better than the instant most Colombian households drink at home. Aguardiente, the anise spirit, is the drink of Antioquia and appears at every celebration."
  - q: "Is Parque Lleras worth going to at night?"
    a: "It is the centre of El Poblado nightlife and it is fine to visit, but go in with realistic expectations. It has become very touristy, the touts are persistent, and it attracts the sort of visitor economy the city itself has mixed feelings about. Many people prefer the quieter bars around Provenza a few streets away, or the more local strip along La 70 in Laureles. Wherever you go: keep your drink with you, keep your phone in your pocket, take a booked ride home, and do not go off with people you have just met."
  - q: "Are hotel breakfasts included in Medellín?"
    a: "Often at mid-range and luxury properties, less reliably at the cheapest ones. A Colombian hotel breakfast is typically fruit, eggs, an arepa, bread and very good coffee, and it is included at a good number of the hotels in this guide. At the £18 to £30 end it is more likely to be an extra or absent. Check what is included on the room page before you book — with bakeries and juice counters on every block, going out for breakfast is no hardship and often better."
  - q: "What is there to do in Medellín with kids?"
    a: "More than you would expect. Parque Explora combines a big interactive science museum with a freshwater aquarium and sits next to the Jardín Botánico and the Parque de los Deseos, so one metro stop covers a whole day. The Metrocable is a cable-car ride that children love and adults find moving for different reasons. Pueblito Paisa on top of Cerro Nutibara is a short, easy hill climb with a replica Antioquian village and a view over the whole valley. ## What to See in Medellín

Start with the thing that makes Medellín unlike anywhere else, which is the city itself. It sits at about **1,500 metres** in the **Aburrá Valley**, a narrow trench in the Andes with the centre on the flat floor and the neighbourhoods climbing the walls on both sides. That altitude gives it the mild, spring-like weather it is famous for — **the City of Eternal Spring** — and the topography gave it a problem that produced its most remarkable answer.

The **Metrocable** is that answer. From the late 1990s onwards, Medellín ran a programme of what planners call social urbanism: instead of clearing the hillside barrios, it connected them. Cable-car gondola lines were built up the valley walls and plugged directly into the metro, turning an hour of walking and packed buses into a few minutes of gliding over rooftops, and library parks, schools and public squares were built at the top stations. It is now one of the most studied pieces of urban transport in the world, and it is also, incidentally, the best sightseeing ride in Colombia — it costs the same as a metro fare, and the view over the whole valley is worth an afternoon. Take Line L on to **Parque Arví**, a separate ticket, for cloud-forest walking trails high above the city.

**Comuna 13** is the other half of that story, and it should be visited with some care. This hillside neighbourhood was, during the worst years of Colombia's conflict, among the most dangerous places in the country. What is there now is what its residents built afterwards: a set of **outdoor escalators** installed in 2011 that turned a punishing climb into a few minutes, and a hillside covered in murals, music, food stalls and viewpoints. It is one of the most visited places in the city — and the right way to see it is on a walking tour led by guides who grew up there, listening to the history they tell rather than hunting for narco anecdotes, asking before you photograph people, and spending money in the shops and cafés while you are there. It is a story about civic renewal, not a crime-tourism attraction, and the neighbourhood is somebody's home.

In the Centro, **Plaza Botero** is filled with rotund bronze figures donated by **Fernando Botero**, Medellín's most famous artistic son, with the **Museo de Antioquia** alongside holding a large collection of his paintings and sculpture. Nearby, **Parque Explora** combines a big hands-on science museum with a substantial freshwater aquarium, next door to the **Jardín Botánico** and its Orquideorama — a lattice of wooden flower-shaped canopies over the orchid collection, and one of the loveliest free-to-wander spots in the city. Up on **Cerro Nutibara**, **Pueblito Paisa** recreates a traditional Antioquian village square, and the terrace beside it gives the classic view over the whole valley.

Each **August** the city gives itself over to the **Feria de las Flores**, a week of concerts, horse parades and street events ending in the **Desfile de Silleteros**, when flower growers from the hills carry vast floral arrangements on their backs through the streets. Nightlife concentrates around **Parque Lleras** and **Provenza** in El Poblado — busy, international and increasingly touristy — and along **La 70** in Laureles, which is where locals go.

For days out: **Guatapé** and **El Peñol** are the classic trip, about two hours east, a town of brilliantly painted houses on a huge reservoir with a 200-metre granite monolith beside it, up which a staircase of somewhere around 700 steps (the exact count is famously disputed) climbs to one of the great views in South America. Closer to home, the **coffee farms** in the surrounding Antioquia hills run half-day tours that walk you from the plant to the cup, and colonial **Santa Fe de Antioquia** is an hour and a half north-west.

## Where to Stay in Medellín: Choosing Your Base

**El Poblado** is the usual first choice and rarely the wrong one. It is the safest and most visitor-friendly district, it holds the great majority of the city's hotels, and it has the restaurants, cafés, rooftop bars and nightlife — Parque Lleras and the Provenza streets — within walking distance of most of them. It is also where you will hear the most English and meet the most other travellers. Two honest catches: it is built on a steep hill, so the metro station at the bottom is a long uphill walk and most people end up taking rides, and it has become expensive and crowded enough that the nightlife around Lleras now feels aimed at visitors rather than at the city.

**Laureles** is the alternative, and the one many repeat visitors prefer. It is flat, leafy, low-rise and properly residential, laid out around circular avenues, with its own bar and restaurant strip along **La 70**, the Atanasio Girardot stadium at its edge and two metro stations serving it. Rooms cost less than El Poblado for the same standard, and it feels like a neighbourhood people live in rather than a district people visit. If you want to walk everywhere, stay here.

**Envigado** is quieter again — technically its own municipality, immediately south, on the same metro line. A real town square, good local restaurants, very little visitor economy and lower prices. It suits longer stays, families and anyone who would rather live somewhere than be entertained by it, at the cost of being further from the sights.

**The Centro** is the cheapest base and the best connected, with Plaza Botero, the Museo de Antioquia and the metro on the doorstep. It is also the most honest trade-off in this guide: intensely busy and noisy by day, run-down in patches, and not somewhere to wander around at night. It works for a short, sightseeing-heavy trip on a tight budget, and it does not work for a relaxed week.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Colombia?**
No. UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Colombia, and visitors are commonly granted up to 90 days on arrival. You will need a passport valid for your stay, and border officers may ask for proof of onward travel, where you are staying and that you can support yourself. Entry rules change, so check the current UK government travel advice for Colombia before you book and again in the week before you fly.

**What currency does Medellín use?**
The Colombian Peso, written COP or $. Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds so UK readers can compare like for like, but you will be charged in pesos on the ground. Peso amounts run into the hundreds of thousands for everyday purchases, which takes a day to get used to — a coffee can be five thousand pesos and a taxi across town thirty thousand. Cards are widely accepted in El Poblado and Laureles, but carry some cash for small cafés, buses and market stalls, and check the current rate shortly before you travel rather than budgeting off an old figure.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Medellín?**
No. There is no direct service between the UK and Medellín, and every route connects. The common options are via Bogotá, via Madrid, via Miami, or via Panama City, and total door-to-door journey time usually lands somewhere between sixteen and twenty-four hours depending on how good the connection is. Bogotá is the busiest gateway and the onward hop to Medellín takes about an hour; Madrid is the main European option if you would rather not clear immigration in the United States.

**Which Medellín airport should I fly into?**
Almost certainly José María Córdova International (MDE), which handles all international flights and most domestic ones. It is not in the city — it sits in the hills near Rionegro, about 35km out, and the drive down into the valley takes roughly 45 minutes to an hour, longer in traffic. The airport is also noticeably higher and cooler than Medellín itself, so do not judge the climate from the arrivals hall. The second airport, Olaya Herrera (EOH), is inside the city and handles short domestic hops on smaller aircraft — useful if you are connecting on to somewhere regional, but not where your flight from the UK will land.

**How do I get from MDE airport into Medellín?**
The simple options are an official airport taxi booked at the desk in arrivals, a transfer arranged by your hotel, or the shared airport minibuses that run down to the city. Use the official counter rather than an offer from someone approaching you in the terminal, agree or confirm the fare before you set off, and allow 45 to 60 minutes for the descent into the valley. If you are landing late at night, a pre-booked transfer is worth the small premium — it is a mountain road and you will be tired.

**Why is Medellín called the City of Eternal Spring?**
Because it barely has seasons. Medellín sits at about 1,500 metres in the Aburrá Valley, high enough to strip the heat out of its near-equatorial latitude but low enough to avoid the chill of Bogotá, which is another thousand metres up. The result is daytime temperatures in the low-to-mid twenties Celsius almost every day of the year, with evenings cool enough for a light layer. It is the single best thing about the city and the reason so many people who come for a week end up staying a month.

**What should I pack for Medellín?**
Layers rather than beachwear. Days are warm but not hot, evenings and mornings are cool, and rain arrives quickly and leaves quickly — so a light waterproof, a jumper or fleece for the evening, and comfortable shoes for a city built on slopes. The sun is strong at altitude even when it does not feel hot, so bring sun cream. If you are heading on to the Caribbean coast or down to the coffee country afterwards, pack for that separately: Medellín itself is not a swimsuit destination.

**Is Medellín safe for tourists?**
Medellín has changed enormously and is a genuinely rewarding city to visit, but it is not risk-free and it deserves a straight answer rather than either scaremongering or a shrug. Street robbery, phone snatching and scams do happen, and visitors are obvious targets. The precautions that work are ordinary ones: keep your phone out of sight rather than navigating with it in your hand, carry only the card and cash you need, use Uber or another app-based ride or a booked taxi at night instead of walking unfamiliar streets, and stick to well-populated areas after dark. Read the UK government's current travel advice for Colombia before you go, and check it again the week you fly.

**What does no dar papaya mean?**
It is the Colombian phrase you will hear most often, and it is the whole safety briefing in three words. Literally it means do not give papaya; in practice it means do not make yourself an easy target. A phone left on a café table, a wallet in a back pocket, an expensive watch on a night out, a laptop visible through a car window — that is papaya. Colombians apply the rule to themselves as much as to visitors, and if you follow it you have removed most of the risk before it arises.

**Is drink spiking a real risk in Medellín, and what about dating apps?**
Yes, and it is worth stating plainly rather than burying. Drugging and robbery of visitors — usually after a drink is spiked in a bar or a hotel room — is a documented problem in Medellín, and the UK government travel advice for Colombia specifically warns about it, including cases involving people met through dating apps. The practical rules are simple: do not accept drinks or food from strangers, watch your drink being poured and keep it with you, do not invite people you have just met back to your accommodation, and if you are meeting someone from an app, meet in a busy public place and tell somebody where you are. This is not a reason to avoid the city — it is a reason to be sensible in it.

**Which neighbourhood should I stay in in Medellín?**
For a first visit, El Poblado or Laureles. El Poblado is the usual first choice and the safest, most visitor-friendly base — it holds most of the hotels, the restaurants, the rooftop bars and the nightlife around Parque Lleras and Provenza, and it is where almost everyone who is unsure ends up staying. Laureles is the alternative many repeat visitors prefer: flatter, greener, more residential and more Colombian, with its own bar strip along La 70 and better value on rooms. Envigado is quieter and more suburban again, and the Centro is the cheapest and best connected but busy by day and not somewhere to wander at night.

**El Poblado or Laureles — which should I choose?**
El Poblado if you want convenience, English-speaking front desks, restaurants at every price point and the shortest learning curve; Laureles if you want to feel like you are in Colombia rather than in a visitors quarter. The honest trade-offs: El Poblado is built on a steep hill, so the metro station at the bottom is a long uphill walk from Parque Lleras and most people end up taking rides everywhere, and the nightlife around Lleras can be loud and aggressively touristy. Laureles is flat, walkable, leafier and cheaper, with the Estadio and Floresta metro stations nearby, but it is quieter and has fewer high-end hotels. Neither is a mistake.

**Is Envigado a good base?**
Envigado is technically its own municipality rather than part of Medellín, immediately south of El Poblado and on the same metro line. It is quieter, more residential and more affordable, with a proper town square, good local restaurants and very little of the visitor economy — which is exactly why some people love it and others find it too far from the action. It suits longer stays, people travelling with family, and anyone who would rather live somewhere than be entertained by it. For a three-night first trip it is probably one neighbourhood too far out.

**Is the Centro a good place to stay?**
It depends what you want. Medellín Centro has the lowest room rates in the city, the best metro connections and most of the sights — Plaza Botero, the Museo de Antioquia, Parque Berrío — within walking distance. It is also intensely busy by day, run-down in patches, and the standing local advice is not to wander around it at night. If your budget is the deciding factor and you plan to be out sightseeing by day and back before dark, it works. If you want to eat and drink where you sleep, pay the extra and stay in Laureles or El Poblado.

**Where should I stay in Medellín on a budget?**
Laureles and the area around the Atanasio Girardot stadium give the best combination of low prices, safety and walkability — a cluster of the cheapest hotels in this guide sit there, several under £30 a night, with the metro and a genuine neighbourhood on the doorstep. Envigado and Itagüí, both on metro Line A, are cheaper again and perfectly practical if you do not mind a 20-minute ride into town. The Centro has the very lowest rates but the biggest trade-offs. El Poblado has budget options too, but you pay a premium of roughly £10 to £15 a night for the postcode.

**Is it appropriate to visit Comuna 13, and how should I do it?**
Yes, and thousands of people do every week — but how you do it matters. Comuna 13 was one of the most dangerous neighbourhoods in the city during the worst years of Colombia's conflict, and the people who live there lived through it. What you are visiting today is what the community built afterwards: a set of outdoor escalators installed in 2011 that turned a punishing hillside climb into a few minutes, murals and street art made by local artists, music, food stalls and viewpoints over the valley. Go with a guide from the neighbourhood itself — community-led walking tours are easy to find and the money stays local — listen to the history rather than hunting for crime-tourism anecdotes, ask before photographing people, and spend money in the shops and cafés while you are there. Treated with respect it is one of the most moving things you can do in Colombia.

**What is the Metrocable and is it worth riding?**
The Metrocable is a network of cable-car gondola lines that plug the steep hillside barrios directly into the metro system, and it is the single most-studied piece of urban transport in Latin America. It was built as public transport rather than as an attraction — it cut journey times for residents of the hillside comunas from an hour of walking and buses to minutes — and it is included in the ordinary metro fare on the commuter lines. Ride it: the views over the valley are extraordinary and it explains more about how Medellín changed than any museum. Line L up to Parque Arví, the forest park above the city, is a separate and more expensive ticket, and worth it for the cloud-forest walks at the top.

**Is the Medellín metro useful for visitors?**
Very. Medellín has the only rail metro in Colombia, and residents treat it with a pride that is obvious the moment you step on — it is clean, cheap, safe and busy, and locals will tell you it was the first visible sign the city was going to be alright. Two rail lines run the length of the valley, the Metrocable gondolas climb the hillsides, a tram serves the eastern centre and dedicated bus lines fill the gaps. Buy a rechargeable Cívica card to skip the ticket queues. The one thing it does not do well is El Poblado nightlife, which sits high above the station on a steep hill — take a ride for that.

**Can I do a day trip to Guatapé?**
It is the classic Medellín day trip and it deserves the reputation. Guatapé is about two hours east by road, a small town of vividly painted houses with decorated zócalo panels along the base of every wall, sitting on a huge man-made reservoir. Next to it stands El Peñol, a 200-metre granite monolith with a staircase built into a fissure in the rock — somewhere around 700 steps, and the exact count is famously disputed — with lake and island views from the top that are the single best photograph in Antioquia. Go with an organised tour, a hired driver or the public bus from the Terminal Norte, start early, and take water for the climb.

**What other day trips are worth doing from Medellín?**
Santa Fe de Antioquia, about an hour and a half north-west, is a whitewashed colonial town that was the regional capital before Medellín took over — hot, low-lying and completely different in feel. Jardín, three hours south, is a postcard pueblo of painted balconies around a square where people still ride in on horseback. Closer to home, Parque Arví is reachable on the Metrocable, and the coffee farms in the hills around the city run half-day tours that walk you from the plant to the cup. Colombia drives on the right, and mountain roads plus heavy lorry traffic make journeys slower than the distances suggest — most visitors hire a driver or take a tour rather than self-driving.

**When is the Feria de las Flores?**
Each August. The Feria de las Flores is Medellín's biggest festival and the one that defines the city year — a week or so of concerts, horse parades, classic car displays and street events, climaxing in the Desfile de Silleteros, when growers from the hillsides above the city carry vast wooden flower arrangements on their backs through the streets. It is a genuine local tradition rather than a tourist production. Hotel rooms in El Poblado and Laureles sell out and prices rise, so book well ahead if your dates fall in August.

**Is Medellín good for digital nomads and long stays?**
It is one of the most popular remote-work bases in the Americas, for obvious reasons: the climate never changes, the cost of living is low by western European standards, the internet is good, and the time difference from the UK is manageable at five hours behind. El Poblado and Laureles have the co-working spaces and the cafés that will let you sit for three hours. Several hotels in this guide are set up for it, with desks, kitchens or co-working floors. Practical notes: monthly apartment rates undercut nightly hotel rates substantially, check the current visa position for stays beyond a normal tourist entry, and be aware that the influx has pushed rents up in El Poblado and is a live local grievance — be a good guest.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Medellín?**
Some, yes. English is spoken at the bigger hotels, on organised tours and in a good number of El Poblado restaurants, but far less widely than in Europe and hardly at all with taxi drivers, in neighbourhood shops or in the Centro. Paisa Spanish — the accent of Medellín and Antioquia — is clear and often described as one of the easiest in Latin America to understand, which helps. Learn the courtesies, carry an offline translation app, and expect warmth in return for any attempt at all.

**Can I drink the tap water in Medellín?**
Tap water in Medellín is generally considered safe to drink and is treated to a standard locals rely on daily — the city water supply has a good reputation, and many residents and long-stay visitors drink it straight from the tap. Bottled water is cheap and universally available if you would rather not, and it is the cautious choice if you have a sensitive stomach or you are travelling on into smaller rural towns where standards vary.

**When is the best time to visit Medellín?**
Any time — the eternal spring is not marketing, and average temperatures barely move across the year. What does change is rainfall: December to February and July to August are typically the drier stretches, while April to May and September to November see more afternoon downpours. Rain here usually means a heavy hour rather than a lost day. August brings the Feria de las Flores, which is the most exciting time to be in the city and the most expensive; December brings the Alumbrados, the enormous Christmas light installations along the river, which are free and genuinely spectacular.

**How many days do you need in Medellín?**
Three to four full days covers the city properly: one for the Centro, Plaza Botero and the Museo de Antioquia, one for the Metrocable and a community-led Comuna 13 tour, one for the Jardín Botánico, Parque Explora and Pueblito Paisa, and a fourth for Guatapé. Add more if you are working remotely or planning coffee-farm trips — plenty of people arrive with four days booked and leave three weeks later, which is a genuine pattern here rather than a figure of speech.

**What should I eat in Medellín?**
Start with the bandeja paisa, the regional plate that arrives as beans, rice, minced beef, chicharrón, chorizo, fried plantain, avocado, an arepa and a fried egg — it is enormous, it is the local badge of identity, and one between two is a reasonable strategy. Beyond that: arepas at every corner, buñuelos, sancocho stew, fresh fruit juices in flavours you will not recognise, and coffee that is far better than the instant most Colombian households drink at home. Aguardiente, the anise spirit, is the drink of Antioquia and appears at every celebration.

**Is Parque Lleras worth going to at night?**
It is the centre of El Poblado nightlife and it is fine to visit, but go in with realistic expectations. It has become very touristy, the touts are persistent, and it attracts the sort of visitor economy the city itself has mixed feelings about. Many people prefer the quieter bars around Provenza a few streets away, or the more local strip along La 70 in Laureles. Wherever you go: keep your drink with you, keep your phone in your pocket, take a booked ride home, and do not go off with people you have just met.
