  - q: "What are the ghats in Varanasi?"
    a: "The ghats are the broad stone steps that run down to the Ganges along the city's riverfront — around 80 of them, stretching several kilometres. They are the heart of Varanasi: people bathe, pray, do laundry, practise yoga, sell flowers and cremate their dead here. Dashashwamedh Ghat is the busiest and the setting for the main evening aarti; Assi Ghat at the southern end is calmer and popular with travellers."
  - q: "What is the Ganga Aarti in Varanasi?"
    a: "It is the grand evening ceremony of light and sound offered to the Ganges, performed daily at dusk at Dashashwamedh Ghat by robed priests with tiered brass lamps, incense, bells and chanting. It draws large crowds; many people watch from a hired boat on the water for a clearer, calmer view. A smaller, gentler aarti also takes place at Assi Ghat. It is free to attend from the steps."
  - q: "Should I take a sunrise boat ride?"
    a: "Yes — it is the quintessential Varanasi experience. In the soft early light, rowing boats slip along the ghats while the city wakes, bathers enter the river and the steps glow gold. Agree the price and the route with the boatman before you set off, expect to pay per boat rather than per person, and choose a rowing boat over a noisy motorboat for atmosphere. Sunset rides that end at the aarti are the other popular option."
  - q: "What are the Manikarnika and Harishchandra ghats?"
    a: "They are the two main cremation ghats, where Hindu funeral pyres burn — Manikarnika is the larger and older, Harishchandra the smaller. To die and be cremated in Varanasi is considered by many Hindus to bring liberation, so families travel here from across India. These are places of grief and deep religious meaning, not tourist attractions; visit quietly and respectfully if at all, and keep your distance from the pyres and the mourners."
  - q: "Is photography allowed at the cremation ghats?"
    a: "No. Photographing or filming the cremations, the pyres or the mourning families is not permitted and is deeply disrespectful — do not do it, and firmly decline anyone who offers to guide you to a 'photo spot'. You may be approached by people asking for donations 'for the wood' or for a hospice; polite refusal is fine, and any genuine giving is better done through a recognised charity than a stranger on the steps."
  - q: "What is the Kashi Vishwanath Temple?"

It sits just back from the river in the old city. Security is tight, queues can be very long on festival days, and there are rules on what you may carry inside — phones and cameras are generally not allowed beyond the cloakrooms."
  - q: "What is the Kashi Vishwanath Corridor?"

It has made access far easier and the approach much grander, though some lament the old maze that was lost. Expect airport-style security, broad marble walkways and, on big days, enormous crowds."
  - q: "Can non-Hindus enter the Kashi Vishwanath Temple?"

  - q: "What is Sarnath and is it worth visiting?"

It is a calm, green half-day trip: the towering Dhamek Stupa, the ruins of ancient monasteries, the Ashokan pillar remains and an excellent archaeological museum. It is a complete change of pace from the intensity of the ghats and well worth the short drive."
  - q: "What is Dev Deepawali?"

Every ghat along the river is lined with hundreds of thousands of oil lamps, there is a huge coordinated aarti, and boats fill the water. It is breathtaking and correspondingly crowded — book accommodation and a boat weeks or months in advance if you want to be there."
  - q: "Is the Ganges clean, and can I bathe in it?"

Pilgrims bathe for religious reasons, and you will see them do so every morning; that is their faith and should be respected. As a visitor, most people admire the river from the steps or a boat rather than entering it. If you do touch the water, wash your hands well afterwards and keep it away from your face and mouth."

## Varanasi is a holy city and a working cremation city — read this before you book

Varanasi — also called Kashi and Banaras — is one of the oldest continuously lived-in cities on earth and one of the holiest places in Hinduism. It is also a working cremation city: many Hindus travel here to die, believing that being cremated on these ghats and having their ashes given to the Ganges brings moksha, release from the cycle of rebirth. That means the riverfront you have come to see is, at Manikarnika and Harishchandra ghats, a place where funerals take place around the clock. Approach it with that in mind and the city makes sense; approach it as a spectacle and you will cause offence.

The single most important rule: **do not photograph or film the cremations, the pyres or the mourning families, and keep a respectful distance.** Anyone who offers to take you to a "viewing point" or a "photo spot" for a fee should be politely declined, as should requests for donations "for the wood" — genuine charity is better given through a recognised organisation. Watch quietly from a distance if you wish, or from a boat on the river, and let the families grieve in peace. These are the most sacred moments in many people's lives, not content for a phone.

Beyond the cremation ghats, the same spirit of respect applies everywhere. This is an active place of worship for millions. **Dress modestly, remove your shoes at temples, do not touch offerings or shrines, and ask before photographing individuals at prayer.** Alcohol is available in Varanasi — unlike the dry cities of Rishikesh and Haridwar — but it has no place at the ghats or near the temples, and Varanasi is emphatically not a party destination. Food near the temples and along the ghats is largely vegetarian, often cooked without onion or garlic in the strictest kitchens, though meat is available in other parts of the city.

## What to See and Do in Varanasi

The ghats are the reason to come — around 80 stone stairways running for several kilometres along the west bank of the Ganges. Start early: a **sunrise boat ride** is the quintessential Varanasi experience, the low light turning the steps gold as the city bathes, prays and washes at the water's edge. Agree the price and route with the boatman first, pay per boat rather than per head, and choose a quiet rowing boat over a motorboat. Then walk the ghats on foot, from calm **Assi Ghat** at the southern end past the bathing and prayer ghats to the busy heart at **Dashashwamedh**.

At dusk, **Dashashwamedh Ghat** hosts the **Ganga Aarti**, the great nightly ceremony of fire, lamps, incense and chanting offered to the river by robed priests. It is mesmerising and very crowded; many people watch from a boat for a calmer view, and there is a smaller, gentler aarti at Assi Ghat too. Both are free to watch from the steps.

Set back from the river in the old city is the **Kashi Vishwanath Temple**, dedicated to Shiva and one of Hinduism's holiest shrines, now approached through the wide **Kashi Vishwanath Corridor** opened in 2021, which links the temple's grand new precinct down to the Ganges. Security is airport-style and phones and cameras are left at the cloakroom; queues can be enormous on festival days. Around it winds the old city itself — a labyrinth of narrow **galis** (lanes) too tight for cars, full of temples, silk shops, sweet stalls, cows and the constant flow of pilgrims. Getting a little lost in them is part of the experience.

The two cremation ghats, **Manikarnika** and **Harishchandra**, are covered above: visit only with respect, and never with a camera. For a complete change of pace, **Sarnath**, about 10 km northeast, is where the Buddha gave his first sermon — a peaceful half-day of the great Dhamek Stupa, monastery ruins, the Ashokan pillar remains and a fine archaeological museum. Closer in, **Banaras Hindu University (BHU)** has a leafy campus, the New Vishwanath Temple and a good art museum, and **Ramnagar Fort** sits across the river.

If you can time it, **Dev Deepawali** in November — the "Diwali of the Gods" on the Kartik full moon — sees every ghat lit with hundreds of thousands of oil lamps and is the most spectacular night of the Varanasi year. It is also the most crowded, so book far ahead.

## Where to Stay in Varanasi

**Near the ghats** is where most first-time visitors want to be, and for good reason — you can walk out to a dawn boat or the evening aarti in minutes. **Assi Ghat**, at the southern end, is the calmer, greener, more traveller-friendly choice, with cafés and an easier pace; the lanes around **Dashashwamedh** put you in the heart of the action but mean carrying your bags on foot through crowded, vehicle-free galis. Riverfront rooms at any price, from the BrijRama palace to a £9 guesthouse, share that unbeatable location.

**The old city (Kashi)** — the warren of lanes behind the central ghats — is full of cheap guesthouses and homestays, atmospheric and central but noisy, tight and confusing to navigate with luggage. It suits budget travellers who want to be immersed and do not mind the walk in.

**The Cantonment (Cantt), Sigra and Mahmoorganj** are the modern districts inland, near the railway station and a manageable drive from the airport. This is where the big comfortable hotels sit — the DoubleTree, Clarks and the modern four- and five-stars — with car access to the door, quieter nights and pools. Choose this side if you value comfort and a calm retreat over stepping straight onto the ghats.

**Sarnath**, 10 km out, is the quietest base of all, green and calm around the Buddhist sites — a good choice for travellers who want serenity and heritage over the riverfront intensity, and do not mind driving in for the ghats.

## Varanasi Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Varanasi?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party 'visa services' charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Varanasi?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work at the smarter hotels and larger restaurants, but carry cash for boatmen, autorickshaws, temple offerings, small guesthouses and the market stalls in the old city.

**How do I get to Varanasi from the UK?**
There are no direct flights from the UK to Varanasi. You connect: either fly to Delhi or Mumbai and take a domestic hop to Varanasi (VNS), or route through a Gulf hub such as Dubai, Abu Dhabi or Doha and pick up an onward Indian domestic flight. Allow a full travel day either way, and leave a sensible connection margin if your bags have to be rechecked between international and domestic terminals.

**What is the nearest airport to Varanasi?**
Lal Bahadur Shastri International Airport (VNS) at Babatpur, about 25 km northwest of the city — roughly 45 minutes to an hour by taxi depending on traffic. It handles domestic flights from Delhi, Mumbai and other Indian cities plus a handful of regional international routes, so most UK travellers arrive on a domestic connection. Prepaid taxis and app cabs serve the terminal; agree the fare or use the meter.

**Can I reach Varanasi by train?**
Yes — Varanasi is a major railway junction with good connections across northern India, and the overnight trains from Delhi are a classic way to arrive. Varanasi Junction (also called Varanasi Cantt) is the main station; Banaras and Pt. Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Junction also serve the city. Book reserved-class tickets well ahead through the official Indian Railways system, as popular trains sell out.

**When is the best time to visit Varanasi?**
October to March. Those cooler, drier months are far more comfortable for long days of walking the ghats and the old-city lanes, and they take in the biggest festivals. November is especially special because of Dev Deepawali. April to June is intensely hot, often over 40°C, and July to September is the monsoon, when the river rises and floods the lower ghats.

**What is the monsoon like in Varanasi?**
July to September brings the monsoon, and the Ganges rises dramatically — the lower steps of the ghats flood, the riverside walkway between them can become impassable, and boat operations and the aarti are sometimes moved or curtailed. Rooms are cheaper and the city is quieter, but if walking the full sweep of the ghats or a dawn boat ride is central to your trip, avoid the flood months.

**What are the ghats in Varanasi?**
The ghats are the broad stone steps that run down to the Ganges along the city's riverfront — around 80 of them, stretching several kilometres. They are the heart of Varanasi: people bathe, pray, do laundry, practise yoga, sell flowers and cremate their dead here. Dashashwamedh Ghat is the busiest and the setting for the main evening aarti; Assi Ghat at the southern end is calmer and popular with travellers.

**What is the Ganga Aarti in Varanasi?**
It is the grand evening ceremony of light and sound offered to the Ganges, performed daily at dusk at Dashashwamedh Ghat by robed priests with tiered brass lamps, incense, bells and chanting. It draws large crowds; many people watch from a hired boat on the water for a clearer, calmer view. A smaller, gentler aarti also takes place at Assi Ghat. It is free to attend from the steps.

**Should I take a sunrise boat ride?**
Yes — it is the quintessential Varanasi experience. In the soft early light, rowing boats slip along the ghats while the city wakes, bathers enter the river and the steps glow gold. Agree the price and the route with the boatman before you set off, expect to pay per boat rather than per person, and choose a rowing boat over a noisy motorboat for atmosphere. Sunset rides that end at the aarti are the other popular option.

**What are the Manikarnika and Harishchandra ghats?**
They are the two main cremation ghats, where Hindu funeral pyres burn — Manikarnika is the larger and older, Harishchandra the smaller. To die and be cremated in Varanasi is considered by many Hindus to bring liberation, so families travel here from across India. These are places of grief and deep religious meaning, not tourist attractions; visit quietly and respectfully if at all, and keep your distance from the pyres and the mourners.

**Is photography allowed at the cremation ghats?**
No. Photographing or filming the cremations, the pyres or the mourning families is not permitted and is deeply disrespectful — do not do it, and firmly decline anyone who offers to guide you to a 'photo spot'. You may be approached by people asking for donations 'for the wood' or for a hospice; polite refusal is fine, and any genuine giving is better done through a recognised charity than a stranger on the steps.

**What is the Kashi Vishwanath Temple?**
It is Varanasi's most important temple, dedicated to Shiva as Vishwanath and one of the twelve Jyotirlinga shrines, making it among the holiest sites in Hinduism. It sits just back from the river in the old city. Security is tight, queues can be very long on festival days, and there are rules on what you may carry inside — phones and cameras are generally not allowed beyond the cloakrooms.

**What is the Kashi Vishwanath Corridor?**
It is the wide new pilgrimage precinct, opened at the end of 2021, that cleared a congested tangle of lanes to connect the Kashi Vishwanath Temple directly to the Ganges at Lalita Ghat. It has made access far easier and the approach much grander, though some lament the old maze that was lost. Expect airport-style security, broad marble walkways and, on big days, enormous crowds.

**Can non-Hindus enter the Kashi Vishwanath Temple?**
Non-Hindus are generally permitted to enter, but rules and security are strict and can change, particularly around festivals, so check locally on the day. You will pass through tight security, leave phones, cameras, bags and leather items in a cloakroom, and may face long queues. Dress modestly, remove your shoes, and follow the stewards' instructions — this is an active place of worship, not a monument.

**What is Sarnath and is it worth visiting?**
Sarnath, about 10 km northeast of the centre, is where the Buddha gave his first sermon after his enlightenment, making it one of Buddhism's four great pilgrimage sites. It is a calm, green half-day trip: the towering Dhamek Stupa, the ruins of ancient monasteries, the Ashokan pillar remains and an excellent archaeological museum. It is a complete change of pace from the intensity of the ghats and well worth the short drive.
**What is Dev Deepawali?**
Dev Deepawali, the 'Diwali of the Gods', falls on the full moon of Kartik (November, fifteen days after Diwali) and is Varanasi's most spectacular festival. Every ghat along the river is lined with hundreds of thousands of oil lamps, there is a huge coordinated aarti, and boats fill the water. It is breathtaking and correspondingly crowded — book accommodation and a boat weeks or months in advance if you want to be there.

**Is the Ganges clean, and can I bathe in it?**
The Ganges at Varanasi is heavily polluted despite long-running clean-up programmes, and it is not clean by Western bathing standards. Pilgrims bathe for religious reasons, and you will see them do so every morning; that is their faith and should be respected. As a visitor, most people admire the river from the steps or a boat rather than entering it. If you do touch the water, wash your hands well afterwards and keep it away from your face and mouth.

**Can I drink the tap water in Varanasi?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only, and be cautious with ice, salads and anything raw until your stomach has adjusted. Carry a reusable bottle and refill from filtered stations where your hotel or a café offers one. Do not drink or brush your teeth with tap water, and never drink from the river, however holy it is.

**Is Varanasi safe for solo and female travellers?**
Varanasi is visited by large numbers of solo and female travellers and is broadly safe, but it is intense, crowded and full of hustle, which can feel overwhelming at first. Ordinary precautions apply: dress modestly, keep valuables secure in the crush, avoid unlit ghats and empty lanes after dark, be firm with persistent touts, and use a trusted app cab or a hotel-arranged car at night. Read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go.

**How do I deal with touts and guides at the ghats?**
Expect a steady stream of offers — boat rides, silk shops, 'free' temple guiding, blessings that end in a demand for money. Most people are simply making a living, but the pressure is real, especially around Dashashwamedh and the main temple. A calm, firm 'no, thank you' while you keep walking works best; do not accept an unsolicited flower, tikka or guide unless you are willing to pay. Book boats and tours through your hotel or an established operator if you would rather avoid the haggling.

**What should I wear in Varanasi?**
Dress modestly — cover shoulders and knees, and lean conservative near the temples and ghats, where you are among pilgrims and mourners. Loose cotton is coolest in the heat and easy to buy locally. You will remove your shoes at temples, so slip-on footwear is practical, and the ghats and lanes are uneven, so wear something you can walk in. Bring a light layer for cool winter mornings and evenings on the water.

**How should I behave at the ghats and temples?**
Quietly and respectfully. Remove your shoes where required, ask before photographing individuals, and never photograph the cremations or mourners. Keep a respectful distance at the funeral ghats, do not use the river as a backdrop for anything flippant, and follow each temple's rules on phones, cameras and dress. This is a living sacred city for millions of people — approach it as a guest at someone's place of worship, not as a spectacle.

**How many days do I need in Varanasi?**
Two to three days is enough for most visitors: a sunrise boat ride, an evening aarti, a slow walk along the ghats, the old-city lanes and the Kashi Vishwanath Corridor, plus a half-day at Sarnath. Add a day if you want to settle into the rhythm of the place, take a second boat trip, or make a longer excursion. Many people combine Varanasi with Agra and Delhi on a northern circuit.

**Which area should I stay in in Varanasi?**
Near the ghats for atmosphere — Assi Ghat at the southern end is calmer, greener and popular with travellers, while the lanes around Dashashwamedh put you in the thick of it but mean carrying bags through crowded, vehicle-free galis. The Cantonment (Cantt) near the station and the modern Sigra and Mahmoorganj districts are easier for cars, business travel and a quieter night's sleep. Sarnath suits anyone wanting calm and Buddhist heritage over riverfront buzz.

**Is Varanasi good for families?**
It can be, for families with older children who will find the boats, the ghats and the ceremonies fascinating — but it is intense, crowded and sensory, and the cremation ghats need an honest word with children beforehand. Very young children may find the crowds and uneven lanes hard work. The calmer base of Assi Ghat, or a comfortable hotel in the Cantonment with a pool, makes a family trip much easier.

**How do I get around Varanasi?**
The old city along the ghats is a maze of narrow lanes closed to cars, so you walk — cars and larger autorickshaws drop you at the edge. For longer hops use app cabs (Uber and Ola operate here) or autorickshaws, agreeing the fare or using the meter; cycle-rickshaws are cheap and characterful for short distances but slow. Boats handle the river. Allow extra time everywhere, because traffic and crowds are heavy.

**Is alcohol available in Varanasi?**
Yes, but discreetly — unlike the dry holy cities of Rishikesh and Haridwar, Varanasi is not legally dry, and some hotels and licensed restaurants serve alcohol. It is not a nightlife or party destination, though, and drinking is inappropriate at or near the ghats and temples. Be low-key, and do not carry or consume alcohol in the religious areas. Food near the temples is largely vegetarian, though meat is available in other parts of the city.
