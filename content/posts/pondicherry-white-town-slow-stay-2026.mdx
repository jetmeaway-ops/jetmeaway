French is genuinely *useful* but not necessary — older residents (60+) often speak French at home, the French Consulate runs Alliance Française language classes for the young Indo-French descendants, and a handful of restaurants and the French Pharmacy still hold French as their first language. Heavy ashram and Indo-French expatriate presence sets a respectful tone, the French Quarter is genuinely walkable until 11pm, and Auroville's intentional-community ethos is built around safety.   - q: "What's the dress code at Sri Aurobindo Ashram?"
    a: "Modest. Shoulders and knees must be covered for both women and men entering the **main Ashram building** on rue de la Marine. No shorts, no vests, no sleeveless tops. A scarf or shawl is enough to cover shoulders if you're wearing a sleeveless top — many travellers carry one in their bag as a quick-cover layer. Photography is not permitted inside the Samadhi (the central shrine). The Ashram is not a tourist site; it's a working spiritual practice — silence is expected, and the staff will politely but firmly redirect anyone treating it casually."

Slots are limited (about 30-40 visitors per day), and you'll be issued a 'concentration pass' specifying your time. The **Viewing Point** (looking at the dome from a garden 100m away) is free and walk-up — you watch a 10-minute orientation video, get a pass, walk a shaded path to the viewing area. **Pro-tip**: have your hotel call the booking centre 48 hours ahead. Hotels with established relationships (Palais de Mahe, Maison Perumal, Le Dupleix) get inner-chamber slots their guests can't always secure independently."

For a visiting UK traveller, the practical takeaway is simpler: it's not a theme park and not a commune tour — treat the Matrimandir and Visitors Centre with the same quiet respect you'd bring to a cathedral, don't wander into residential sectors uninvited, and budget a half-day rather than a drive-by. ## 1. Two cities, one canal <a id="two-cities"></a>

Pondicherry is split by a 200-year-old canal — built by the French East India Company in the 18th century to drain the swamp the city was being raised on, now a quiet dividing line that determined two completely different urban grids.

To the **east** is **Ville Blanche** — White Town. Paved streets at right angles, French street names (rue Romain Rolland, rue de la Marine, rue Suffren), 18th-century colonial villas behind walled courtyards, the **Sri Aurobindo Ashram** as the spiritual anchor, and a sea-front promenade where every Pondicherrian goes for evening walks.

To the **west** is **Ville Tamoule** — Black Town. Vibrant temple streets, traditional Tamil shophouses with painted shutters, the **Goubert Market**, and the **Sunday Bazaar** that takes over MG Road. Ginger-garlic in the air, scooters everywhere, the actual working city.

Linked by history, separated by 200 years of colonial planning. Most UK visitors stay only in White Town and miss the Tamil Quarter entirely. The 7-day shape we suggest below fixes that — you base in either, walk to the other in 10 minutes.

The canal itself is worth pausing on, even though it rarely makes anyone's must-see list. It's mostly dry or shallow today, spanned by a handful of low bridges that barely register as you cross them, but it's the reason Pondicherry has two distinct characters instead of one blended one. Walk it end to end at dusk (roughly 20 minutes) and you'll notice the shift in real time — French shopfront signage gives way to Tamil script, quiet café murmur gives way to temple bells and traffic, mustard-yellow colonial paint gives way to the bright pastels of Tamil shophouses. It's the single best way to understand the city's geography in one walk, and most guidebooks skip it entirely in favour of sending you straight to the postcard streets.

## 2. The 500-metre lifestyle <a id="five-hundred-metres"></a>

In **White Town**, everything is a 10-minute walk. From the Promenade you can stroll to the Sri Aurobindo Ashram (4 minutes), the **Cluny Embroidery Centre** (5 minutes — a heritage workshop where local women hand-embroider the linens stocked at half the boutique hotels in the city), the **Pondicherry Museum** (6 minutes — French and Tamil colonial artefacts, free entry), three Michelin-quality restaurants, four boutique heritage hotels.

This is **slow travel** in its purest form — no rickshaw haggling, no Google Maps anxiety, no losing 30 minutes to traffic. You wake up, walk 4 minutes to coffee, walk 6 more minutes to lunch, walk back via the Ashram for evening meditation.

The mental shift this triggers is the actual point of the trip. By Day 3 you stop checking your phone for directions; by Day 5 you've forgotten which day of the week it is.

**On the Cluny Embroidery Centre specifically**: this is one of White Town's most quietly rewarding stops and one of the easiest to walk straight past. It's a working convent-tradition embroidery workshop — you can watch the linens being made rather than just buy the finished product, and prices run noticeably below what the same items cost once a boutique hotel gift shop has marked them up. Budget 20-30 minutes, and pair it with the Pondicherry Museum a few doors down for a single unhurried White Town cultural loop that costs nothing but time.

Booking direct via [our hotels page](/hotels) means you specify the room.

**How to choose between the five.** If your priority is being inside the working spiritual heart of the city — closest to the Ashram, able to hear the sea from your courtyard — **Palais de Mahe** is the pick, and its CGH Earth sister property **Maison Perumal** is the better choice if you'd rather anchor in the Tamil Quarter and treat White Town as the daily walk rather than the home base. **Le Dupleix** wins on pure hotel amenity — the courtyard pool is genuinely the best in the city, and the bar is the destination in its own right for an evening drink even if you're not staying there. **Villa Shanti** and **La Villa** are the two picks for travellers who want the White Town aesthetic without the top-tier heritage price tag — Villa Shanti leans modern-minimalist and has the stronger brunch scene, while La Villa is the better pure value play if budget is the deciding factor.

**Booking timeline.** For November-February travel, aim to book these five properties at least 3-4 months ahead — longer if your dates fall on a weekend, since the recurring Bangalore weekend crowd (a 3-4 hour drive away) fills White Town's tiny heritage inventory fast. Shoulder months (October, March) have noticeably more flexibility, sometimes bookable 4-6 weeks out.

**Beyond the five.** If all five are full for your dates, White Town has a second tier of smaller guesthouses and homestays that don't carry the same heritage-brand recognition but often sit on the same streets — search [our hotels page](/hotels) for live White Town availability rather than defaulting to a generic mid-range hotel outside the French Quarter, which undoes the entire point of a slow stay here.

**What you actually get for the price difference.** At the top of the range, Palais de Mahe and Maison Perumal charge for restoration quality as much as service — original lime-plaster walls, reclaimed Chettinad teak doors, antique furniture sourced from the same regional estates the buildings themselves once belonged to. Le Dupleix sits just below that on room rate but wins on shared amenity — its courtyard pool and bar are genuinely destination-grade, which matters if you're the kind of traveller who spends more waking hours in the hotel's public spaces than in the room itself. Villa Shanti and La Villa strip back the antique-furniture layer but keep the essential White Town formula — a walled courtyard, a small pool or plunge pool, breakfast worth getting up for — at a price point that lets a 7-night stay pencil out against a UK travel budget without the trip becoming solely about the hotel.

**A note on noise and light**, since it catches first-time White Town guests off guard: these are 200-year-old buildings on public streets, not resort compounds set back from traffic. Street noise (motorbikes, the occasional temple procession, roosters at dawn) reaches interior courtyard rooms more than a purpose-built modern hotel would allow. Ask specifically for a room facing the internal courtyard rather than the street if light sleeping is a concern — every one of the five properties above can accommodate that request if you flag it at booking rather than on arrival.

## 5. The spiritual heart: Sri Aurobindo Ashram <a id="ashram"></a>

The Ashram isn't a tourist site — it's a living practice, founded in 1926 by Sri Aurobindo and run after his 1950 passing by **The Mother (Mirra Alfassa)** until her own death in 1973. Today's residents number around 2,000 disciples spread across 80 buildings in White Town. They run schools, farms, ateliers, the Auroville township 12 km north, and a publishing house that's still in print.

**What to do**: visit the **Samadhi** (the joint shrine where Sri Aurobindo and The Mother are buried, in the Ashram main building's courtyard, free entry, 8am-12pm and 2pm-6pm). Sit in silence for 10 minutes. Read one of the Mother's *Conversations* on the bench afterwards. That's the "tourist visit." For UK travellers genuinely interested, the deeper engagement is reading, not visiting — *The Mother on Education*, *Letters on Yoga*, the *Synthesis of Yoga*.

**The Concierge angle**: Don't just walk in. Ask your hotel — especially **Palais de Mahe** and **Maison Perumal**, both run by CGH Earth which has long-running ashram relationships — to introduce you to a resident practitioner. A 30-minute conversation about The Mother's philosophy with someone who's been practising for 40 years is worth more than five tourist visits.

**Dress and conduct**, in more detail than the FAQ box: shoulders and knees covered for both men and women, no sleeveless tops (a light scarf solves this instantly if you're wearing one), no photography inside the Samadhi itself, and no loud conversation anywhere on the grounds. The atmosphere is genuinely different from a typical Indian temple visit — there's no ticket booth energy, no vendors, no queue-jostling. Staff are soft-spoken but will firmly and immediately correct anyone treating the space casually, and that correction is entirely fair; this is 2,000 people's daily spiritual home, not a photo backdrop.

**Beyond the Samadhi**: the Ashram's broader campus includes a bookstore (the Sri Aurobindo Ashram Press publishes The Mother's and Sri Aurobindo's complete works, sold at genuinely reasonable prices — a better souvenir than almost anything in the White Town boutiques), a small handicrafts outlet selling Ashram-made goods, and — for visitors who arrange it through their hotel in advance — occasional access to talks or readings open to respectful outside guests. None of this is heavily signposted; asking your hotel concierge is the reliable way in, not wandering and hoping.

## 6. Auroville & the Matrimandir (12km north) <a id="auroville"></a>

Founded 1968 by The Mother as an experimental township for "human unity in diversity." Today: ~3,300 residents from 60 nationalities, no money in the traditional sense (residents use the Auro Card credit system), governed by community consensus, surrounded by reforested land that was a barren plateau when The Mother started.

The headline attraction is the **Matrimandir** (literally "Temple of the Mother") — a 30-metre golden geodesic dome at the geometric centre of Auroville, completed in 2008 after 37 years of construction. Inside is a meditation chamber lit by a single sunbeam through a quartz crystal sphere; outside is gardens designed around the Mother's twelve "qualities."

**Two ways to visit**:

- **Viewing Point** (free, walk-up): watch a 10-minute orientation video at the Visitors Centre, get a viewing pass, walk a shaded path 100m to a raised garden looking at the dome. About 90 minutes round-trip from the Visitors Centre.
- **Inner Chamber** (free but pre-booked): a 30-minute silent meditation inside the dome itself. Slots limited to ~30-40 per day. **Pro-tip**: have your hotel call the booking centre 48 hours ahead — hotels with established relationships secure slots their guests can't always book independently.

**Beyond the Matrimandir**, Auroville rewards a proper half-day rather than a drive-by. The **Visitors Centre** itself has a small exhibition on the township's founding philosophy and a bookshop worth 15 minutes. **Auroville Beach** is a genuine, uncrowded stretch of sand a short drive from the centre — bring a book, not a party mindset. The **Solar Kitchen** (Auroville's largest communal dining hall, powered by one of the town's solar bowl installations) serves an inexpensive, largely vegetarian lunch to residents and respectful visitors alike — around £4 for a filling plate. Artisan workshops worth a stop include **Auroville Bakery** and **Bread & Chocolate** for genuinely excellent French-style pastry, **Auro Pottery** for ceramics, and **Maroma** for incense and home fragrance — all Auroville-founded businesses whose profits feed back into the township's own development funds rather than a private owner's pocket, which is worth knowing if you'd like your souvenir spending to mean something.

**Getting around Auroville itself**: the township is spread over a wide area and its internal roads are sandy, unpaved in stretches, and genuinely easy to get lost on without local knowledge. Most visitors either book a half-day driver through their hotel (the simplest option) or rent a bicycle at the Visitors Centre for a slower, more atmospheric exploration of the immediate Matrimandir-and-Solar-Kitchen area — do not attempt the wider township on foot in the midday heat.

Plan a half-day at minimum, a full day if you want to add Serenity Beach or a longer sit at the Viewing Point garden. Return to White Town by 6pm for the seafront promenade, which is when the city's own evening rhythm kicks in and the day genuinely rounds itself off.

## 6. Paradise Beach & Serenity Beach <a id="beaches"></a>

Pondicherry's own Promenade is a sunset-walk beach, not a swimming one — rocky, atmospheric, built for the evening stroll every local and visitor does rather than for a day lying in the sand. For the actual beach day, two options cover very different moods.

**Paradise Beach** is the postcard version — a genuinely soft, clean sandy stretch reached by a short boat ride across a backwater from **Chunnambar**, about 8km south of the city centre. The boat itself (roughly a 15-20 minute crossing, around ₹300 / £3 return) is part of the appeal, gliding through a calm backwater channel lined with casuarina trees before opening onto open sea and sand. Once there: sun loungers, a couple of simple food stalls, watersports available seasonally (jet-ski, banana boat), and genuinely swimmable water. It gets busier on weekends with the domestic day-tripper crowd from Chennai and Bangalore, so a weekday visit — or an early-morning boat before 10am — gets you the beach closer to empty. Budget half a day; it doesn't reward rushing, but it also doesn't need a full day the way Auroville does.

**Serenity Beach**, north of the city towards Auroville, is an entirely different register — the surf, yoga and beach-café hub that's grown up alongside Pondicherry's digital-nomad and wellness crowd. Board rentals, sunrise surf lessons, a cluster of relaxed beachfront cafés serving smoothie bowls and strong coffee rather than the resort-day-trip food stalls of Paradise Beach. It's less curated, more genuinely local-feeling, and pairs naturally with an Auroville morning since it's roughly on the route. Currents here can run stronger than Paradise Beach's more sheltered stretch, so stick to areas with visible lifeguard presence or ask your hotel or a surf-school operator about current conditions before swimming beyond the shallows.

**Which one for your itinerary**: if you have one beach day in a 5-7 day trip, Paradise Beach is the higher-consensus pick — easier logistics (a fixed boat crossing rather than an open beach to navigate), calmer water, and a clearer "day trip" shape that fits neatly into the itinerary below. If you're staying longer, want to try surfing, or are the kind of traveller who prefers an unpolished local hangout to a curated day-trip destination, add Serenity Beach as a second, more casual half-day, ideally combined with the Auroville visit on the same outing.

## 7. Bharti Park & the Promenade <a id="bharti-park"></a>

**Bharat Park** (widely written and signposted as Bharti Park) is the green square at the literal centre of White Town, fronting the **Puducherry Legislative Assembly** building and the **Raj Nivas** — the old French Governor's residence, still in official use today, which means you'll see it from outside rather than tour its interior. It's not a headline attraction on the level of the Ashram or the Matrimandir, and it doesn't need to be: it's a genuinely pleasant green pause point where locals walk dogs, jog and gather at dawn and dusk, sitting within a few minutes' walk of the Promenade, the Ashram, and several of the boutique hotels covered above.

The honest advice on Bharti Park is to treat it as a natural loop-in on any White Town walk rather than a special-trip destination — 15-20 minutes is enough, ideally at the golden-hour end of the day when the light through the park's mature trees is genuinely lovely and the evening cross-section of Pondicherrian life (families, joggers, the odd cricket game) is at its most active.

**The Promenade (Goubert Avenue)** deserves its own mention beyond Bharti Park. It runs the full length of White Town's sea frontage, closed to traffic in the evenings, and functions as the city's genuine communal living room — everyone from Ashram residents to White Town hoteliers to visiting Bangalore families does the same evening walk along the same stretch of sea wall. It's rocky rather than sandy (see the beaches section above for where to actually swim), but as a daily ritual it's the single most reliable way to feel the rhythm of the city rather than just look at it. The **Gandhi Statue** and the **Old Lighthouse** both sit along this stretch and are worth the extra five minutes if you haven't seen them on an earlier pass.

## 8. French colonial cuisine meets Tamil cooking <a id="cuisine"></a>

Pondicherry's food identity is the clearest expression of its dual heritage, and the smart way to eat here is to lean into both rather than pick a lane.

**The French Quarter register**: proper croissants and pain au chocolat (genuinely good, not the sad approximation found elsewhere in India), filter coffee served the French way alongside South Indian filter kaapi, steak-frites, ratatouille, and — the genuinely distinctive local specialty — Franco-Tamil fusion dishes that blend French technique with South Indian spice and ingredient logic. This is the food of White Town's cafés and the heritage-hotel dining rooms, and it's priced accordingly (a full meal running £8-15 per person at the upper end).

**The Tamil Quarter register**: crossing the canal changes the food entirely. Dosa, idli, banana-leaf thalis, filter kaapi as the everyday drink rather than a novelty, and fiery Chettinad-adjacent curries that don't appear on a single White Town menu. This is dramatically cheaper — a full thali lunch at a genuine Tamil Quarter eatery runs £2-3, not £15 — and arguably more representative of what most of Pondicherry actually eats day to day.

**The seafood angle**, specific to the Coromandel coast: Pondicherry sits directly on the Bay of Bengal, and fresh-caught seafood is a strength of both registers — French-technique grilled fish and prawns at the White Town restaurants, and simpler, spice-forward Tamil coastal preparations (fish curry, prawn masala) in the Tamil Quarter and at the more local-facing beach food stalls near Paradise Beach and Serenity Beach. Ask any hotel concierge for that day's catch recommendation rather than defaulting to the printed menu — the best seafood meals here are usually the ones built around what came in that morning.

**The smart daily pattern**: French breakfast, Tamil lunch, whichever mood strikes for dinner. This isn't just a novelty pairing — it genuinely reflects how Pondicherry itself eats, and doing the same gives you both registers of the city's food culture rather than staying in the (more expensive, less representative) French Quarter bubble the whole trip.

## 9. The work-from-sabbatical café cluster <a id="cafes"></a>

Pondicherry is the digital nomad capital of South India. The café cluster runs through White Town with reliable WiFi, plug points, and the kind of unhurried café culture that lets you sit for 4 hours without anyone asking you to leave.

- **Café des Arts** (rue Suffren). Best WiFi in the city, authentic French breakfast (real croissants, proper coffee). Daytime: laptop crowd. ~£3 for coffee + croissant, ~£8 a full meal.
- **Coromandel Café** (rue Romain Rolland). Set in a heritage mansion with garden seating. Pink guava sangrias, brunch eggs, the strongest mid-day vibe in the city.
- **Bread & Chocolate** (Auroville campus). Organic French bakery, pastries that actually rival what you'd get in Paris, slow service in the right way.
- **Le Dupleix Bar** (in Le Dupleix Hotel). Best evening cocktails in the French Quarter, courtyard tables under banyan trees.
- **Surguru** (rue Suffren). No WiFi, no English menu, no concessions to tourism. Best Tamil vegetarian food in Pondicherry. Perfect for a "phones-down" lunch — you'll eat with your hands off a banana leaf, drink filter coffee at the end, and pay £2.50 for the privilege.

The day rhythm: 8am Café des Arts breakfast (laptop, write, plan) → 11am back to your hotel or a walk to the Promenade → 1pm Surguru lunch (phones down) → 3-5pm work or Coromandel café reading → 6pm Promenade walk to Le Dupleix Bar → 7:30pm dinner → 9pm back to your hotel for ashram-side silence.

**Why this cluster works for genuine remote work, not just tourist café-hopping**: reliable in-café power outlets are the norm rather than the exception at Café des Arts and Coromandel Café, WiFi speeds are consistently good enough for video calls in both, and — crucially — nobody rushes you to turn a table over, which is the single biggest difference between Pondicherry's café culture and a typical Indian tourist-town coffee shop. This is exactly why the city has quietly become a 1-3 month base for the wider remote-work and digital-nomad crowd rather than just a 3-day tourist stop.

## 10. Best months to visit <a id="best-months"></a>

**October through March** is the prime window — cool dry weather in the 22-30°C range, low humidity, and both the Ashram and Auroville running their full normal schedules rather than a rain-curtailed one.

**November and February** are the specific sweet spots within that window: noticeably smaller crowds than the December-January peak (when Indian domestic holiday travel is at its highest), and weather that's genuinely more comfortable than the April-May shoulder, which can climb to 38°C with rising humidity.

**April-May** is workable if your UK travel dates are constrained — front-load outdoor activity (Ashram, Auroville, walking tours) into the early morning before 10am, and treat the afternoon as pool or café time rather than sightseeing time.

**June-September, the southwest monsoon**, is the window to avoid if you have any flexibility at all: heavy rain, high humidity, occasional cyclone warnings along the Coromandel coast, and if you're combining the trip with an Andaman Islands extension, ferry cancellations are common. Auroville's outdoor elements (the Viewing Point garden walk, the wider township exploration) are considerably less pleasant in heavy rain, and Paradise Beach's boat crossing can be suspended in rough weather.

**Festival timing worth knowing**: Pondicherry's French heritage means **Bastille Day (14 July)** sees a modest but genuine local celebration in White Town, despite falling inside the less favourable monsoon window — worth knowing if you have a personal reason to be there on that date specifically, but not worth restructuring an otherwise October-March trip around. **Pongal** (mid-January, the Tamil harvest festival) adds real village and Tamil Quarter colour if your dates land inside the December-January peak-crowd window anyway.

## 11. Getting there from the UK <a id="getting-there"></a>

**The route**: Fly London Heathrow to **Chennai (MAA)** direct on **British Airways** — roughly 10 hours, and the only reliable direct UK service into the region. Off-peak return fares land around £550; peak December pricing climbs to £750 or more, so booking outside the December-New Year surge saves meaningfully. [Compare flights to Chennai](/flights) on JetMeAway to check live pricing across the booking window that suits your dates.

**The onward transfer**: from Chennai, it's a 3-hour drive south along the scenic **East Coast Road (ECR)**, with the Bay of Bengal on your left for most of the journey. Two realistic options — a private car, bookable through your hotel for roughly £25-30, which is the option almost every White Town heritage property arranges without fuss, or the **Tamil Nadu State Transport bus** from Chennai's CMBT terminal, around ₹150 (£1.50) and 4 hours, the budget option for travellers comfortable with a public-transport transfer after a long-haul flight.

**Pondicherry's own airport (PNY)** exists but shouldn't anchor your travel plan — it sees occasional small-carrier flights from Hyderabad and Bangalore, but routes and schedules change frequently enough that Chennai remains the dependable gateway for a UK-originating trip.

**If you're combining Pondicherry with the wider Tamil coast**, the same Chennai gateway and ECR route serves Mahabalipuram (90 minutes south of Chennai, before you reach Pondicherry) and Tranquebar (a further 3 hours south of Pondicherry) — see our [Mahabalipuram & Tranquebar guide](/blog/mahabalipuram-tranquebar-tamil-coast-guide-2026) for a route that sequences all three on one trip.

**Booking window**: 10-16 weeks ahead is the standard sweet spot for this route on British Airways, tightening to 4-5 months out if your dates fall over Christmas, New Year or the Indian Pongal/Republic Day period in mid-January, when both airfare and White Town's tiny hotel inventory come under simultaneous pressure.

## 12. Survival checklist for UK travellers <a id="survival"></a>

- **Getting there**: Fly London → **Chennai (MAA)** direct on **British Airways** (~£550 return). Then a private car for the 3-hour drive south via the **East Coast Road (ECR)** — book through your hotel for ~£25, or take the Tamil Nadu State Transport bus from Chennai's CMBT (₹150 / £1.50, 4 hours). Pondicherry's own airport (PNY) gets occasional small-carrier flights from Hyderabad and Bangalore but routes change frequently — Chennai is more reliable.
- **The alcohol hack**: Pondicherry is a tax-haven for spirits because alcohol duties are far lower in this Union Territory than in Tamil Nadu. World-class cocktails at Le Dupleix and Promenade bars cost half of Chennai equivalents. Bottle prices in liquor shops are 30-50% cheaper than mainland India.
- **Dress code**: Pondicherry is liberal — beachwear at Auroville Beach is fine, mid-thigh skirts and short sleeves are unremarkable everywhere except the Ashram. The **Ashram requires modest dress** (shoulders and knees covered) — bring a shawl or scarf as a quick-cover layer. Hindu temple visits in the Tamil Quarter follow standard Indian temple norms.
- **Sunday Bazaar**: MG Road becomes a giant market on Sundays — fresh produce, brassware, textiles, the actual texture of Tamil city life. Stay at **Maison Perumal** to be on the scene by 7am, or walk over from White Town in 10 minutes if you're staying east.
- **Cash vs card**: ATMs everywhere, UK debit cards (Monzo, Starling, Wise) work. Most cafés take cards; the small Tamil-Quarter restaurants are cash-only.
- **eSIM**: see the FAQs above. Airalo works reliably in Pondicherry; backup with a local Airtel SIM at Chennai airport.
- **Getting around White Town**: walk. It's flat, shaded, and every core sight sits within 10-15 minutes of the Promenade. Rent a bicycle from your hotel for the Tamil Quarter crossing or short Auroville-adjacent hops; book a car through your hotel for Auroville itself and the beach day trips.
- **Booking timeline**: lock the hotel at the same time as the flight, not after. The five heritage picks above (10-15 rooms each) fill for weekends months ahead in peak season.
- **Matrimandir inner chamber**: ask your hotel to call the Auroville booking centre 48 hours ahead rather than trying to book independently on short notice — this is the single most common trip-planning miss.
- **Health and insurance**: confirm current India travel-health guidance with a UK travel clinic or GP well ahead of departure, and arrange comprehensive travel insurance covering medical treatment and activities before you fly. This is general orientation, not medical advice.
- **Vegetarian and dietary needs**: genuinely easy here — the Tamil Quarter is naturally vegetarian-strong, and White Town cafés all carry vegetarian options even on French-leaning menus.

## 13. The 7-day Pondicherry shape <a id="seven-day-itinerary"></a>

A genuinely-relaxed week from London:

- **Day 1**: London → Chennai overnight on BA. Land Chennai 11am, private car 11:30am, arrive Pondicherry 2:30pm. Check in. Promenade walk at sunset. Dinner at Coromandel Café.
- **Day 2**: Settle into White Town. Sri Aurobindo Ashram morning visit. Café des Arts brunch. Walk Cluny Embroidery Centre + Pondicherry Museum. Bharti Park at golden hour. Promenade sunset.
- **Day 3**: Auroville morning (Visitors Centre, Matrimandir Viewing Point, Solar Kitchen lunch, beach). Return to Pondi 4pm.
- **Day 4**: Tamil Quarter immersion. Goubert Market 8am. Surguru lunch. Walk the temple streets. Sunday Bazaar if it's a Sunday.
- **Day 5**: Slow day. Hotel pool morning (Le Dupleix or Palais de Mahe). Boutique browsing rue Romain Rolland. Massage at the spa.
- **Day 6**: Matrimandir inner chamber if your hotel secured the slot. Otherwise Paradise Beach day-trip (60-min boat ride, ₹300 / £3 round-trip from Chunnambar). Serenity Beach as an add-on if you're up for a second, more casual beach stop.
- **Day 7**: Slow morning. Final coffee at Café des Arts. Private car to Chennai 12pm. Evening flight back to London.

For the splurge version, swap two White Town nights for a closing night at **Palais de Mahe** with a sea-view room and a 90-minute Ayurvedic massage. For the budget version, stay all 7 nights at **La Villa** (~£100/night) and skip the Auroville inner chamber.

**The 5-day version**, for travellers whose UK leave doesn't stretch to a full week: compress Days 2 and 4 above into a single day (Ashram morning, Tamil Quarter afternoon), keep the Auroville half-day as-is, and choose either Paradise Beach or the spa/boutique day rather than both. You lose the Matrimandir inner chamber attempt (the 48-hour advance booking window is tight against a 5-day trip) but keep every other headline experience.

**The 3-day version**, for a stopover or a taster ahead of a longer return trip: Day 1 arrival and Promenade/Ashram; Day 2 full Auroville half-day plus Tamil Quarter afternoon; Day 3 slow White Town morning (cafés, Cluny Embroidery Centre, Bharti Park) before the return transfer to Chennai. This genuinely works as a "is this destination for me" taster, and most travellers who do the 3-day version come back planning a longer stay.
