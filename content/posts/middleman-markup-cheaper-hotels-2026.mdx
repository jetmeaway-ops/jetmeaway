## Why the Same Hotel Room Costs Different Prices

Search the exact same room, the exact same hotel, the exact same two nights on five different websites, and you'll typically see five different numbers. Nothing about the room changed — same bed, same view, same cancellation terms in most cases. What changed is which layer of the hotel distribution chain sold you that price.

Hotel distribution runs in layers, roughly like this: the property sets a base rate and contracts inventory in bulk to one or more **bed banks** (wholesale B2B distributors). Those bed banks redistribute the inventory to travel businesses — comparison engines, travel agents, tour operators — who each add their own margin before showing you a final number. A large retail OTA sits furthest from the source and typically carries the heaviest markup, because it's funding an enormous marketing and technology operation on top of the room cost. A wholesale-direct comparison engine sits much closer to the source, so there's less margin stacked on top by the time the price reaches your screen.

Three forces drive the gap:

- **Commission pressure.** Large online travel platforms charge hotels a significant commission on every booking made through them — typically 15-25% of the room rate. Hotels build that cost into the rate they show on those channels, and the traveller pays it without ever seeing it itemised.
- **Wholesale rates most travellers never see.** Bed banks buy inventory in volume at negotiated net rates well below what any individual consumer could negotiate. Those rates exist and are entirely legitimate — they're just hard to find unless the site you're using connects to that layer directly.
- **The illusion of a single "best" site.** Because a handful of big-brand OTAs dominate search results and advertising, most travellers assume the first familiar name they land on is automatically the cheapest. It's frequently not — it's just the most visible.

None of this makes big OTAs dishonest. They provide real infrastructure: 24/7 multilingual support, huge inventory breadth, loyalty programmes, buyer protection. That infrastructure costs money, and the commission funds it. The point isn't that OTAs are a scam — it's that their price is one option among several, and it's rarely the cheapest one for a specific room on a specific night.

## How Wholesale Rate Contracts Actually Work

A wholesale rate contract is the deal struck between a hotel (or a hotel group) and a bed bank. Understanding the mechanics explains why the wholesale price can be meaningfully lower than retail, without anyone cutting corners on the actual room.

**The volume-for-rate trade.** A hotel commits a block of rooms — sometimes a fixed allotment, sometimes a soft commitment with cancellation windows — to a bed bank at an agreed net rate. In exchange, the bed bank brings distribution reach the hotel couldn't easily build alone: thousands of downstream travel businesses, many of them in markets or niches the hotel has no direct sales presence in. The hotel is effectively pre-selling a slice of future occupancy at a rate that's lower per-room than its best public rate, in exchange for near-certainty that those rooms will fill.

**Net rate vs sell rate.** The wholesale net rate is what the hotel actually receives per room, after the bed bank's own margin (typically a smaller, volume-driven cut rather than the 15-25% a retail OTA charges). Downstream distributors — including JetMeAway's LiteAPI, RateHawk and Webbeds integrations — then add their own retail margin on top of the net rate to arrive at the price shown to you. Because that stack has fewer, thinner layers than the traditional big-OTA path, the price at the end is usually lower for the identical room.

**Why this isn't a "secret" or a loophole.** Wholesale hotel distribution is a mainstream, decades-old part of the travel industry — it's how most package holidays, tour operators and white-label travel sites have sourced inventory for years. What's changed in the last decade is that comparison-engine technology has matured enough for smaller, more transparent platforms to connect directly to that wholesale layer via API, rather than relying on retail-affiliate links to big-name OTAs. ## Real Markup Ranges by Hotel Tier: 5% to 25%

The "how much is the markup" question doesn't have one answer — it moves with property tier, city, season and which specific OTA you're comparing against. Based on how hotel-distribution commission structures typically work across the market, here's the realistic range by tier.

| Hotel tier | Typical retail markup over wholesale net rate | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Budget / economy (1-2 star, hostels) | 5-10% | Thin margins to begin with; OTAs compete hard on price to win volume in this segment |
| Midscale (3 star) | 8-14% | Standard commission bands apply; less brand-trust premium than upscale properties |
| Upper-midscale (4 star) | 12-18% | Meaningful brand and loyalty-programme overhead baked into the retail price |
| Luxury (5 star, boutique) | 18-25%+ | High demand, fewer rooms to fill, strong brand-trust premium OTAs charge for |
| Peak-season / high-demand city (any tier) | Add 3-8 percentage points | Dynamic pricing widens the absolute gap even when the percentage stays similar |

A few things worth understanding about this table. First, the markup is a *percentage of a moving number* — as the underlying wholesale rate rises with demand, the retail markup rises in cash terms even if the percentage itself doesn't change, which is why the gap feels bigger during peak season and around major events. Second, luxury properties carry the highest percentage markup precisely because OTA traffic is worth more to them — a five-star hotel with 120 rooms benefits enormously from the visibility a major OTA provides, and is willing to pay for it, and that cost lands in your price. Third, budget properties sit at the low end not because OTAs are being generous, but because thin margins force tighter commission negotiation — there's less room to mark up a rate that's already close to the property's cost floor.

None of these figures are guarantees on any single booking — always verify against a live comparison for your specific dates. But as a planning heuristic, expect the gap to widen as you move up in star rating and as you move into peak-demand windows.

## How to Spot a Marked-Up Hotel Rate

You don't need a comparison engine open at all times to develop an eye for when a price looks inflated. A few tells:

**The cancellation-to-price mismatch.** A fully-refundable, same-day-cancellation rate that's priced only slightly above a non-refundable rate for the same room is often carrying a heavier markup — flexibility is genuinely valuable, but if the gap between flexible and non-flexible is unusually small, the flexible rate is frequently just the platform's standard (marked-up) price with a flexible label attached, rather than a true premium for the flexibility itself.

**"Member exclusive" badges on already-standard rates.** Some large retail platforms show an inflated "public rate" crossed out next to a "member" or "signed-in" price that's actually just their normal rate. If creating a free account instantly drops the price by 10-15%, that's a strong signal the uncrossed price was never a genuine market rate to begin with.

**Round, suspiciously tidy numbers on dynamic-pricing platforms.** Wholesale and true dynamic rates tend to land on odd numbers (£71, £134, £248) because they reflect a live calculation. A price that lands suspiciously close to a round number across multiple different search dates can indicate a platform applying a flat markup percentage to whatever the underlying rate is, rather than passing through a genuinely live number.

**Compare at least two sources before booking anything above a night or two.** The single most reliable way to spot a markup is the boring one: open the same room and dates on two different platforms — ideally one retail OTA and one wholesale-direct comparison engine like JetMeAway — before you commit. If the numbers match closely, you've found a fair market price. If one is meaningfully higher for identical terms, you've found your markup.

**Check for resort fees and city tax hidden at the final step.** These aren't technically a "markup" in the distribution sense — they're separate charges levied by the property or local authority — but a headline price that looks cheap and then grows substantially at checkout is a different kind of price-inflation problem worth watching for regardless of which channel you're booking through.

## When the Big OTA Actually Wins

Direct and wholesale don't win every time, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Three genuine cases where a large OTA like Expedia, Trip.com or Agoda is the better or only choice:

**Independent boutiques and B&Bs without wholesale distribution.** Many smaller, family-run properties don't have a bed-bank contract at all — they rely on OTA traffic for the bulk of their bookings and either don't run a competitive direct-booking engine or don't have wholesale coverage through LiteAPI, RateHawk or Webbeds. In that case, the retail OTA listing may be the only route to book that specific hotel, and it isn't overpriced relative to any alternative — it's simply the property's main channel.

**Genuine last-minute flash discounts.** In the final 24-72 hours before a stay, OTAs sometimes discount unsold rooms sharply to avoid an empty inventory night — a hotel would rather sell at a steep discount than sell nothing. These short-window deals can briefly undercut even the wholesale rate. If your dates are genuinely last-minute and flexible, it's worth checking a retail OTA's "tonight" or "last-minute" deals section alongside the standard comparison.

**Time-limited loyalty or promotional pricing.** Big OTA loyalty programmes occasionally run promotional pricing — a members-only sale window, a booking-app-exclusive discount — that temporarily beats the standing wholesale rate for a specific hotel and date. These are real, if inconsistent, and worth catching when they appear.
