## The Honest Price Picture: When Salzburg Costs What

This deserves its own section, because it is the single most useful thing in this guide.

The 49 rates above were sampled for **late August**, which is very close to the worst week of the year to buy a Salzburg hotel room. Here is why. Salzburg has **two enormous demand spikes**, and both are cultural rather than seasonal-weather events, which means you can plan around them.

The first and biggest is the **Salzburg Festival (Salzburger Festspiele)**, one of the most important classical music and opera festivals in the world. It runs for around five weeks from **late July into the end of August** and brings a wealthy international audience that books the best hotels months in advance and holds rates at their annual peak. That is the whole story. A baroque town of Salzburg's size does not naturally sustain £187 median summer room rates — it sustains them for the weeks the festival and the markets are on, and then it does not.

## Where to Stay in Salzburg: Choosing Your Base

Salzburg is small, and split by the **Salzach**. Almost everything a first-time visitor wants sits within a compact core either side of the river, so the choice of base is really a choice between paying for the old town, paying a little less to be across the river from it, or paying least to be a bus ride out. Here is the honest breakdown.

**The Altstadt (left bank)** is the postcard and the priciest place to sleep. This is the UNESCO-listed baroque old town under the Hohensalzburg Fortress: the cathedral and Residenzplatz, Getreidegasse with Mozart's birthplace, the DomQuartier, the churches and the squares. Staying inside it buys you the district at seven in the morning and again late in the evening, before and after the day crowds — genuinely the best version of Salzburg. The costs are real: the hotel stock is small and heavily boutique, so it prices at the very top and sells out first; most of the quarter is pedestrianised, so you cannot drive to the door; and in festival season it is at capacity. **Altstadthotel Kasererbräu** is the clearest old-town pick in this guide, with **Am Neutor** a genuinely central budget option on its western edge.

**The Neustadt (right bank)** is the practical, better-value centre. This is the right-bank half of the historic core, built around **Mirabell Palace and Gardens** — the ones every Sound of Music tour photographs — with the Mozart Residence, the Kapuzinerberg hill above and a good spread of restaurants. It holds a lot of the city's mid-range and four-star hotels and it is only a short walk across the **Makartsteg** footbridge to the old town. For most visitors who want to be central without paying Altstadt prices, this is the sweet spot: **Hotel Sacher, Hotel Bristol, the Sheraton Grand, both IMLAUER hotels, Dorint City-Hotel** and the well-reviewed **Altstadt Hotel Hofwirt** all sit on this side.

**The station district (Hauptbahnhof)** to the north is where the affordable, useful hotels cluster. It is not pretty, but it is genuinely practical: the Munich and Vienna trains arrive here, the airport buses stop here, and the old town is a 15 to 20 minute walk or a couple of stops on the frequent buses. For a budget-conscious visitor without a car, this is the most sensible base in the city. **H+ Hotel, Atel Hotel Lasserhof, Das Jedermann, Mercure Salzburg City** and **NH Collection Salzburg City** are the picks here.

**The northern and western outer districts** — Lehen, Itzling, Liefering, Maxglan and the airport belt — are cheaper again and built around drivers. Rooms are larger, parking is easy and the rates are the lowest in the guide, but you trade a walkable centre for a bus or a short drive in. **Hotel Turnerwirt, B&B Hotel Salzburg-Nord, Hotel Lehenerhof, Hotel Kohlpeter, Hotel Restaurant Auerhahn** and the **Leonardo Hotel Salzburg Airport** live out here. Treat the airport ones as flight-night hotels; the closer-in northern ones work as budget bases if you accept the commute.

**The southern approaches** — Nonntal, Aigen, Herrnau and out along the Alpenstraße — are leafier and a little out, with the same drivers'-base logic and easy access to the lakes and mountains to the south. **ARCOTEL Castellani** (a former monastery with a garden), **Austria Classic Hotel Hölle** and **Amadeo Hotel Schaffenrath** sit on this side, and they suit a trip that mixes the city with day trips into the Salzkammergut and Berchtesgaden.

The short version: **for a first trip, base yourself in the Altstadt or the Neustadt and walk everywhere.** For the same city at a lower price, take the station district and ride the bus in. Only go to the outer districts and the airport if you are driving, on a tight budget, or catching an early flight — and check the walk or the bus time on the live page before you book, because it is the single thing that decides whether a low number is a bargain or a daily chore.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Salzburg

Start where the city shows off. **The Altstadt** is one of the best-preserved baroque city centres north of the Alps, and its heart is the great open square-sequence of **Domplatz, Residenzplatz and Kapitelplatz** around the **Salzburg Cathedral (Dom)** — a vast early-baroque church with a marble façade and an interior built for the archbishops who ran Salzburg as an independent church-state for centuries. The **DomQuartier** museum route links the cathedral, the Residenz state rooms and the abbey galleries in one ticket and is the best single way to understand why this small city is so grand. A few streets over, **Getreidegasse** is the famous shopping lane — narrow, tall, hung with wrought-iron guild signs even over the modern chain stores — and at number 9 is **Mozart's Birthplace (Geburtshaus)**, the yellow building where Wolfgang Amadeus was born in 1756 and now a museum.

Then go up. The **Hohensalzburg Fortress** is the enormous white castle on the Festungsberg that dominates every view of the city, one of the largest fully preserved medieval fortresses in Europe. You can walk up the steep path in 15 to 20 minutes or take the **FestungsBahn funicular** from Festungsgasse, and the reward is the definitive view over the baroque rooftops to the Alps. Inside are state rooms, a couple of museums and the medieval fortress kitchens. Across the river, the **Kapuzinerberg** and the smaller **Mönchsberg** give quieter, greener walks with their own viewpoints — the Mönchsberg has the **Museum der Moderne** and a lift up from the old town if you would rather not climb.

**The Mozart industry is real, and worth navigating with your eyes open.** Mozart was born here, and the city has built a substantial visitor economy on it: the Birthplace on Getreidegasse and the **Mozart Residence (Wohnhaus)** across the river are both genuine museums worth doing, the **Mozartkugel** chocolate is sold on every corner (the original hand-made version comes from **Café Fürst** in the old town; the shiny foil-wrapped ones everywhere else are the mass-market spin-off), and Mozart concerts run year-round. It is touristy, and it is also the actual biography of the actual composer — take the two museums and as much or as little of the chocolate and the concerts as you like.

**The Sound of Music is the other industry, and here honesty helps.** The 1965 film was shot in and around Salzburg, and the bus tours to **Mirabell Gardens**, **Schloss Leopoldskron**, **Hellbrunn** (the gazebo), **Nonnberg Abbey** and **Mondsee** church are enormously popular with international visitors — for a great many people it is the reason they came, and if you love the film you will love the tour. The honest local footnote is that **the film is barely known among Salzburgers** — many have never seen it — and the whole phenomenon is a visitor thing, not a local one. None of that makes it less fun if it is your thing; it just means you lose nothing by skipping it and seeing the same lovely places on foot if it is not.

**On the edges of the city, two things are worth the short trip.** **Hellbrunn Palace**, just south, is a 17th-century pleasure palace famous for its **Wasserspiele** — the trick fountains an archbishop built to soak his dinner guests, still working and still soaking visitors, which makes it the best family hit in Salzburg on a hot day. And **Schloss Mirabell** back in the centre has the gardens that are free to wander and glorious in summer. Further out, the working salt mines at **Hallein (Salzwelten)** — salt is the "Salz" in Salzburg and the source of its historic wealth — take you underground on miners' slides, and the **Untersberg cable car** from St. Leonhard lifts you to around 1,800 metres for an enormous view over the city and into Bavaria (take warm layers; the summit is far colder than the valley).

**And the lakes.** The **Salzkammergut** lake district begins just east of the city, and it is one of the best reasons to base yourself here: **Wolfgangsee, Mondsee and Fuschlsee** are all within about 30 to 50 minutes, and **Hallstatt**, the impossibly photogenic lakeside village, is a full day out (around 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 30 each way). Go to Hallstatt early — it is tiny and gets extremely crowded by mid-morning in summer. Just over the German border, **Berchtesgaden**, the **Königssee** and the **Eagle's Nest** make another popular day trip.

**The food is a genuine pleasure, not an afterthought.** Seek out **Salzburger Nockerl**, the city's signature dessert — a huge sweet baked soufflé in three peaks meant to echo the hills, big enough to share — along with **Wiener Schnitzel**, **Tafelspitz** (boiled beef) and **Kasnocken** (Austrian cheese dumplings). The **Augustiner Bräustübl**, a vast monastery-run beer hall where you pour your own from wooden barrels and bring food from the stalls, is an experience in its own right and much loved by locals. And **Café Sacher** does the famous **Sachertorte** if you want the full old-Salzburg café ritual.

## Getting Around Salzburg

**Walking is the answer for the centre.** Salzburg is one of the most walkable small cities in Europe: the old town, the fortress hill, Mirabell Gardens and most of the sights sit within a compact core either side of the Salzach, and you can cross the whole historic centre on foot in 20 to 25 minutes. Once you are based centrally you will barely use public transport at all. Good shoes and a light waterproof matter more than any travel pass.

**The bus network (Obus)** — Salzburg runs a clean, frequent electric trolleybus system — covers everywhere the walk does not, and it is what you use to reach the outer hotels, the airport and the edges of the city. Buy a single ticket from the machine or driver, or a 24-hour ticket if you are staying out of the centre and riding in daily; tickets are cheaper bought in advance from a Tabak-Trafik kiosk than from the driver. There is no metro or tram — the bus is the system.

**From the airport**, Salzburg Airport (SZG) is only about **4 kilometres west** of the centre, so it is a quick, cheap trip: city buses **2 and 10** run from outside the terminal to the main station and the edge of the old town in around 20 minutes for the price of a normal ticket, and a taxi takes 10 to 15 minutes. If you have flown into **Munich** instead, direct **airport-to-Salzburg buses** and the train via Munich Hauptbahnhof both take around **1 hour 30 to 2 hours** — a genuinely easy connection that many UK visitors use.

**By rail**, Salzburg Hauptbahnhof is a major stop on the Munich–Vienna line: fast trains reach **Munich in around 1 hour 30 to 1 hour 50** and **Vienna on the railjet in about 2 hours 20**, which makes both cities easy add-ons or arrival points. **Do not hire a car for the city itself** — the centre is walkable, much of the old town is pedestrianised or restricted, and parking is pushed into paid garages on the edge. Hire a car only for the lakes and mountains, remember that Austrian motorways require a **toll vignette** (buy it at the border or online) that Germany does not, and consider picking the car up on the day you leave town rather than paying to park it unused during your city days. Austria drives on the right.

**Two money habits worth building.** First, unlike North America, **VAT is already in the price** on menus and shelves, so the number you see is broadly the number you pay — the only extra on a hotel bill is a small per-person **city tourist tax** of a couple of euros a night, which some hotels fold into the rate and others add at checkout. Second, **tipping is modest**: round up the bill or add 5 to 10 per cent for good service in restaurants, told to the server as you pay rather than left on the table, round up a taxi fare, and leave a euro or two for a porter. Nobody here works for tips the way they do in the United States.

**Can I do a day trip to Hallstatt or the lakes from Salzburg?**
Yes, and it is one of the best reasons to base yourself here. The Salzkammergut lake district begins just east of the city: the Wolfgangsee, Mondsee (whose church features in the Sound of Music wedding scene) and Fuschlsee are all within about 30 to 50 minutes by car or bus. Hallstatt, the much-photographed lakeside village, is farther — around 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 30 each way by car, or a longer bus-and-boat or train-and-ferry combination — so it makes a full day rather than a half. Go early, because Hallstatt in particular is small and gets extremely crowded by mid-morning in summer.

**Can I visit the salt mines or the Untersberg?**
Both, and both are excellent half-days. Salzburg means 'salt castle' and salt is why the city is rich — the working show mines at Hallein (Salzwelten), about 20 minutes south, take you underground on miners' slides and across an underground lake, and are a reliable hit with children. The Untersberg, the great massif that looms over the city to the south, has a cable car from St. Leonhard that lifts you to around 1,800 metres for an enormous view over the city and into Bavaria; take warm layers because the summit is much colder than the valley. Berchtesgaden and the Eagle's Nest, just over the German border, are a longer but popular day out.

**Is Munich a good way to reach Salzburg?**
It is often the best way. Munich Airport has far more UK connections than Salzburg, especially outside the winter ski season, and Salzburg is only about 1 hour 30 minutes away — there are direct trains from Munich Hauptbahnhof (around 1 hour 30 to 1 hour 50), direct express buses, and an easy drive on the A8 autobahn. Plenty of UK visitors fly into Munich, spend a night or two there, then continue to Salzburg by train, and the two cities pair naturally on one trip. If you go by car, remember Austria requires a motorway vignette and Germany does not.

**Do I need to speak German in Salzburg?**
No. German is the official language and the local dialect is Austrian rather than the textbook German you may have learned, but Salzburg is a major international tourist city and English is widely spoken in hotels, restaurants, shops and at the sights. A few words go a long way socially — Grüß Gott is the standard Austrian hello, danke is thank you — and using them is appreciated, but you will never be stuck. Menus and signs in the tourist core are very often bilingual.

**Is Salzburg safe for tourists?**
Yes. Salzburg is a small, prosperous and very safe city by any international standard, and the overwhelming majority of visitors have an entirely uneventful trip. The realistic risks are the ordinary ones of any busy tourist destination: pickpocketing in the most crowded parts of the old town and at the station in high season, and slips on wet cobbles or winter ice. Use normal city sense with bags and phones in crowds, take care on the steep paths up to the fortress and the Kapuzinerberg in the wet, and check the current UK Foreign Office travel advice for Austria before you go.

**How much should I tip in Salzburg?**
Modestly, and less than in North America. In restaurants and cafés the convention is to round up the bill or add roughly 5 to 10 per cent for good service, told to the server as you pay rather than left on the table — you state the total you want to pay when you hand over the money or card. Round up a taxi fare, leave a euro or two for a hotel porter, and a couple of euros a day for housekeeping if you wish. Service is not run on tips the way it is in the United States, so there is no need to over-think it.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Salzburg?**
Yes — it is excellent, and you should drink it. Salzburg's tap water comes largely from protected alpine and groundwater sources and is high quality straight from the tap, so there is no need to buy bottled water for your room. Public drinking fountains around the city run clean mountain water too. Carry a refillable bottle and you will save money and plastic; asking for tap water — Leitungswasser — in a restaurant is fine, though a small charge is not unheard of.

**Do Salzburg hotels add taxes and fees on top of the rate?**
A little. Austrian VAT is already included in the quoted room rate, so unlike North America the headline price is close to the real price. On top of it, the city levies a small local tourist or accommodation tax per person per night — a couple of euros a night — which some hotels include in the rate and others add at checkout. A few properties charge separately for parking or breakfast. It is minor compared with the taxes stacked onto a North American hotel bill, but check the total on the live page rather than the headline rate before you compare two hotels.

**Is Salzburg good for families?**
It is a manageable and rewarding family city. The centre is compact and walkable, the fortress with its funicular and museums is a reliable hit, the Hellbrunn trick fountains are built to soak children on a hot day, the Haus der Natur museum is genuinely good in any weather, and the salt mines and Untersberg cable car sit a short trip away. The one honest caveat is hotel stock: family rooms in the historic centre are limited and pricey in summer, so families often do best in the mid-range and budget hotels near the station or the outer roads, where rooms are larger and cheaper and the bus in is short.

**What should I eat in Salzburg?**
A few local things are worth seeking out. Salzburger Nockerl is the city's signature dessert — a huge, sweet baked soufflé in three peaks meant to echo the hills around the city, big enough to share. Wiener Schnitzel, Tafelspitz (boiled beef) and Kasnocken (Austrian cheese dumplings) anchor the savoury side, and the Augustiner Bräustübl, a vast monastery-run beer hall, is an experience in itself. On the sweet side the Mozartkugel — a pistachio-marzipan and nougat chocolate ball — is everywhere; the original hand-made version comes from Café Fürst in the old town, while the foil-wrapped ones in every shop are the mass-market spin-off. Café Sacher does the famous Sachertorte.
