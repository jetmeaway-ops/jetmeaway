## Rishikesh is a dry, vegetarian holy city — read this before you book

This is the thing most people find out too late, so we will say it plainly. **Rishikesh is a holy city and alcohol is officially prohibited.** There are no bars. Hotels — including the expensive resorts — do not run a licensed bar, and you should not expect wine with dinner. Do not carry alcohol into an ashram or drink it openly anywhere in town. If a few cold beers by the river is part of your idea of a holiday, this is not the destination, and it is far better to know that now than on your first evening.

The same goes for food: **Rishikesh is largely vegetarian**, by custom and by regulation. The overwhelming majority of restaurants, cafés and hotel kitchens serve pure vegetarian food only, and eggs are inconsistent — some traveller cafés serve them, plenty do not. In practice this is much less of a hardship than it sounds. The cooking is genuinely good and enormously varied, from thalis and dosas to the German Bakery-style cafés around Tapovan doing porridge, falafel and banana pancakes for a long-stay international crowd.

**Dress modestly.** Cover shoulders and knees, especially at temples, ghats and ashrams. You will see other travellers ignoring this; it does not make it acceptable, and loose cotton clothes are cheap to buy on any street here. **Be respectful at the ghats and ashrams**: take your shoes off where asked, do not use soap or shampoo in the river, do not photograph people bathing or at prayer without permission, and follow each ashram's own rules on silence, meal times and separate areas. No smoking near temples. The Ganges is treated as a deity in Rishikesh, not as scenery, and the town works much better when you approach it on those terms.

## What to See and Do in Rishikesh

The **Ganga Aarti** is the evening centrepiece — the river ceremony of lamps, fire and chanting performed daily at sunset. The two best-known settings are **Triveni Ghat** in the town centre, which is bigger and busier, and **Parmarth Niketan** on the Swarg Ashram side, which is more structured and popular with visitors. It is free, it happens every day, and it is the thing most people remember.

Crossing the river is an event in itself, over the **Lakshman Jhula** and **Ram Jhula** suspension footbridges — narrow, swaying pedestrian bridges shared with motorbikes and the occasional cow. Ram Jhula links Swarg Ashram to Muni Ki Reti; Lakshman Jhula sits further upstream near Tapovan. Repair works have affected access in recent years, so check locally which crossing is open when you arrive.

Then there is the yoga. Rishikesh calls itself the **Yoga Capital of the World** and backs it up with hundreds of schools and ashrams, from drop-in morning classes you can simply turn up for to month-long teacher training courses that need booking many months ahead. Every March the town hosts the **International Yoga Festival**, which is wonderful if you want to attend and worth avoiding if you want a quiet Rishikesh. Nearby, the **Beatles Ashram (Chaurasi Kutia)** — the abandoned site where the band studied meditation in 1968 — is now a paid attraction of overgrown domed meditation huts, a ruined hall and layers of later graffiti art.

On the water, **white-water rafting** on the Ganges is the other reason people come. The season runs roughly **September to June**, with commercial rafting **closed through the monsoon**; the standard tourist stretches are beginner-friendly, but book a licensed operator, take the safety briefing seriously and check your insurance covers adventure activities. The same corridor of road does **bungee jumping** and cliff jumping if you want more of that.

Further out: **Neelkanth Mahadev Temple** up in the hills, an important Shiva shrine reached by a winding drive or a stiff walk; **Kunjapuri**, the classic sunrise viewpoint with Himalayan peaks on a clear morning; and forest and waterfall walks upstream around Phool Chatti. Rishikesh is also the **gateway to the Himalaya** — the starting point for treks and for the **Char Dham** pilgrimage routes towards Kedarnath and Badrinath — and **Haridwar**, the other great Ganges pilgrimage city, is about **45 minutes away** and easy as a day trip.

## Where to Stay in Rishikesh

**Tapovan and Lakshman Jhula** is where most visitors should start. This is the yoga-and-café side of town: the schools, the shops selling malas and harem trousers, the long-stay traveller crowd, and the great bulk of the cheap guesthouses and hostels in this guide. It is busy, sociable and walkable, and if you are here for classes it saves you a daily commute.

**Ram Jhula and Swarg Ashram** is quieter and more devotional. This is ashram territory — Parmarth Niketan, the aarti, temples, simple vegetarian canteens and an earlier bedtime. Choose it if you want the spiritual side of Rishikesh rather than the backpacker side, and be aware that some ashram accommodation comes with rules on timings and behaviour.

**Muni Ki Reti and the riverside** is where the smarter hotels sit — the boutique riverside properties, the ones with pools and ghats of their own. It is a little removed from the noise, close to Ram Jhula, and the natural choice if you want a comfortable room with a view of the water rather than a room above a café.

**Rishikesh town centre, near Triveni Ghat**, is the practical option: temples, markets, the biggest evening aarti, and the best transport connections for buses, taxis and the railway station. It feels more like an ordinary Indian town and less like a yoga village, which some people prefer. The five-star resorts, by contrast, sit well outside town in the hills — wonderful, but you will be driving to everything.

## Rishikesh Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Rishikesh?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party "visa services" charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Rishikesh?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work at resorts and larger cafés, but carry cash for autorickshaws, ashram donations, small guesthouses and market shopping.

**Is alcohol available in Rishikesh?**
No. Rishikesh is a holy city and alcohol is officially prohibited — there are no bars, hotels do not run a licensed bar, and you should not expect a glass of wine with dinner even at the five-star resorts. Do not bring alcohol into ashrams or drink openly. If a night out with drinks is part of what you want from a holiday, Rishikesh is not that destination, and it is better to know that before you book.

**Can I eat meat in Rishikesh?**
Largely no. Rishikesh is a vegetarian city by custom and regulation, and the overwhelming majority of restaurants, cafés and hotels serve pure vegetarian food only. Eggs are inconsistent — some cafés serve them, many do not. The upside is that the vegetarian cooking is excellent and varied, from thalis and dosas to the traveller cafés around Tapovan doing everything from falafel to banana porridge.

**How do I get to Rishikesh from the UK?**
There are no direct international flights to Rishikesh. The nearest airport is Dehradun / Jolly Grant (DED), roughly 35 minutes' drive away, which is served by domestic flights within India. Most UK travellers fly to Delhi and then either take a domestic hop to Dehradun, or travel overland — roughly six hours by train or road, usually via Haridwar.

**What is the nearest airport to Rishikesh?**
Dehradun's Jolly Grant Airport (DED), about 35 minutes by road. It is a domestic airport with connections to Delhi and other Indian cities, so you will be connecting rather than flying in from the UK directly. Hotels can arrange an airport transfer, and prepaid taxis are available at the terminal.

**Can I reach Rishikesh by train?**
Yes, though the usual approach is to take a train to Haridwar, which is about 45 minutes away by road and has far better rail connections, then continue by taxi or bus. Rishikesh has its own small station and the newer Yoga Nagari Rishikesh terminus, but services are more limited. From Delhi, allow roughly six hours overall.

**When is the best time to visit Rishikesh?**
September to November and February to April. Those are the two comfortable windows — clear Himalayan foothill air, warm days, cool evenings, and the Ganges running at a good level for rafting. October and March are especially popular, and March also brings the International Yoga Festival, so book earlier if you are travelling then.

**What is the monsoon like in Rishikesh?**
July to September is the monsoon and it changes the trip completely. The Ganges runs high and brown, commercial rafting is closed, landslides can block mountain roads and treks, and heavy rain is a daily fact. Room rates drop and the hills are green and beautiful, but do not plan a rafting or trekking holiday in that window.

**Is Rishikesh cold in winter?**
December and January are genuinely cold, especially at night and early morning by the river — this is the Himalayan foothills, not the plains. Days are often bright and pleasant, but pack proper layers, and check that your room has heating, because plenty of budget guesthouses do not. Sunrise yoga on a January morning is a chilly business.

**When is the rafting season in Rishikesh?**
Roughly September to June, with the river closed to commercial rafting through the monsoon months of July to August, and sometimes into September depending on conditions. The most reliable stretches are autumn and spring. Always book with a licensed operator, wear the helmet and life jacket provided, and be honest about your swimming ability.

**Is white-water rafting in Rishikesh safe?**
It is a well-established, heavily regulated activity with thousands of runs each season, and the standard tourist stretches are graded for beginners. The risks are real but manageable: use a registered operator rather than the cheapest tout, insist on a proper safety briefing, wear the full kit, and never go out on a high or rising river. Check your travel insurance covers adventure activities.

**Do I need to book a yoga course in advance?**
For drop-in classes, no — Tapovan and Lakshman Jhula are full of schools running daily classes you can simply turn up for. For a structured multi-week course, especially a teacher training programme, yes, book well ahead: the good schools fill months in advance, and the season around the March Yoga Festival is the busiest of all.

**What is the International Yoga Festival?**
An annual festival held in Rishikesh in March, with classes, talks and demonstrations from teachers across many traditions. It draws large numbers of international visitors, which means accommodation across Tapovan and Swarg Ashram gets tight and prices rise. If you want to attend, book both the festival and your room early; if you want a quiet Rishikesh, avoid that fortnight.

**What is the Ganga Aarti?**
The evening river ceremony, in which lamps are offered to the Ganges with chanting, bells and fire, performed daily at sunset. In Rishikesh the two best-known settings are Triveni Ghat in the town centre, which is larger and busier, and Parmarth Niketan on the Swarg Ashram side, which is more structured and popular with visitors. It is free to attend.

**What is the Beatles Ashram?**
Chaurasi Kutia, the abandoned ashram where the Beatles stayed in 1968 while studying meditation. It sits inside Rajaji forest land on the far bank and is now open as a paid site — overgrown domed meditation huts, a ruined hall and a great deal of later graffiti art. It is atmospheric rather than polished, and worth an hour or two.

**What are Lakshman Jhula and Ram Jhula?**
The two famous suspension footbridges over the Ganges. Ram Jhula links the Swarg Ashram side to Muni Ki Reti, and Lakshman Jhula sits further upstream near Tapovan. They are pedestrian bridges shared with motorbikes and the occasional cow, they sway, and they are the main crossing points for anyone staying on the ashram side. Access has been affected by repair works in recent years, so check locally which crossing is open.

**Is Rishikesh safe for solo and female travellers?**
Rishikesh is one of the easier places in India for solo travellers, with a large long-stay foreign community, plenty of hostels and a generally calm, sober atmosphere — helped by the fact that it is a dry city. Ordinary precautions still apply: avoid unlit riverside paths at night, be firm with anyone offering unsolicited "guidance", keep valuables secure in dorms, and read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go.

**What should I wear in Rishikesh?**
Dress modestly. Cover shoulders and knees, especially at temples, ghats and ashrams, and avoid vest tops and short shorts in town even though you will see other travellers wearing them. Loose cotton is comfortable and easy to buy locally. Bring something warm for evenings and a swimsuit only if your hotel has a pool — the ghats are not for sunbathing.

**How should I behave at the ghats and ashrams?**
Quietly and respectfully. Remove your shoes where asked, do not use soap or shampoo in the river, do not photograph people bathing or praying without permission, and follow whatever the ashram's own rules are on silence, meal times and gender-separated areas. No alcohol or smoking anywhere near a temple or ashram. The river is a deity here, not a water feature.

**How many days do I need in Rishikesh?**
Three to four days for most visitors. That gives you a rafting run, a couple of yoga classes, an evening Ganga Aarti, the Beatles Ashram, the two bridges and a sunrise trip up to Kunjapuri, without rushing. Stay longer if you are doing a structured yoga course, or if you are using Rishikesh as the base for a Himalayan trip further north.

**Can I drink the tap water in Rishikesh?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only, and go easy on ice at small stalls until your stomach has settled. Many cafés and hostels now offer refill stations with filtered water, which is cheaper and cuts down on plastic, so bring a reusable bottle. Do not drink from the river, however holy it is.

**Which area should I stay in in Rishikesh?**
Tapovan and Lakshman Jhula for the yoga schools, traveller cafés and the bulk of the cheap guesthouses and hostels. Ram Jhula and Swarg Ashram for ashrams, temples and a quieter, more devotional feel. Muni Ki Reti and the riverside for the smarter resorts with views and pools. Rishikesh town centre near Triveni Ghat for temples, markets, the aarti and the best transport connections.

**Is Rishikesh good for families?**
It works well for families with older children — rafting has minimum age limits with most operators, and the bridges, temples and forest walks are engaging. It is less suited to very young children, because the town is hilly, the bridges are narrow and busy, and the riverbanks are unfenced. The riverside resorts at Muni Ki Reti are the easiest base with kids.

**Which side of the road do they drive on in India?**
The left, as in the UK — but almost nobody visiting Rishikesh self-drives. The town is compact and hilly, parking near the bridges is difficult, and autorickshaws, shared vikrams and app cabs cover everything cheaply. For day trips to Kunjapuri, Neelkanth or Haridwar, hiring a car with a driver is normal and inexpensive.

**Do Rishikesh hotels charge extra fees?**
There is no resort fee, but Indian hotel rates carry GST, which varies by room-rate band, and some properties price breakfast separately or add a service charge. Ashram-style stays and retreats may bundle meals and classes into the rate instead. Always check what is included on the hotel's live page before comparing two similar-looking rates.
