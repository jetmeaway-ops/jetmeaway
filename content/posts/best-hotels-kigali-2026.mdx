  - q: "Is it true that plastic bags are banned in Rwanda?"
    a: "Yes, and it is enforced. Rwanda has a well-known ban on single-use plastic bags, and bags can be confiscated at the airport on arrival — luggage is sometimes checked for them. Do not pack single-use plastic bags, and repack anything wrapped in them before you fly. Use fabric or paper bags instead. It is one of the reasons the country is so noticeably free of litter."
  - q: "What is Umuganda and will it affect my trip?"
    a: "Umuganda is Rwanda's national community work morning, held on the last Saturday of every month. Much of the country, Kigali included, effectively shuts down for the morning while residents take part in community clean-up and building projects. Shops, offices and many attractions open late, and traffic is restricted. It is not a problem — just plan a slow hotel morning and start sightseeing after lunch on that date."
  - q: "Should I visit the Kigali Genocide Memorial?"
    a: "Yes. The Kigali Genocide Memorial at Gisozi is the resting place of more than 250,000 victims of the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi, and it is the single most important place in the city. It is a sombre, dignified and emotionally heavy visit, and it explains more about modern Rwanda than anything else you will see. Entry is free with a paid audio guide available. Dress and behave respectfully, keep your voice low, and be careful about photography — this is a burial site, not a sight."
  - q: "How much does gorilla trekking cost and how do I book?"
    a: "Mountain gorilla permits for Volcanoes National Park are expensive, strictly limited in number and must be booked well in advance through the Rwanda Development Board or a licensed tour operator. Permit prices are set by the government and change, so check the official current price rather than any figure you read in an old blog. Trekking is a real hike at altitude in forest terrain, so bring boots and be honest about your fitness."
  - q: "How far is Volcanoes National Park from Kigali?"
    a: "Roughly two to three hours by road to Musanze and the park headquarters, depending on traffic out of the city. Many visitors sleep in Kigali the night before, leave very early and trek the same day, but treks start early in the morning so staying closer to the park the night before is more relaxed. Kigali is the natural arrival and departure city either way."
  - q: "Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Rwanda?"

  - q: "Do I need malaria tablets for Kigali?"
    a: "Kigali sits at around 1,500 metres, and malaria risk in the city is lower than in the lowland parts of the country — but malaria is present elsewhere in Rwanda, including areas you may pass through on the way to Volcanoes National Park, Nyungwe Forest or Lake Kivu. Get advice from your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly, and pack repellent and long sleeves for dusk. This is general information, not medical advice."
  - q: "When is the best time to visit Kigali and Rwanda?"
    a: "The long dry season from June to September is the most reliable window, and the short dry season from December to February is the other good one. Those are also the easier months for gorilla trekking, because the forest trails are less muddy. The rains make everything greener and cheaper but the trekking harder, and Kigali itself is comfortable year-round thanks to the altitude."
  - q: "What is the weather like in Kigali?"
    a: "Mild and surprisingly comfortable for a city almost on the equator, because it sits at around 1,500 metres across a series of hills. Days are warm rather than baking and evenings can feel genuinely cool, so pack a light jacket or fleece. Rain, when it comes, tends to arrive in heavy bursts rather than all-day drizzle."

  - q: "What language is spoken in Kigali?"

  - q: "Is Kigali walkable?"
    a: "Parts of it are, but be realistic: the city is built across a series of steep hills, and what looks like a short walk on a map can be a long uphill climb in the sun.   - q: "How do I get around Kigali?"
    a: "Ride-hailing apps and registered taxis work well and are inexpensive by UK standards. Buses run set routes with tap cards. Hotels will also arrange a driver by the half-day, which is the easiest option for a packed sightseeing day."
  - q: "Is Kigali expensive?"

  - q: "What should I do in Kigali in one day?"

  - q: "Do they drive on the left in Rwanda?"
    a: "No — Rwanda drives on the right, unlike the UK, Kenya and Tanzania. Roads in and around Kigali are in notably good condition and traffic is orderly by regional standards, but the hills, the motos and the unfamiliar side of the road mean most short-stay visitors are better off with a taxi or a hired driver."
  - q: "Is tipping expected in Rwanda?"
    a: "It is appreciated rather than obligatory. Rounding up a taxi fare, leaving five to ten per cent in a restaurant, and tipping guides, drivers and trackers on a gorilla trek are all normal. Small notes in Rwandan Francs are the most useful thing to carry for this."

## What to See in Kigali

Begin, as almost every visitor does, at the **Kigali Genocide Memorial** at Gisozi. More than **250,000 victims of the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi** are buried in its gardens, and the memorial exists both as their resting place and as a careful, unflinching account of how the country arrived at those hundred days and what has happened since. **Entry is free, with a paid audio guide** that is worth taking. Give it a full morning and expect it to be emotionally heavy: there are rooms here — particularly the children's room — that stay with people for years. Dress modestly, keep your voice low, follow the guidance on photography, and remember that Rwandans visiting the same gardens may be visiting their own family. Whatever else you do in Kigali, do this first; nothing else in the city makes proper sense without it. Nearby, the **Camp Kigali Belgian Peacekeepers Memorial** marks where ten Belgian UN soldiers were killed at the start of the genocide, ten stone columns standing for the ten men.

Then let the city itself show you what it has become. Kigali is repeatedly named among **Africa's cleanest and safest capitals**, and it is not marketing — the streets are swept, the verges are planted, and there is almost no litter anywhere. Part of the reason is **Umuganda**, the national community work morning held on the **last Saturday of every month**, when much of the country stops and residents turn out to clean, build and repair together. If your visit lands on that Saturday, plan a slow start: shops and offices open late and traffic is restricted until around midday. It is a genuinely interesting thing to witness, and a genuinely annoying thing to be caught out by.

For everyday Kigali, spend a morning at **Kimironko Market**, the city's biggest, where the fabric section alone is worth the trip and tailors will run you up something from a length of kitenge in a day or two. **Inema Arts Center** in Kacyiru is a working studio and gallery founded by two Rwandan brothers, with resident artists, live music some evenings and work you can actually buy; **Niyo Arts Gallery** in Kiyovu pairs a gallery with a school programme funded by sales. The **Nyamirambo Women's Center** runs excellent walking tours of Nyamirambo, the city's oldest and liveliest quarter, led by local women — the single best way to see how Kigali actually lives. And Rwanda grows superb coffee it historically exported rather than drank, so the modern café scene is a real pleasure: **Question Coffee** is the best-known of them, sourcing from women-led cooperatives.

Kigali is also the **gateway to mountain gorilla trekking**. **Volcanoes National Park**, on the Virunga chain in the far north-west, is roughly **two to three hours' drive** from the city, and it is where the world's most famous gorilla families live. Be clear-eyed about the practicalities: **permits are expensive, strictly limited in number and must be booked well in advance through the Rwanda Development Board** or a licensed operator, and the trek itself is a real hike at altitude on steep, often muddy forest ground. It is, by common consent, worth every bit of the money and effort. Further afield, **Nyungwe Forest** offers chimpanzees and a canopy walkway, and **Lake Kivu** gives you beaches, boat trips and lakeside towns like Gisenyi and Kibuye. For planning, the official tourism site [Visit Rwanda](https://www.visitrwanda.com/) covers parks, permits and events, and you should read the current [UK FCDO travel advice for Rwanda](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/rwanda) before you book — it carries the up-to-date entry rules, health requirements and safety guidance.

## Choosing Where to Stay in Kigali

- **Kiyovu** — the smartest and most central of Kigali's hills, quiet, leafy and full of embassies, good restaurants and the city's more characterful boutique hotels. The easiest all-round base for a short visit.
- **Nyarutarama** — green, calm and residential, wrapped around the golf course and the lake. The quietest place to sleep in Kigali, with the trade-off that you will drive to most things.
- **Kacyiru** — the government, embassy and convention district, home to the Kigali Convention Centre and Inema Arts Center. The natural choice for business travel and conferences.
- **Remera** — good value, close to Kigali International Airport and the stadium, with a mix of newer hotels and cheaper rooms. Sensible for a late arrival or an early flight.
- **City centre (Nyarugenge)** — downtown, with the most walkable budget rooms, banks, bus links and everyday shops on the doorstep. Busier and less green than the hills above it, but the cheapest place to base yourself.

## Practical Honesty — What Nobody Warns You About

**Kigali is hilly, and walking it is a workout.** The city is spread over a series of steep ridges and valleys, and a two-kilometre walk on a map can be a long climb in the sun. Pavements are good and the city feels safe on foot by day, but budget more time and more energy than the distance suggests, and use moto taxis for anything uphill — they are cheap, regulated and by far the fastest way around.

**It is notably safe, but not magic.** Kigali's reputation for safety is deserved and most visitors remark on how relaxed they feel here compared with other cities in the region. Take normal precautions anyway: keep valuables out of sight, use registered taxis or an app late at night, and do not leave bags unattended.

⚠️ **Do not pack single-use plastic bags.** Rwanda has a well-known **ban on single-use plastic bags**, and it is enforced — bags can be confiscated at the airport, and luggage is sometimes checked on arrival. Repack anything wrapped in plastic before you fly and use fabric or paper bags instead. It is one of the reasons the country looks the way it does.

A few more: Rwanda **drives on the right**, not the left, so watch the traffic when you cross. **English is an official language** alongside Kinyarwanda and is used everywhere in hotels and business, so you will get by easily. And at around **1,500 metres**, evenings can turn genuinely cool — pack a light jacket even for a trip that is nominally to equatorial Africa.

## Kigali Hotels FAQs

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Rwanda?**
Rwanda is one of the more open African destinations for visitors. UK passport holders can generally get a **visa on arrival** at Kigali International Airport, or **apply online in advance** through the **Rwanda Directorate General of Immigration and Emigration**. Fees, validity and the exact process change from time to time, so check the current rules on the official immigration site and the UK FCDO travel advice page for Rwanda before you book.

**What currency is used in Kigali?**
The **Rwandan Franc (RWF)**. Cards are accepted in hotels, larger restaurants and supermarkets, and mobile money is used widely by locals, but you will want some cash for markets, moto taxis and small cafés. The pound figures in this guide are rough conversions for guidance only, not exact quotes — check live rates before you travel.

**Are there direct flights from London to Kigali?**
Yes. **RwandAir flies direct from London to Kigali International Airport (KGL)**, and there are plenty of one-stop options via the Gulf carriers, Brussels and Nairobi. Airline schedules and routings change from season to season, so always check current timetables and live prices for your dates rather than assuming last year's schedule still runs.

**Is Kigali safe for tourists?**
Kigali is widely described as one of the **cleanest and safest capital cities in Africa**, and most visitors are struck by how calm and orderly it feels, including after dark. That is not a reason to switch your brain off: take the same precautions you would in any city — keep valuables out of sight, use registered taxis or ride-hailing apps late at night, and do not leave bags unattended. Read the current UK FCDO travel advice for Rwanda before you go.

**Is it true that plastic bags are banned in Rwanda?**
⚠️ Yes, and it is enforced. Rwanda has a well-known **ban on single-use plastic bags**, and bags **can be confiscated at the airport** on arrival — luggage is sometimes checked for them. Do not pack single-use plastic bags, and repack anything wrapped in them before you fly. Use fabric or paper bags instead. It is one of the reasons the country is so noticeably free of litter.

**What is Umuganda and will it affect my trip?**
**Umuganda** is Rwanda's national community work morning, held on the **last Saturday of every month**. Much of the country, Kigali included, effectively shuts down for the morning while residents take part in community clean-up and building projects. Shops, offices and many attractions open late and traffic is restricted. It is not a problem — just plan a slow hotel morning and start sightseeing after lunch on that date.

**Should I visit the Kigali Genocide Memorial?**
Yes. The **Kigali Genocide Memorial** at Gisozi is the resting place of **more than 250,000 victims of the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi**, and it is the single most important place in the city. It is a sombre, dignified and **emotionally heavy** visit, and it explains more about modern Rwanda than anything else you will see. **Entry is free, with a paid audio guide** available. Dress and behave respectfully, keep your voice low, and be careful about photography — this is a burial site, not a sight.

**How much does gorilla trekking cost and how do I book?**
Mountain gorilla permits for **Volcanoes National Park** are **expensive, strictly limited in number and must be booked well in advance through the Rwanda Development Board** or a licensed tour operator. Permit prices are set by the government and change, so check the official current price rather than any figure you read in an old blog. Trekking is a real hike at altitude in forest terrain, so bring boots and be honest about your fitness.

**How far is Volcanoes National Park from Kigali?**
Roughly **two to three hours by road** to Musanze and the park headquarters, depending on traffic out of the city. Many visitors sleep in Kigali the night before, leave very early and trek the same day, but treks start early in the morning so staying closer to the park the night before is more relaxed. Kigali is the natural arrival and departure city either way.

**Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Rwanda?**
⚠️ It depends on your route. A **yellow fever certificate may be required** if you are arriving from or transiting a country with yellow fever transmission risk, and some onward countries want proof of vaccination if you have been in the region. Requirements change, so check with a travel clinic and the official guidance well before departure.

**Do I need malaria tablets for Kigali?**
⚠️ Kigali sits at around **1,500 metres**, and **malaria risk in the city is lower** than in the lowland parts of the country — but **malaria is present elsewhere in Rwanda**, including areas you may pass through on the way to Volcanoes National Park, Nyungwe Forest or Lake Kivu. Get advice from your GP or a **travel clinic** well before you fly, and pack repellent and long sleeves for dusk. This is general information, not medical advice.

**When is the best time to visit Kigali and Rwanda?**
The **long dry season from June to September** is the most reliable window, and the **short dry season from December to February** is the other good one. Those are also the easier months for gorilla trekking, because the forest trails are less muddy. The rains make everything greener and cheaper but the trekking harder, and Kigali itself is comfortable year-round thanks to the altitude.

**What is the weather like in Kigali?**
Mild and surprisingly comfortable for a city almost on the equator, because it sits at around **1,500 metres** across a series of hills. Days are warm rather than baking and evenings can feel genuinely cool, so **pack a light jacket or fleece**. Rain, when it comes, tends to arrive in heavy bursts rather than all-day drizzle.

**Can I drink the tap water in Kigali?**
Stick to **bottled or properly filtered water**. Most hotels provide bottled water or a filtered dispenser, and many have refill stations — Rwanda takes waste seriously, so refilling is easy and welcomed. Use bottled water for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach.

**How many days do you need in Kigali?**
**Two full days** covers the city properly: the Kigali Genocide Memorial and the Camp Kigali memorial on one day, and Kimironko Market, the Inema Arts Center, a Nyamirambo walking tour and the coffee shops on the other. If you are adding **gorilla trekking**, build in at least two more days, and more again if you want Nyungwe Forest or Lake Kivu.

**Which area of Kigali should I stay in?**
**Kiyovu** and **Nyarutarama** are the smartest, quietest and greenest parts of the city and hold most of the best hotels. **Kacyiru** is the embassy and government district and suits business trips. **Remera** offers good value and sits close to the airport and the stadium. The **city centre (Nyarugenge)** has the most walkable budget rooms and the best transport links. Kigali is spread over hills, so pick your base by what you want to be near rather than by map distance.

**What language is spoken in Kigali?**
**Kinyarwanda** is the national language and is what you will hear on the street, but **English is an official language** and is widely used in hotels, restaurants, business and signage, and French is still spoken by many older Rwandans. You will have no trouble getting by in English, and learning *muraho* for hello and *murakoze* for thank you goes down very well.

**Is Kigali walkable?**
Parts of it are, but be realistic: the city is built across a series of **steep hills**, and what looks like a short walk on a map can be a long uphill climb in the sun. Pavements are good and it feels safe to walk by day, which is not true of every capital in the region. For anything more than a few hundred metres, take a taxi or a moto.

**How do I get around Kigali?**
Ride-hailing apps and registered taxis work well and are inexpensive by UK standards. **Moto taxis** — motorbikes with a spare helmet — are the local workhorse and by far the quickest way across the hills, and Kigali's are regulated and metered in a way that surprises most visitors. Buses run set routes with tap cards. Hotels will also arrange a driver by the half-day, which is the easiest option for a packed sightseeing day.

**Is Kigali expensive?**
Food, transport and budget rooms are cheap by UK standards, and a good meal out costs a fraction of a London equivalent. Hotel rooms at the top end are less of a bargain than you might expect, because a lot of Kigali's inventory is built around conferences and business travel. **Gorilla permits are the genuinely expensive part** of any Rwanda trip and should be budgeted separately from everything else.

**What is Hotel des Mille Collines?**
It is a long-established four-star hotel in central Kigali that **sheltered more than a thousand people during the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi**, and became known internationally through the film *Hotel Rwanda*. It operates as a normal working hotel today, with a well-known pool terrace and gardens. Guests who stay there should treat its history with respect rather than as a tourist attraction.

**What should I do in Kigali in one day?**
Start at the **Kigali Genocide Memorial** in the morning and give it real time. Have lunch and a coffee somewhere calm afterwards, because you will need it. In the afternoon visit **Kimironko Market** for fabric and produce, then the **Inema Arts Center** or Niyo Arts Gallery, and finish with a drink somewhere with a view over the hills as the lights come on.

**Do they drive on the left in Rwanda?**
No — **Rwanda drives on the right**, unlike the UK, Kenya and Tanzania. Roads in and around Kigali are in notably good condition and traffic is orderly by regional standards, but the hills, the motos and the unfamiliar side of the road mean most short-stay visitors are better off with a taxi or a hired driver.

**Is tipping expected in Rwanda?**
It is appreciated rather than obligatory. Rounding up a taxi fare, leaving five to ten per cent in a restaurant, and tipping guides, drivers and trackers on a gorilla trek are all normal. Small notes in Rwandan Francs are the most useful thing to carry for this.

**Can I use Kigali as a base for the rest of Rwanda?**
Yes, and most people do. Kigali is central, the roads are good, and it is the arrival point for almost every visitor. From here it is around **two to three hours north to Volcanoes National Park** for gorilla trekking, a longer drive west and south to **Nyungwe Forest** for chimpanzees and the canopy walk, and a scenic run west to **Lake Kivu** for beaches and quiet lakeside towns.
