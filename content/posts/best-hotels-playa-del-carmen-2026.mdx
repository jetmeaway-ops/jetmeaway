  - q: "How do I get from Cancún airport to Playa del Carmen?"

  - q: "What currency does Playa del Carmen use — pesos or dollars?"

Pay in pesos wherever you can, use ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than the freestanding machines on the tourist strip, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, decline and let your own bank do the conversion."
  - q: "What is sargassum seaweed and when is it worst in Playa del Carmen?"
    a: "Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and the Riviera Maya including Playa del Carmen gets it. It is typically worst from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach — one stretch of sand can be clear while another a mile away is covered. When it lands in volume it smells of sulphur as it rots and makes swimming unpleasant. We would rather flag it honestly than pretend the sea is always postcard-perfect: hotels along the beach usually rake and clear their own stretch each morning, some install offshore barriers, and the Cozumel side of the channel and the cenotes are unaffected."

  - q: "When is hurricane season in Playa del Carmen?"
    a: "Atlantic hurricane season runs roughly June to November, with the highest risk in September and October. Most trips in those months pass without incident — the more common effect is heavy afternoon rain rather than a named storm — but the risk is real, and it is the main reason prices drop in that window. Take travel insurance that covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your dates, and follow the hotel's guidance if a storm is forecast."

  - q: "What are cenotes and how do you visit them?"
    a: "Cenotes are the Yucatán's freshwater sinkholes — collapsed limestone caverns filled with startlingly clear groundwater, some open like natural swimming pools, others cave-like and lit by shafts of daylight. Dos Ojos, Gran Cenote and Cenote Azul are among the best known near Playa del Carmen, and dozens more line the highway south towards Tulum. You can reach them by hire car, colectivo minibus along the 307, taxi or organised tour; take cash for entry fees, bring a towel, and use only biodegradable sunscreen or none at all, as regular sunscreen is banned in most cenotes to protect the water."
  - q: "How do I get to Tulum from Playa del Carmen?"
    a: "Tulum sits about an hour south of Playa del Carmen by road. Colectivo minibuses run frequently from the Centro and are the cheapest way, ADO coaches are more comfortable, and taxis or a hire car give you the freedom to stop at cenotes on the way. The main draw is the clifftop Maya ruins overlooking the Caribbean — go early in the day for cooler temperatures, thinner crowds and better light, and bring water, a hat and cash for the entrance."
  - q: "Can you visit Chichén Itzá as a day trip from Playa del Carmen?"

It is one of the New 7 Wonders of the World and worth the effort, though if you would rather not spend six hours on a coach, Cobá and the Tulum ruins are far closer alternatives. Whichever you choose, start early: the midday heat on an open site with little shade is punishing."

Services run through the day with more than one operator, so buy at the pier or online and check the current timetable for the last return sailing. Cozumel is world-class for reef diving and snorkelling, and the crossing is easy enough to do as a day trip — though the channel can get choppy, so take a tablet if you are prone to seasickness."

Xel-Há is built around a natural inlet and is snorkelling-focused, usually sold with food and drinks included. Xplor is the adventure one, with zip lines, amphibious vehicles and cave rivers.   - q: "Can I see turtles at Akumal?"

Access is regulated to protect the animals, which usually means going with a licensed guide, wearing a life jacket and staying out of the marked zones — follow the rules, keep your distance and never touch or chase the turtles. Sightings are likely but never guaranteed."

## What to Do in Playa del Carmen

- **Quinta Avenida (Fifth Avenue)** — the long pedestrian strip of bars, restaurants and shops that defines Playa and gives the town its character. Walk it end to end at least once: the southern blocks near the ferry pier are loud and touristy, the stretch north of Calle 26 is calmer and better for eating.
- **The town beaches and Playacar** — public access points at the end of most numbered streets mean you do not need a beachfront hotel to use the sand, and Playacar's beach just south of the centre is the quieter option.
- **The ferry to Cozumel** — roughly 45 minutes from the pier at the end of Fifth Avenue to an island fringed by world-class reef. Divers plan whole trips around it; snorkellers and day trippers can do it and be back for dinner.
- **The cenotes** — the Yucatán's freshwater sinkholes, and the single most memorable thing most visitors do here. **Dos Ojos**, **Gran Cenote** and **Cenote Azul** are among the best known, all within easy reach by colectivo, taxi or hire car. Biodegradable sunscreen or none at all.
- **The Maya sites** — **Tulum**'s clifftop ruins sit about an hour south above the Caribbean; **Chichén Itzá**, one of the New 7 Wonders of the World, is roughly three hours west and usually done as a long organised day; and **Cobá** offers a jungle-set alternative much closer to Tulum.
- **Xcaret, Xel-Há and Xplor** — the big eco-parks of the Riviera Maya: Xcaret for underground rivers, wildlife and the evening show, Xel-Há for snorkelling in a natural inlet, Xplor for zip lines and cave adventure. Book ahead and go early.
- **Akumal** — the bay 30 to 40 minutes south known for green turtles grazing its seagrass, visited with licensed guides under rules designed to protect the animals.
- **Playa's nightlife** — concentrated on and around **Calle 12**, where the bars and clubs run late. Excellent if that is what you came for, and a genuine reason to check your hotel's exact street if it is not.

## Where to Base Yourself in Playa del Carmen

**Centro / Quinta Avenida** is the default answer and the right one for most people. Staying in the numbered grid within a few blocks of Fifth Avenue means you walk to the beach, walk to dinner, walk to the ferry and walk to the ADO bus station — and it is where the genuine budget value in this guide sits, at £22 to £45 a night. The trade is noise: the closer you are to Calle 12 and the southern blocks, the later the street stays awake.

**Playacar**, the gated resort and residential district immediately south, swaps street life for calm — golf, quiet roads, a more relaxed beach and resorts like Allegro Playacar and The Reef Playacar. It suits families and anyone whose ideal day ends by a pool rather than on a bar stool, and Fifth Avenue is still reachable on foot or by a very short taxi.

**The north end around Coco Beach** is the quieter beach option inside town — hotels a little removed from the crush, a calmer stretch of sand, and a walk of 15 to 25 minutes into the busy part of Fifth Avenue. Good for couples and repeat visitors who already know what the middle of town is like.

**The all-inclusive resort strip** further out along the Riviera Maya, in both directions, is where the big names sit. Be honest with yourself about the trade: those resorts are often cheaper per night for what is included, but you will taxi in for anything that is not inside the gates, and in a town where the restaurants are one of the main attractions, that adds up in both money and lost evenings. If you are booking a resort mainly to rest, it is a good deal. If you want Playa itself, stay in Playa itself.

## Honest Practicalities for Playa del Carmen

**The airport is Cancún (CUN), not Playa.** Playa del Carmen has no airport of its own, and the drive from CUN takes roughly **45 to 70 minutes** depending on traffic and terminal. Pre-book a shuttle, take the ADO coach from the airport to the bus station on Fifth Avenue, or hire a car — and never accept a lift from someone who approaches you inside the terminal. Tulum's newer airport (TQO) about an hour south is worth checking as an alternative, though its international schedule is still building.

**Sargassum seaweed is real, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.** The brown seaweed drifts onto Caribbean-facing beaches and can affect Playa del Carmen, typically at its worst from **roughly April to August**. How bad it gets varies enormously from year to year, week to week and beach to beach — one stretch can be clear while another a mile away is buried. Hotels along the beach generally rake and clear their own stretch each morning, some run offshore barriers, and the cenotes and the Cozumel side are unaffected. Check recent dated photos and daily sargassum maps before you travel rather than relying on hotel marketing shots.

**Hurricane season runs roughly June to November**, with peak risk in September and October. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than heavy afternoon rain, and prices reflect the risk — but take insurance that covers weather disruption and keep some flexibility.

**Everyday sense goes a long way.** Use official taxis from ranks or a ride-hailing app rather than unmarked cars, do not walk empty stretches of beach at night, use ATMs inside bank branches rather than freestanding machines on the tourist strip, drink bottled or filtered water rather than tap, and pay in **Mexican Pesos (MXN)** rather than US dollars where you can — dollars are widely accepted but the rate is usually worse. Before you book anything, read the current [UK government travel advice for Mexico](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/mexico), which carries region-by-region guidance that changes, and for a broader look at what else the country offers beyond the Riviera Maya, the official tourism site [Visit Mexico](https://visitmexico.com/) is a reasonable starting point. Mexico drives on the right, the language is Spanish (English is widely spoken in Playa), and tipping at around 10 to 15 percent is expected.

## Playa del Carmen Hotels FAQs

**Where should I stay in Playa del Carmen?**

For a first visit, stay in the Centro around Quinta Avenida (Fifth Avenue) — the pedestrian strip that runs parallel to the beach and holds most of the restaurants, bars, shops, the ferry pier and the ADO bus station. It is walkable, it has the best budget value in town, and you never need a taxi for dinner. Playacar, the gated resort and golf district just south, suits travellers who want calm and a quieter beach; the north end around Coco Beach suits those who want beach hotels away from the noise; and the all-inclusive resort strip further out along the Riviera Maya is cheaper per night but means a taxi ride for anything beyond your own resort gates.

**How do I get from Cancún airport to Playa del Carmen?**

Playa del Carmen has no airport of its own — Cancún International (CUN) is the gateway, roughly 45 to 70 minutes north by road depending on traffic and which terminal you land at. Your three sensible options are a pre-booked private shuttle (door to door, easiest with luggage or kids), the ADO coach that runs from the airport terminals to Playa del Carmen bus station on Fifth Avenue (much cheaper per person, comfortable, fixed fares), or a hire car if you plan to drive to cenotes and ruins. Pre-book the shuttle rather than arranging one in the arrivals hall, and ignore anyone who approaches you before you are outside the terminal.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Playa del Carmen?**

No — there is no direct UK service to Playa del Carmen itself, because the town has no airport. You fly into Cancún (CUN), which does have direct flights from the UK with British Airways, TUI and Virgin Atlantic taking roughly 10 hours from London, then transfer 45 to 70 minutes south by road. Tulum's newer airport (TQO) sits about an hour south of Playa and is worth checking as an alternative, though its international schedule is still developing — compare both before booking.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Mexico?**

UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Mexico. You complete Mexico's immigration process on arrival — historically the FMM tourist form, now handled digitally or by stamp at many airports — and the officer grants the number of days you are permitted to stay, so check the stamp before leaving the desk. Rules change, so confirm the current requirements and your passport validity on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly.

**What currency does Playa del Carmen use — pesos or dollars?**

The Mexican Peso (MXN) is the currency. US dollars are widely accepted along Fifth Avenue and in the resorts, but you will almost always get a worse rate than paying in pesos, and change is often given back in pesos at the shop's own rate. Pay in pesos wherever you can, use ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than the freestanding machines on the tourist strip, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, decline and let your own bank do the conversion.

**What is sargassum seaweed and when is it worst in Playa del Carmen?**

Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and the Riviera Maya including Playa del Carmen gets it. It is typically worst from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach — one stretch of sand can be clear while another a mile away is covered. When it lands in volume it smells of sulphur as it rots and makes swimming unpleasant. We would rather flag it honestly than pretend the sea is always postcard-perfect: hotels along the beach usually rake and clear their own stretch each morning, some install offshore barriers, and the Cozumel side of the channel and the cenotes are unaffected.

**How can I check the sargassum situation before I travel?**

Look at recent dated photos and reviews rather than hotel marketing shots, check the local sargassum monitoring pages and social media groups that publish daily beach-by-beach colour maps for the Riviera Maya, and read the most recent guest reviews on the hotel's own page. If clear water matters more than anything else on your trip, travel outside roughly April to August, book a hotel with a good pool, or plan the swimming around cenotes and a Cozumel day trip, which are not affected by beach sargassum.

**When is hurricane season in Playa del Carmen?**

Atlantic hurricane season runs roughly June to November, with the highest risk in September and October. Most trips in those months pass without incident — the more common effect is heavy afternoon rain rather than a named storm — but the risk is real, and it is the main reason prices drop in that window. Take travel insurance that covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your dates, and follow the hotel's guidance if a storm is forecast.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Playa del Carmen?**

No — stick to bottled or filtered water. Hotels almost always provide purified drinking water, either as bottles in the room or from dispensers, and restaurants use purified water and ice as standard. Brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine for most people, but drinking it is not advised.

**Is Playa del Carmen safe for tourists?**

Playa del Carmen is a busy, well-established tourist town and most visits are trouble free, but it is not risk free and sensible precautions matter. Use official taxis from ranks or a ride-hailing app rather than unmarked cars, do not walk empty stretches of beach at night, withdraw cash from ATMs inside bank branches rather than standalone machines, keep valuables in the room safe, and be careful with drink measures and late nights around Calle 12's bar strip. Read the UK government travel advice for Mexico before you go, as it carries region-specific guidance that changes.

**Playa del Carmen, Cancún or Tulum — which should I choose?**

Cancún is the easiest arrival (you land there), has the biggest hotel zone, the strongest big-resort infrastructure and the most nightlife, but the Hotel Zone is a strip of towers rather than a town. Playa del Carmen is the middle option and the one most repeat visitors settle on — a real walkable town with a pedestrian avenue of restaurants and bars, beaches, the Cozumel ferry, cheap taxis to cenotes, and genuinely cheap rooms behind Fifth Avenue. Tulum is the smallest and most style-driven, with the clifftop ruins and boho beach hotels, but it is the most expensive per night, the beach hotels are strung out along one road, and you need a bike, taxi or car for everything. Choose Cancún for convenience, Playa for balance and value, Tulum for atmosphere and a bigger budget.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Playa del Carmen?**

The Centro blocks behind Quinta Avenida — roughly Calle 1 to Calle 30, one to five streets inland from the beach. That is where the £22 to £40 rooms in this guide sit, and it is also the most useful part of town, since you can walk to the beach, the restaurants, the bus station and the ferry pier. Prices climb as you move onto the avenue itself, onto the sand, or into Playacar and the all-inclusive strip.

**What is Quinta Avenida (Fifth Avenue)?**

Quinta Avenida, or Fifth Avenue, is Playa del Carmen's long pedestrian spine — a traffic-free street running roughly parallel to the beach, one block inland, lined with restaurants, bars, cafés, souvenir shops, pharmacies and hotel entrances. It is the reason Playa feels like a town rather than a resort strip, and it is why staying in the Centro is so practical: everything you need is a short flat walk away. The southern end near the ferry pier is the busiest and most touristy; walk north past Calle 26 and it gets calmer and more local.

**What are cenotes and how do you visit them?**

Cenotes are the Yucatán's freshwater sinkholes — collapsed limestone caverns filled with startlingly clear groundwater, some open like natural swimming pools, others cave-like and lit by shafts of daylight. Dos Ojos, Gran Cenote and Cenote Azul are among the best known near Playa del Carmen, and dozens more line the highway south towards Tulum. You can reach them by hire car, colectivo minibus along the 307, taxi or organised tour; take cash for entry fees, bring a towel, and use only biodegradable sunscreen or none at all, as regular sunscreen is banned in most cenotes to protect the water.

**How do I get to Tulum from Playa del Carmen?**

Tulum sits about an hour south of Playa del Carmen by road. Colectivo minibuses run frequently from the Centro and are the cheapest way, ADO coaches are more comfortable, and taxis or a hire car give you the freedom to stop at cenotes on the way. The main draw is the clifftop Maya ruins overlooking the Caribbean — go early in the day for cooler temperatures, thinner crowds and better light, and bring water, a hat and cash for the entrance.

**Can you visit Chichén Itzá as a day trip from Playa del Carmen?**

Yes, and thousands do — but it is a long day. Chichén Itzá is roughly three hours west of Playa del Carmen each way, so organised tours leave very early and get back in the evening, usually bundling a cenote swim and a lunch stop. It is one of the New 7 Wonders of the World and worth the effort, though if you would rather not spend six hours on a coach, Cobá and the Tulum ruins are far closer alternatives. Whichever you choose, start early: the midday heat on an open site with little shade is punishing.

**How do I take the ferry to Cozumel?**

The Cozumel ferry leaves from the pier at the southern end of Fifth Avenue, in the middle of town, and takes roughly 45 minutes each way. Services run through the day with more than one operator, so buy at the pier or online and check the current timetable for the last return sailing. Cozumel is world-class for reef diving and snorkelling, and the crossing is easy enough to do as a day trip — though the channel can get choppy, so take a tablet if you are prone to seasickness.

**Do I need to speak Spanish in Playa del Carmen?**

No. Spanish is the language, but Playa del Carmen is one of Mexico's most international towns and English is widely spoken in hotels, restaurants, dive shops and tour offices. A few polite words go a long way with taxi drivers, market stalls and smaller family-run places away from Fifth Avenue — hola, por favor, gracias, la cuenta — and are always appreciated.

**How much should I tip in Playa del Carmen?**

Tipping is customary and matters to service staff. Around 10 to 15 percent is normal in restaurants, and you should check the bill first, as some places on Fifth Avenue already add a service charge. Small tips for hotel housekeeping, porters, tour guides and dive crew are expected, and at all-inclusive resorts a few pesos for bar and pool staff goes a long way. Tip in pesos where you can, and carry small notes for it.

**When is the best time to visit Playa del Carmen?**

December to April is the driest and most reliable stretch of weather, and also the busiest and priciest — Christmas, New Year and Easter are peak. May and November are good shoulder months. June to November is hurricane season with heavier rain and the highest chance of disruption, and roughly April to August is when sargassum seaweed is most likely on the beaches. If clear water is the priority, aim for the winter months; if price is the priority, the summer and autumn discounts are real, provided you go in with your eyes open.

**How many days do you need in Playa del Carmen?**

Four to five nights covers the town itself plus two big outings — say a cenote day and either Tulum or the Cozumel ferry. Seven nights lets you add Chichén Itzá, an eco-park such as Xcaret or Xel-Há, and a proper do-nothing beach day without rushing. If Playa is one stop on a wider Yucatán trip, three nights is enough to get the feel of the place before moving on.

**Is Playacar a good place to stay?**

Playacar is the gated residential and resort district immediately south of the town centre, with a golf course, quieter streets and a calmer beach. It suits travellers who want resort-style peace and are happy to walk 15 to 25 minutes or take a short taxi to reach Fifth Avenue's restaurants. If your idea of a good evening is wandering out to a different taquería every night, the Centro is the better base; if it is a lounger and a quiet stretch of sand, Playacar is.

**Are all-inclusive resorts worth it in Playa del Carmen?**

It depends where you are staying. Out on the Riviera Maya resort strip, where there is little within walking distance, all-inclusive genuinely saves money and hassle. In the Centro it is a harder case, because Playa's restaurant scene is one of the main reasons to be there and cheap, excellent food is a two-minute walk from your door — paying twice for dinner is a poor trade. Families and drinkers tend to come out ahead on all-inclusive; independent eaters usually do not.

**Is Playa del Carmen good for families?**

Very — it is one of the easier Caribbean bases with children. The beach shelves gently in places, the town is flat and walkable, the eco-parks at Xcaret, Xel-Há and Xplor are built for family days out, Akumal offers a chance to see turtles, and the cenotes are a hit with older kids. Family-focused resorts such as the Nickelodeon property on the Riviera Maya go further with themed entertainment. Note that a large share of Playa's better-rated resorts are adults-only, so check that filter carefully before you book.

**What is the beach like in Playa del Carmen?**

White sand and warm, shallow Caribbean water, with the town beaches running north from the ferry pier and quieter sand at the north end around Coco Beach and down in Playacar. Public access points sit at the end of most numbered streets, so you do not need to be in a beachfront hotel to use the beach. The honest caveat is sargassum seaweed, which can land in volume between roughly April and August and varies hugely by year and by beach — hotels usually clear their own stretch each morning.

**Do I need a hire car in Playa del Carmen?**

Not for the town itself, which is easily walkable, and not for Tulum or the cenotes on the main highway, which colectivos, ADO coaches and taxis cover cheaply. A hire car earns its keep if you want to chase quieter cenotes, beaches and ruins on your own schedule, or if you are staying out on the resort strip. Remember that Mexico drives on the right, that Mexican third-party liability insurance is compulsory and usually charged locally on top of the online rate, and that the toll road inland to Chichén Itzá costs extra.

**Is Playa del Carmen walkable?**

Yes — the Centro is a flat, numbered grid, and almost everything a visitor needs sits within a 20-minute walk of Fifth Avenue: the beach, the ferry pier, the ADO bus station, restaurants, dive shops and pharmacies. That walkability is the single biggest reason to prefer the Centro budget hotels in this guide over cheaper rooms further out, where every meal becomes a taxi ride.

**What are Xcaret, Xel-Há and Xplor?**

They are the big eco-parks of the Riviera Maya, all within easy reach of Playa del Carmen. Xcaret is the largest and most varied, mixing underground rivers, wildlife and a large evening show of Mexican music and dance. Xel-Há is built around a natural inlet and is snorkelling-focused, usually sold with food and drinks included. Xplor is the adventure one, with zip lines, amphibious vehicles and cave rivers. Tickets are not cheap, so buy ahead, go early, and check what each price actually includes.

**Can I see turtles at Akumal?**

Akumal, about 30 to 40 minutes south of Playa del Carmen, is known for green turtles that graze the seagrass in its bay, and snorkelling there is one of the most popular half-day trips from town. Access is regulated to protect the animals, which usually means going with a licensed guide, wearing a life jacket and staying out of the marked zones — follow the rules, keep your distance and never touch or chase the turtles. Sightings are likely but never guaranteed.

**Are there quiet hotels away from the nightlife?**

Yes. Playa's nightlife is concentrated around Calle 12 and the southern end of Fifth Avenue, so noise is a real consideration if you book within a block or two of it. The north end of town around Coco Beach, the Playacar side and the Riviera Maya resorts are all noticeably quieter, and many of the adults-only properties in this guide sell themselves on exactly that calm. If you are booking in the Centro, read recent reviews for noise comments and ask for a room away from the street.
