  - q: "Who is the Dalai Lama and why does his presence define McLeod Ganj?"
    a: "**His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso**, is the spiritual and (historically) political leader of Tibetan Buddhism, recognised as a child in Tibet in the 1930s and enthroned as Tibet's temporal leader in 1950. Following the failed 1959 Tibetan uprising against Chinese rule, he fled across the Himalayas into India, and the Indian government granted him and the Tibetan government-in-exile a home in the former British hill station of **McLeod Ganj**, above Dharamshala in Himachal Pradesh. Sixty-six years on, his residence — the **Tsuglagkhang Complex** — is still the town's spiritual and physical centre, and McLeod Ganj is still widely known as **'Little Lhasa'**, home to the largest Tibetan exile community outside Tibet itself. His public teachings, when he gives them, remain the single biggest draw for both Tibetan Buddhists and secular Western visitors."
  - q: "What is the Tibetan government-in-exile, and can visitors learn about it?"
    a: "The **Central Tibetan Administration (CTA)**, commonly called the Tibetan government-in-exile, is the democratically elected body that governs the Tibetan exile community's civic affairs — education, health, culture and diplomacy — from its base in Dharamshala. It is not recognised as a sovereign government by India or any other state, but it functions as the de facto administrative and cultural authority for the roughly 100,000 Tibetans living in exile worldwide. Visitors can learn its history at the **Tibet Museum** near the Tsuglagkhang Complex, which documents the 1959 exodus, the Long March into exile and the community's rebuilding in India through photographs, oral histories and film. It's a sobering, essential 90 minutes before you explore the rest of the town."
  - q: "What is the Norbulingka Institute, and is it worth the day trip?"
    a: "**Yes, unreservedly.** The Norbulingka Institute, in Lower Dharamshala about an hour's drive from McLeod Ganj, is the official body preserving traditional **Tibetan arts** — thangka painting, wood carving, appliqué, bronze statue casting and traditional garment-making — through a working campus of studios, a temple, formal Japanese-influenced gardens and a museum. Named after the Dalai Lama's former summer palace in Lhasa, it trains young Tibetan refugees in disciplines that would otherwise have died out in exile. You can walk through active workshops and watch artisans at work, visit the **Losel Doll Museum** (dioramas of Tibetan life in traditional dress), and buy directly from the institute's shop, with proceeds supporting the artists. Chonor House's hand-painted rooms are furnished by Norbulingka artists, which is why staying there feels like a preview of the institute itself."
  - q: "What is the Tsuglagkhang Complex, and what's inside it?"
    a: "The Tsuglagkhang Complex is McLeod Ganj's spiritual centre — a compound containing the **Dalai Lama's official residence and private temple**, the main **Tsuglagkhang temple** (housing a large Shakyamuni Buddha statue plus statues of Avalokiteshvara and Padmasambhava), the **Kalachakra Temple**, the **Tibet Museum**, and the start of the **Kora** — the ritual circumambulation path that circles the whole complex. It is free to enter, open daily, and functions as both an active place of worship (expect monks debating in the courtyards most afternoons, a genuinely captivating spectacle of clapping hands and rapid-fire Tibetan logic) and the town's default gathering point. Security is airport-style at the entrance — no large bags, and photography rules vary by hall, so check signage before shooting inside."
  - q: "What is the Kora, and how do I walk it properly?"
    a: "The Kora is the ritual circumambulation path that loops around the Tsuglagkhang Complex and the Dalai Lama's residence, roughly 3km with light gradient, taking 35-50 minutes at a slow, meditative pace. **Walk clockwise, always** — this is the single most important etiquette rule in town, observed by every Tibetan Buddhist practitioner and expected of visitors too. The path is lined with prayer wheels (spin them clockwise as you pass, with your right hand), prayer flags, small chörtens and elderly Tibetan pilgrims doing multiple daily laps with mala beads in hand. The best time to walk it is **dawn, around 6am**, before the temple properly wakes — the light through the deodar forest, the quiet, and the handful of monks and elders already on their rounds make it the single most calming 40 minutes most visitors have in McLeod Ganj. It's free, requires no booking, and you should do it daily if you're staying the full 14 days."
  - q: "What is Bhagsu Falls, and is it worth visiting?"
    a: "Bhagsu Falls is a roughly 20m seasonal waterfall a 20-30 minute walk (or short taxi) above Bhagsu village, itself a 15-minute walk from McLeod Ganj's main square. It's at its most dramatic just after monsoon (September) and thins to a modest trickle by late spring, so set expectations by season before you make the trip specifically for the falls. The walk up through Bhagsu village — past guesthouses, cafés and the Bhagsunag Temple — is arguably more rewarding than the waterfall itself, and it's a good half-day combined with a coffee stop at one of Bhagsu's rooftop cafés looking out over the Kangra Valley. Skip it in peak monsoon (July-August), when the path gets slippery and the water can be dangerously fast-flowing near the base."
  - q: "What is the Triund trek, and how difficult is it for an average UK visitor?"
    a: "Triund is a roughly 9km, one-way uphill hike from McLeod Ganj (or a shorter start from Galu Devi Temple, reachable by taxi, cutting the walk to about 7km) to a 2,875m grassy ridge with a panoramic, close-up view of the entire snow-capped **Dhauladhar Range**. It's rated moderate — a reasonably fit UK visitor unused to altitude should budget 4-5 hours up and 2.5-3 hours down, with a decent pair of broken-in walking shoes and 2 litres of water. Most visitors either do it as a long day trip (up and back down before dark) or camp overnight at Triund in a rented tent or one of the basic guesthouses, catching sunrise over the Dhauladhar range before descending — the overnight version is meaningfully more rewarding and only moderately harder. Avoid it entirely during monsoon (July-August), when the trail turns to slick mud and landslip risk rises sharply, and start early (7-8am) in any season to avoid afternoon cloud cover obscuring the view."
  - q: "Can I extend the Triund trek to Indrahar Pass, and should I?"
    a: "Yes, for experienced trekkers only. **Indrahar Pass (4,342m)** continues beyond Triund via **Lahesh Caves** or **Ilaka**, adding 2-3 further days of genuine high-altitude trekking with snow underfoot even in summer, and it's a different order of undertaking from the Triund day hike — proper mountaineering boots, a sleeping bag rated for sub-zero temperatures, and ideally a local guide or a registered trekking agency booking rather than a solo attempt. It's not part of the standard 14-day sabbatical itinerary in this guide, which is deliberately built around rest and reflection rather than summit-chasing, but it's worth knowing about if you're the kind of UK traveller who reads a 'gentle Himalayan reset' guide and immediately wants the hardest version available. Book it as an add-on for a return trip once you know the region, not as a first-visit stretch goal."
  - q: "What is Vipassana, and how does the Dharamkot course actually work?"
    a: "Vipassana, in the S.N. Goenka tradition taught at **Dhamma Sikhara** in Dharamkot, is a 10-day silent meditation course: no talking, no eye contact, no phone, no reading or writing, from the evening you arrive until the final morning. Days are structured around roughly 10 hours of seated meditation broken into sessions, two vegetarian meals (breakfast and lunch, nothing after midday beyond tea and fruit for new students), and nightly discourse videos from Goenka himself explaining the technique. It is completely free — accommodation, food and tuition — funded entirely by voluntary donations from students who've completed a previous course, which is part of the tradition's philosophy. Apply online 6-8 weeks ahead at the official Dhamma Sikhara website; courses fill quickly in the March-June and September-November high season. It is genuinely intense — several UK travellers leave partway through — and this guide's earlier FAQ on beginner-friendliness stands: don't make a 10-day silent course your first meditation experience of any kind."
  - q: "What is Tushita Meditation Centre, and how is it different from Vipassana?"
    a: "Tushita, in Dharamkot, teaches within the **Tibetan Gelugpa Buddhist tradition** rather than the Theravada-derived Vipassana method, and its flagship offering — a roughly 10-day 'Introduction to Buddhism' course — includes talking, group discussion, gentle guided meditation and daily teachings from resident Western and Tibetan teachers, making it considerably more approachable for a first-timer than Dhamma Sikhara's total silence. It's donation-based, typically £100-200 covering the full course including simple vegetarian accommodation and meals, and also runs shorter weekend courses and drop-in meditation sessions for visitors who don't want to commit to the full 10 days. Book the flagship course 4-6 months ahead in peak season (March-June, September-November); weekend and drop-in sessions can usually be booked with a few weeks' notice."

    a: "Live on **momos** (steamed or fried dumplings, usually vegetable or cheese, sometimes chicken) and **thukpa** (Tibetan noodle soup, the definitive comfort food at 1,800m) for your full stay — it's light, warming and genuinely good for a reset. **Tibet Kitchen**, just off the main square, is the most consistently recommended sit-down spot for both. **Lhamo's Croissants**, tucked down a side lane, does a surprisingly excellent Western-Tibetan hybrid bakery menu — good for a change of pace mid-retreat. For a proper thali or South Indian breakfast, several small cafés along Temple Road serve reliable vegetarian food at low prices. Skip chasing an 'English breakfast' — most attempts in McLeod Ganj are a pale imitation of the real thing and you'll enjoy the trip more eating what the town does well."

  - q: "What's the deal with the monkeys in McLeod Ganj?"
    a: "McLeod Ganj has a resident rhesus macaque population, much like Hampi, Varanasi and other Indian hill and temple towns, and they are confident, opportunistic and genuinely capable of opening an unlatched window or unzipped bag. Keep balcony doors and windows closed when you're out, don't leave food visible on a balcony or windowsill, hold sunglasses and phones securely in open areas (they've been known to snatch shiny objects), and never make direct prolonged eye contact with an adult macaque, which reads as a challenge.   - q: "What's the altitude like in McLeod Ganj, and do I need to worry about altitude sickness?"
    a: "No, not meaningfully. The one exception is the **Triund trek**, which climbs to 2,875m: a fit visitor doing it as a single day trip is very unlikely to have problems, but if you're combining Triund with a further push toward Indrahar Pass (4,342m), take altitude seriously exactly as you would in Spiti or Ladakh — pace the ascent, hydrate heavily, and turn back at the first sign of a persistent headache or nausea. When the **14th Dalai Lama** fled Tibet in 1959 and the Indian government settled him in **Dharamshala**, the upper hill town of **McLeod Ganj** became the de facto capital of the Tibetan government-in-exile. Sixty-six years later, the town remains a working centre of **Tibetan Buddhism** — the **Tsuglagkhang Complex** (the Dalai Lama's temple), the **Norbulingka Institute** (preserving Tibetan arts), and the dense network of chortens, prayer wheels and butter-lamp halls that still anchor daily life. Add a thriving **Western meditation-retreat scene** in the Dharamkot hills above town, the seasonal drama of **Bhagsu Falls**, the day-hike-to-overnight-trek at **Triund**, and the tea gardens of nearby **Palampur**, and you have the most unlikely thing in the Indian Himalayas: a place that is genuinely good for your mental health, and genuinely easy to reach from the UK.

## 1. The sabbatical mindset: why 14 days?

You don't come to McLeod Ganj for a weekend. The destination rewards time in a way few places do, and unlike Spiti's altitude-forced slowness, here the slowness is a choice you have to actually make — the town would let you rush through it in three days if you wanted to.

**The threshold.** It takes 4 days to shed London speed. The phone-checking habit, the inbox anxiety, the calendar-fragmentation of urban professional life — all of it lives below conscious awareness, and all of it takes 4 days of mountain air and reduced stimulation to surface and dissolve. **By Day 7**, the rhythmic chanting of the Tsuglagkhang Complex starts to feel less like exotic ambient sound and more like a cue your nervous system has been asking for.

**The vibe.** Maroon robes, the smell of butter lamps, the sound of bells. Old Tibetan men spinning prayer wheels at dawn. Western meditators in fleece walking the **Kora** in silence. The smell of Tibetan thukpa from a streetside kitchen, mist rolling off the Dhauladhar peaks by mid-afternoon. It is a **Sabbatical** where the hotel isn't just a room — it is your portal to Tibetan philosophy.

**Why this destination specifically, and not just "somewhere in the Himalayas"?** Because McLeod Ganj offers something genuinely rare: a functioning, living centre of a specific spiritual tradition, in exile, that has spent six decades building an infrastructure explicitly designed to welcome outside visitors into its practice — meditation centres teaching in English, monasteries that permit respectful observation, a museum that explains its own history plainly. Most spiritual destinations ask you to interpret; McLeod Ganj explains itself. That's a large part of why it works so well as a first Himalayan sabbatical for a UK professional who has never done anything like this before.

## 3. The 14-day "Reset" itinerary

**Days 1-4 — Acclimatisation.** Walk the **Kora** (the ritual circuit around the Dalai Lama's residence — takes 35-50 minutes at slow pace, do it daily). Drink **butter tea at Namgyal Cafe** inside the Tsuglagkhang Complex. Visit the **Tibet Museum** (a sobering account of the 1959 exodus). Eat thukpa and momos. Sleep early. No phone in bed.

**Days 5-9 — The deep dive.** Move up to **Dharamkot** (the smaller hill village above McLeod Ganj) for a structured retreat. Options: a **5-day silent meditation course at Tushita**, a **breathwork-and-sound-healing weekend**, or a **yoga teacher training residential**. You can also continue staying at Chonor and walk to Dharamkot daily for day-classes — many travellers prefer this for flexibility. Slot in a half-day at **Bhagsu Falls** and Bhagsu village on a lighter day within this block — it's a natural, low-effort break between structured retreat sessions.

**Days 10-14 — Mountain integration.** Trek to **Triund** (a 9km hike up to a 2,875m grass meadow with a panoramic view of the entire Dhauladhar Range — a full day-trip with a cinematic sunrise return, or camp overnight for the sunrise itself). Visit the **Norbulingka Institute** in Lower Dharamshala (1 hour drive) to see traditional **Tibetan thangka painting, wood-carving and bronze-casting** preserved by the institute's resident artisans. If you have a spare day in this block and the weather is clear, a day trip out to the **Palampur tea gardens** (roughly 1.5 hours from McLeod Ganj) makes a genuinely different, gentler final excursion — flat walks between tea rows instead of another climb. Final two days: nothing planned. Read, walk, breathe. Return to London a different person.

## 4. The "Dhauladhar Link" Transit Guide

**The route.** London → **Delhi** on **BA** or **Virgin Atlantic** direct (~9 hours). Then a **75-minute domestic flight** to **Dharamshala (DHM / Gaggal Airport)** on IndiGo or Air India.

**The pro move.** The drive from Gaggal Airport to McLeod Ganj is **steep and winding**, 45 minutes, with hairpins. Have **Chonor House** or **Norbu House** pre-arrange your **mountain-certified driver**. Don't take the airport touts — they overcharge and don't know the upper-McLeod backroads.

**The train alternative.** The overnight **Swaraj Express** from Delhi to **Pathankot Cantt** (10 hours, AC tier), followed by a **3-hour taxi**. It's the Slow Travel route — you watch the Punjab plains turn into the Himalayan foothills as dawn breaks. Atmospheric, romantic, and a real "the journey is the start of the retreat" feel.

**The bus option.** An overnight **Volvo bus** from Delhi's Majnu Ka Tila (the Tibetan colony neighbourhood, worth a wander before you board) direct to McLeod Ganj — around 12 hours of mountain road, roughly £15 one-way. It's the cheapest option by a wide margin and skips the Pathankot taxi transfer entirely, but the road has real switchback stretches in the final two hours and it's genuinely tiring compared to flying. Worth it if budget is the primary constraint or if you want the overland experience without the train-plus-taxi combination.

**Which one should you actually pick?** For most UK travellers on a 14-day sabbatical, the flight is the right call — you land at Gaggal with 12 of your 14 days still ahead of you, rather than losing a day and a half each way to overland transit. Reserve the train or bus option for a return trip once you already know the region, or if slow overland travel is itself part of what you're looking for in the sabbatical.

<a id="tsuglagkhang"></a>

## 5. The Tsuglagkhang Complex and the Kora

This is the town's centre of gravity, and it's worth understanding properly before you arrive rather than stumbling into it as just "the temple."

**Inside the complex.** The **Tsuglagkhang** itself houses a large Shakyamuni Buddha statue alongside statues of Avalokiteshvara (the bodhisattva of compassion, with whom the Dalai Lama lineage is traditionally associated) and Padmasambhava, the 8th-century teacher who helped establish Buddhism in Tibet. The adjoining **Kalachakra Temple** houses vivid, recently restored murals depicting the Kalachakra mandala. The **Dalai Lama's residence and private temple** sit within the same secure compound — not open to casual visitors, but the proximity is part of what makes the whole complex feel alive rather than purely historical. Expect monk debate sessions in the courtyards most afternoons: pairs and small groups of monks clap, gesture and rapid-fire questions at each other in a formal, centuries-old training method for testing logical understanding of Buddhist philosophy. It looks like theatre. It's actually one of the more rigorous intellectual traditions in the complex, and visitors are welcome to watch quietly.

**Walking the Kora properly.** The Kora loops the whole complex — roughly 3km, 35-50 minutes at a slow pace, light gradient. Walk **clockwise, always**; spin prayer wheels clockwise with your right hand as you pass them. Dawn (around 6am) is the best window: soft light through the deodar forest, a handful of elderly Tibetan pilgrims already doing multiple laps with mala beads, and none of the midday tour groups. Do it daily if you're staying the full 14 days — it's free, requires no booking, and several returning UK travellers cite it as the single most calming ritual of their entire trip.

**Registering for a Dalai Lama teaching.** If your dates coincide with a public teaching (typically March-April or October, plus dates around major Tibetan calendar events), registration is mandatory and must happen **in person at the Branch Security Office** in McLeod Ganj, **at least 2 days ahead**, with your passport and 2 passport photos — online registration isn't accepted. Teachings are translated live into English via FM radio; bring a small portable receiver or rent one locally for about £2. Check **dalailama.com/announcements** for the published schedule, which typically comes out 6-8 weeks ahead of the event itself.

<a id="norbulingka"></a>

## 6. The Norbulingka Institute

About an hour's drive from McLeod Ganj in Lower Dharamshala, the **Norbulingka Institute** is the official body preserving traditional Tibetan arts in exile — and it's one of the best half-day excursions in the entire guide, precisely because it's a working campus rather than a static museum.

**What you'll see.** Studios where young Tibetan artisans train for years in **thangka painting** (intricate religious scroll painting, following exact iconographic rules passed down through generations), **wood carving**, **appliqué** and **bronze statue casting** using lost-wax techniques largely unchanged for centuries. A formal temple with a towering gilded Buddha statue. Landscaped Japanese-influenced gardens built around water channels, designed as a deliberate echo of the Dalai Lama's former Norbulingka summer palace in Lhasa, from which the institute takes its name. The **Losel Doll Museum** on-site presents detailed dioramas of traditional Tibetan life and dress — genuinely charming and a good way to absorb Tibetan cultural history without reading a wall of text.

**Why it matters beyond the craft.** Norbulingka exists because these art forms were at genuine risk of dying out entirely in exile, with the master artisans who carried the knowledge growing old and few young Tibetans in a displaced community having access to formal training. The institute's teaching structure — years-long apprenticeships, working directly under senior artists — has kept several of these traditions alive that might otherwise have been lost within a generation or two. Buying from the institute's shop puts money directly back into that training pipeline, which is worth knowing if you want a souvenir that means something beyond decoration.

**The Chonor House connection.** Chonor House's hand-painted rooms are furnished by Norbulingka-trained artists, so visiting the institute after (or before) your stay adds real context to what you're sleeping inside — it stops being "a nice boutique hotel" and becomes a small, lived-in extension of the institute's actual mission.

<a id="bhagsu-triund"></a>

## 7. Bhagsu Falls and the Triund trek

**Bhagsu Falls.** A 20-30 minute walk from McLeod Ganj's main square through Bhagsu village, this roughly 20m seasonal waterfall is at its most dramatic just after monsoon (September) and a modest trickle by late spring — check the season before making the trip specifically for the falls rather than the walk. The village itself, with its cafés, guesthouses and the Bhagsunag Temple, is arguably the better reason to go: a rooftop coffee overlooking the Kangra Valley is a genuinely restful half-day within a busier retreat block. Skip the falls path in peak monsoon (July-August), when it turns slippery and the water flow near the base can be dangerous.

**Triund.** The signature trek of the region — roughly 9km one-way from McLeod Ganj (or about 7km if you taxi to the Galu Devi Temple starting point) up to a 2,875m grassy ridge with a full panoramic view of the snow-capped **Dhauladhar Range**. Budget 4-5 hours up, 2.5-3 hours down for a reasonably fit UK visitor unused to altitude, with broken-in walking shoes and at least 2 litres of water. Two ways to do it: a long day trip there and back before dark, or the meaningfully more rewarding overnight version — camp at Triund in a rented tent or one of the basic on-site guesthouses, and wake to sunrise breaking over the range. Start early (7-8am) in any season, since afternoon cloud cover frequently rolls in and obscures the view. Avoid the trail entirely in July-August monsoon, when it turns to slick mud and landslip risk rises sharply.

**Beyond Triund.** For experienced trekkers only, the trail continues to **Indrahar Pass (4,342m)** via Lahesh Caves, adding 2-3 further days of genuine high-altitude trekking with snow underfoot even in summer. This isn't part of the standard 14-day sabbatical shape in this guide — it's a different, harder trip, better saved for a return visit once you know the region and can book a registered trekking agency or local guide.

<a id="meditation"></a>

## 8. Meditation retreats in Dharamkot

The hill village above McLeod Ganj is where the town's meditation-retreat scene actually lives, and it's built to be accessible to English-speaking beginners in a way few Indian meditation hubs are.

**Tushita Meditation Centre.** Teaches within the Tibetan Gelugpa Buddhist tradition, and its flagship roughly 10-day "Introduction to Buddhism" course includes talking, group discussion, gentle guided meditation and daily teachings from resident Western and Tibetan teachers — considerably more approachable than a silent course for a genuine first-timer. Donation-based, typically £100-200 covering the full course including simple vegetarian accommodation and meals. Also runs shorter weekend courses and drop-in sessions for visitors not ready to commit to the full 10 days. Book the flagship course 4-6 months ahead in peak season.

**Vipassana Dhamma Sikhara.** A 10-day silent course in the S.N. Goenka tradition: no talking, no eye contact, no phone, no reading or writing from the evening you arrive to the final morning, roughly 10 hours of seated meditation daily, two vegetarian meals, nightly discourse videos. Entirely free, funded by voluntary donations from past students. Apply online 6-8 weeks ahead; courses fill quickly in peak season. This is genuinely intense — several travellers leave partway through — and it should not be your first meditation experience of any kind.

**The wider ecosystem.** Beyond the two anchor centres, Dharamkot supports a rotating scene of sound-healing workshops, breathwork sessions and yoga teacher trainings, mostly bookable with a few weeks' notice through local noticeboards, cafés and word of mouth rather than formal websites. If a structured 10-day course feels like too much commitment, a 3-5 day workshop is the sensible way to test the waters before returning for the longer format.

**How to combine a course with the wider itinerary.** Most 14-day travellers either base themselves in Dharamkot for the full meditation block (Days 5-9 in the itinerary above) or stay in McLeod Ganj centre and walk or taxi up daily for day-classes — the second option trades some of the immersive quality of living on-site for more flexibility to also spend time at the Tsuglagkhang Complex and in town.

<a id="tea-gardens"></a>

## 9. Tea gardens and Palampur

Roughly 1.5 hours from McLeod Ganj, **Palampur** is the tea-growing centre of the Kangra Valley — a genuinely different register from the mountain-monastery pace of McLeod Ganj itself, and a good final-week excursion if you want a gentler day than another climb.

**What it offers.** Rolling tea estates on flat-to-gently-sloping terrain (a welcome change of pace from McLeod Ganj's steep lanes), several working tea gardens open for walks and tastings, and views back toward the Dhauladhar range from a lower, warmer vantage point. Kangra tea is a distinct, lesser-known Indian tea region compared to Darjeeling or Assam — lighter, more delicate in character — and a tasting at one of the estate cafés is a low-key, genuinely pleasant way to spend an afternoon.

**How to fit it into the 14-day shape.** It sits naturally in the Days 10-14 mountain-integration block, ideally on the day after Triund when your legs deserve a flatter walk. A private taxi for the round trip plus a couple of hours at an estate is the simplest way to do it; some Palampur estates also offer short tea-processing demonstrations if you book ahead through your McLeod Ganj hotel.

**Worth building a whole day around, or just a half-day?** A half-day is enough for most travellers — the tea gardens are a palate-cleanser within the itinerary, not a second centrepiece destination. If you have a genuinely spare 15th day, Palampur combined with the nearby Andretta Pottery village (a small artists' colony) makes a fuller day trip, but don't sacrifice a Triund or meditation day to fit it in.

<a id="best-time"></a>

## 10. Best time to fly, and the seasons explained properly

**Spring (March-May).** 12-24°C, rhododendron forests in full bloom across the lower slopes, clear views of the Dhauladhar Range on most days. One of the two best windows for the full itinerary — Triund, the Kora, Norbulingka and the Palampur tea gardens are all at their best.

**Early summer (June).** Still within the good window, warming toward the pre-monsoon build-up. Book Triund earlier in the day, as afternoon haze becomes more common as the month progresses.

**Monsoon (July-August) — avoid.** McLeod Ganj is a genuine mountain rainforest, and the mid-summer rains are heavy enough to wash out the Triund trail, turn the Bhagsu Falls path dangerous, and periodically disrupt the Dalai Lama's public teaching schedule. This is the one window in the guide with a hard "don't book" recommendation, not just a soft preference.

**Autumn (September-November).** The second of the two best windows. Post-monsoon clarity brings sharp blue skies and stunning sunsets, the Bhagsu Falls are near their most dramatic in September specifically, and this stretch runs up to the weeks before the Dalai Lama traditionally winters in Bodhgaya.

**Winter (December-February).** Genuinely cold, -2 to 8°C, with occasional snow at McLeod Ganj's elevation and more reliable snow higher up toward Triund. Atmospheric for a meditation-focused retreat — fewer crowds, a real fireside quality to the evenings — but not the season for hiking or the tea gardens, and pack proper cold-weather layers.

## 11. McLeod Ganj Survival List for UK travellers

1. **Monk etiquette.** Always walk **clockwise** around temples and prayer wheels. If the Dalai Lama is giving public teachings, you must register in person at the **Branch Security Office** at least **2 days prior** with passport + 2 passport photos.
2. **The climate.** It's a **mountain rainforest**. It can rain at any time. A high-quality **Gore-Tex jacket** is more important than a swimsuit. Layers always.
3. **The sabbatical diet.** Skip the English breakfast. Live on **momos** (steamed dumplings) and **thukpa** (Tibetan noodle soup) for 14 days. Clean, light, fuels the brain. Best momos in town: **Tibet Kitchen** and **Lhamo's Croissants**.
4. **Monkeys.** Like Hampi and Varanasi, the McLeod monkeys are residents and accomplished thieves. Keep windows locked, balcony snacks hidden, sunglasses on your face.
5. **Altitude.** McLeod Ganj is at 1,800m — gentle altitude, not a problem for most. **Triund (2,875m)** is more demanding — pace yourself, drink water, allow extra time on the climb.
6. **The Kora at dawn.** Doing the ritual Kora circuit at 6:00am, before the temple wakes, is the single most calming experience in the town. Free, takes 35 minutes, no booking. Just go.
7. **Cash.** Most cafés and small shops are cash-only. Withdraw rupees in **Delhi** before the domestic flight. ATMs in McLeod Ganj run out frequently, especially around weekends and festival days.
8. **Water.** Don't drink the tap water. Use sealed bottled water or a filtered/UV bottle — most guesthouses and cafés will refill one for a small fee.
9. **Meditation course applications.** Book Tushita or Dhamma Sikhara 4-8 weeks ahead in peak season (longer for Tushita's flagship course) — both fill up, and a walk-in application in March-June or September-November is a real gamble.
10. **Modest dress at temples.** Cover shoulders and knees when visiting the Tsuglagkhang Complex and other active places of worship, out of respect rather than strict rule enforcement.

## Who McLeod Ganj actually suits — and who should look elsewhere

McLeod Ganj is the most accessible of JetMeAway's Himalayan sabbatical guides, and it's worth being honest about who that suits best. It's the right choice for a UK professional wanting a genuine spiritual and cultural anchor for their reset — a real place of worship, a real exile community, a real museum-grade arts institute — without the demanding physical programme or complex logistics of Spiti, and without needing to build a whole trip around a daily yoga practice the way Rishikesh does. It suits first-time India travellers who want a gentle introduction: low altitude, reliable phone signal, a 45-minute drive from a domestic airport rather than a multi-day mountain transit, and an English-speaking meditation-retreat infrastructure built over six decades specifically to welcome outside visitors.

It's a weaker fit for anyone chasing genuine extreme remoteness or a serious high-altitude trekking challenge — Spiti or a dedicated Indrahar Pass expedition will deliver that instead. It's also not the trip for someone who wants a beach-and-pool holiday with a wellness label attached; McLeod Ganj's reset is built around structure, ritual and cultural immersion, not lounging. And while the town is genuinely welcoming to secular visitors, anyone deeply uncomfortable around active religious practice — daily chanting, monk debate sessions, prayer wheels everywhere you turn — may find the constant presence of Tibetan Buddhist ritual more intense than expected; it's not a neutral backdrop here, it's the whole point.
