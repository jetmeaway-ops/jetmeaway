UNESCO inscribed Kolkata's Durga Puja on its list of intangible cultural heritage. It is extraordinary, and hotels fill months in advance."

Hotel rates are at their lowest then, the city is green and the light is beautiful, so it is not a write-off, but pack for real rain and build slack into every plan."

## What to See and Do in Kolkata

Start with the **Victoria Memorial**, the vast white marble hall standing in formal gardens on the edge of the Maidan. It is the image of the city, it houses a museum of Kolkata's history, and the gardens alone are worth an hour in the early morning or the late afternoon light. Just north, the **Indian Museum** on Chowringhee is the oldest museum in India, opened in 1814, and its sprawling galleries of sculpture, fossils and coins are gloriously old-fashioned in the best way.

Then go to the river. **Howrah Bridge** is a huge cantilever span crossing the Hooghly with no bolts holding it together, carrying an astonishing weight of people and traffic every day, and the **Hooghly ferries** running underneath it are the cheapest and best way to see the city from the water. Around the Howrah end sits the flower market, busiest at dawn. North of there, **Kumartuli** is the idol-makers' quarter, a warren of open workshops where clay figures of Durga are built by hand — busiest and most extraordinary in the weeks before the festival.

The city's soul is arguably on **College Street**, where pavement stalls sell second-hand books by the mile beside Presidency and Calcutta universities, and where the **Indian Coffee House** has been the meeting place of students, writers and film-makers for generations. Kolkata is India's literary capital and that street is why. Nearby, the **Marble Palace** is a nineteenth-century mansion crammed with art and statuary, still lived in by the founding family and open to visitors who arrange it properly.

For faith, the **Kalighat Kali Temple** in the south is one of the most important Shakti pilgrimage sites in India and gives the city its name, while **Dakshineswar Kali Temple** stands beside the river to the north with its cluster of spires. **Mother House of the Missionaries of Charity**, where Mother Teresa lived, worked and is buried, is a quiet, plain building on a busy street and moving precisely because of that.

Get around the old-fashioned way where you can. Kolkata runs **India's only surviving tram network** — much reduced from its heyday, and only some routes still operate, but a slow rattle through the streets on one is a genuine experience. **Park Street** is where the city eats and drinks, with restaurants that have been institutions for decades. And above all, if you can time it, come for **Durga Puja in September or October** — days of enormous illuminated pandals, sculpted goddesses, crowds walking all night and a city entirely given over to a festival that UNESCO has recognised as intangible cultural heritage. It is the single best time to be in Kolkata, and the hardest time to find a room.

## Where to Stay in Kolkata

**Park Street and the Esplanade / Chowringhee belt** is the best base for a first visit. You are walking distance from the Victoria Memorial, the Indian Museum, the Maidan and New Market, you are on the metro, and the restaurants are on your doorstep. The Park Kolkata, The Astor and Kenilworth Hotel sit here, and The Lindsay is the budget answer to the same address.

**Salt Lake, Rajarhat and New Town** on the north-eastern side is the business and airport belt — newer office parks, the convention centre and the modern chain hotels. The Westin Kolkata Rajarhat, Taj City Centre New Town, Lemon Tree Premier and both Holiday Inns are out this way. It is efficient and comfortable, but the historic city is a long drive away, so choose it for meetings and flights rather than sightseeing.

**Ballygunge and South Kolkata** is the leafy, calm option: residential streets, the lakes, some of the best restaurants and cafés in the city, and a slower pace. The Avenue Hotel Ballygunge and Southern Plaza are the picks here. It suits repeat visitors and anyone who wants quiet evenings within an easy metro ride of the centre.

**Sudder Street** is the backpacker strip, a short walk from the Indian Museum and New Market, and it deserves an honest description: many of the rooms around there are very basic, old and small. But it is cheap and it is central, which for some travellers is the whole equation. Just book with your eyes open and read recent reviews rather than the photos.

Whichever you pick, remember the practical frame: **October to February** is the comfortable season, **March to June is very hot and humid**, **June to September is monsoon**, and **Durga Puja in September or October** books out the entire city months ahead. ## Kolkata Hotels FAQs

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Kolkata?**
Yes — UK passport holders need an Indian e-Visa, and it is NOT issued on arrival. Apply online in advance through the official Indian government e-Visa portal, well before you fly. Approval usually comes back within a few days, but leave a comfortable margin. Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages. Only use the official government portal — third-party "visa services" charge a large mark-up for exactly the same document.

**What currency is used in Kolkata?**
The Indian Rupee (INR). Every price in this guide is quoted in pounds as a rough conversion only, because that is what UK readers compare against — the hotel itself charges in rupees, and the exact sterling figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards work in hotels, malls and larger restaurants, but carry cash for taxis, trams, sweet shops and street food.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Kolkata?**
No — and this is the honest answer people are often given wrongly. There is no direct London to Kolkata service. Every route from the UK to Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose International Airport (CCU) is one-stop, either via Delhi or Mumbai on an Indian carrier, or through a Gulf hub such as Dubai, Doha or Abu Dhabi. Total journey time is roughly thirteen to fifteen hours including the connection.

**When is the best time to visit Kolkata?**
October to February. That is the cool, dry, comfortable stretch and the season the city is built around. Durga Puja in September or October is the single best time to be here if you can get a room, and December and January are the mildest, easiest weeks for straightforward sightseeing. Outside that window the weather does most of the deciding for you.

**What is Durga Puja and when does it happen?**
Durga Puja is Kolkata's biggest festival, held over several days in September or October depending on the lunar calendar. The whole city builds elaborate temporary pavilions — pandals — housing enormous sculpted idols of the goddess Durga, and hundreds of thousands of people walk between them through the night. UNESCO inscribed Kolkata's Durga Puja on its list of intangible cultural heritage. It is extraordinary, and hotels fill months in advance.

**How hot does Kolkata get?**
Very hot and very humid from March to June, with sticky nights and little relief until the rains arrive. It is draining rather than dangerous if you pace yourself, but air conditioning stops being a luxury and becomes the reason you pick one hotel over another. Sightsee early and late, and use the middle of the day for the Indian Museum, a long lunch or a coffee house.

**When is the monsoon in Kolkata?**
Roughly June to September. Kolkata gets the south-west monsoon and it is heavy — streets can waterlog quickly and traffic slows to a crawl. Hotel rates are at their lowest then, the city is green and the light is beautiful, so it is not a write-off, but pack for real rain and build slack into every plan.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Kolkata?**
Park Street or the Esplanade / Chowringhee belt. You are walking distance from the Victoria Memorial, the Indian Museum and the Maidan, on the metro, and surrounded by the city's best-known restaurants. For a first trip that central position is worth paying a little more for.

**Where should I stay for business or near the airport?**
Salt Lake, Rajarhat and New Town, on the north-eastern side of the city. That is where the newer office parks, the convention centre and most of the modern chain hotels are, and it is far closer to the airport than the centre. The trade-off is that the historic city is a long drive away — fine for meetings, less good for sightseeing.

**Where should I stay for a quieter, leafier base?**
Ballygunge and the wider South Kolkata belt. It is residential, greener and calmer than the centre, with some of the city's best restaurants and cafés, the lakes nearby, and easy metro access north into town. It suits repeat visitors and anyone who would rather come home to quiet streets.

**What is Sudder Street like?**
Sudder Street is Kolkata's long-standing backpacker strip, a short walk from the Indian Museum and New Market. Be honest with yourself about it: many of the rooms there are very basic, older and small. But it is genuinely cheap and genuinely central, and for travellers who only need a bed and a door it remains one of the best-value locations in the city.

**Is the Kolkata Metro useful for visitors?**
Yes. Kolkata's metro was India's first, opened in 1984, and the original north-south line runs right through the useful parts of the city — Kalighat, Rabindra Sadan for the Victoria Memorial, Park Street, Esplanade and on north towards Dakshineswar. It is cheap, air-conditioned on newer trains and far faster than the roads. Newer lines have extended it east and across the river.

**Can I still ride the trams in Kolkata?**
Kolkata is the only Indian city with a surviving tram network, and riding one is a genuine experience — slow, rattling, cheap and completely unlike anything else in India. The network has shrunk a great deal over the years and only a limited number of routes still run, so check locally what is operating rather than planning around it.

**What are the yellow Ambassador taxis?**
The yellow Hindustan Ambassador taxi is Kolkata's signature image, and a number are still on the road. Agree the meter or the fare before you set off. App cabs are usually easier, cheaper and less argument, but taking one yellow taxi at least once is part of the trip.

**Should I use app cabs in Kolkata?**
Yes. Uber and Ola both work well across the city, give you a fixed fare and a tracked route, and remove the haggling. Autorickshaws in Kolkata often run fixed shared routes rather than door-to-door, which surprises visitors, so apps are simpler for point-to-point trips.

**What is there to actually see in Kolkata?**
The Victoria Memorial and its gardens; Howrah Bridge and the ferries across the Hooghly; the Indian Museum, the oldest museum in India; the Kalighat and Dakshineswar temples; Mother House of the Missionaries of Charity; College Street's second-hand book market and the Indian Coffee House; Kumartuli, where the clay idols are made; the Marble Palace; and the trams.

**What is Kumartuli?**
A working neighbourhood of idol-makers in north Kolkata, where the clay figures of Durga and other deities are built by hand in narrow lanes of open workshops. It is busiest in the weeks before Durga Puja, when the streets are full of half-finished goddesses drying in the sun. Ask before photographing people at work.

**What is College Street?**
A long stretch of pavement bookstalls near Presidency University and Calcutta University, said to be the largest second-hand book market in the world. It is the heart of Kolkata's literary reputation. Halfway along is the Indian Coffee House, a high-ceilinged first-floor hall where students and writers have argued over coffee for generations.

**Is the Victoria Memorial worth visiting?**
Yes — it is the single most recognisable building in the city, a vast white marble hall set in formal gardens on the edge of the Maidan, built in the early twentieth century. Inside is a museum of Kolkata and colonial-era history. Go early or late for the light, and allow time simply to walk the gardens.

**How many days do I need in Kolkata?**
Three full days is a good measure. One for the Victoria Memorial, the Maidan, the Indian Museum and Park Street; one for the north — Howrah Bridge, the flower market, Kumartuli, College Street and the Marble Palace; and one for the temples at Kalighat and Dakshineswar plus Mother House. Add more if you are here for Durga Puja, because the pandals alone can fill several nights.

**Is Kolkata safe for tourists?**
Kolkata is widely regarded as one of India's more relaxed and welcoming big cities, and visitors generally find the atmosphere unhurried and friendly. The realistic risks are traffic, heat, waterlogged streets in monsoon and ordinary petty theft in crowded markets. Use app cabs after dark, keep valuables close in crowds, and read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for India before you go.

**Can I drink the tap water in Kolkata?**
No — drink sealed bottled or properly filtered water only, and skip ice at street stalls until your stomach has adjusted. Hotels almost always supply bottled water in the room. The food itself is one of the best reasons to come, and busy places with high turnover are usually the safest bet.

**What food should I try in Kolkata?**
Bengali fish curry — especially with hilsa or bhetki — is the heart of it, alongside mustard-heavy preparations, kathi rolls wrapped in paratha, phuchka on the street, and the sweets the city is famous for: rosogolla, sandesh and mishti doi. Kolkata biryani, with its potato and egg, is its own local variation and worth trying at least once.

The left, as in the UK — but Kolkata traffic is dense and lane discipline is looser than you will be used to. Breakfast is cheap to buy locally in Kolkata, so compare the room rate first and add breakfast afterwards if you want it.

It can be an excellent one. It is less frantic than Delhi and less expensive than Mumbai, English is widely spoken alongside Bengali, and the city rewards walking and talking rather than ticking off monuments. Go for the architecture, the books, the trams and the food rather than for headline sights, and it will be one of the trips you remember.
