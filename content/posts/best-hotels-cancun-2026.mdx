  - q: "Should I stay in the Hotel Zone or downtown Cancún?"
    a: "This is the decision that shapes your whole trip and your whole budget, and Cancún is genuinely two places. The Zona Hotelera — the Hotel Zone — is the 22km barrier-island strip of big beachfront resorts, most of them all-inclusive, shaped like a number 7 between the Caribbean and the Nichupté lagoon. It is where the beach, the nightlife and almost every famous resort name sit, and it is expensive. El Centro — downtown Cancún — is the ordinary Mexican city a few kilometres inland where local people live and work, where the ADO bus station is, and where every single one of the budget hotels in this guide actually sits. Downtown is not on the beach and does not pretend to be. Stay in the Hotel Zone if beachfront and a resort holiday are the whole point and the budget can take it; stay downtown if you want a real room for £19 to £40 and are happy to take a cheap bus to the sand."
  - q: "Why are Cancún's cheap hotels downtown and not on the beach?"
    a: "Because the beach is a fixed, finite, purpose-built resort strip and downtown is a normal city. The Zona Hotelera was master-planned in the 1970s as a tourism zone: a narrow island of beachfront land carved into large plots and sold to resort operators, so there is very little cheap accommodation on it by design. El Centro grew as the working city that houses the staff who run those resorts, and it has a normal urban hotel market with dozens of independents competing on price. That is why £19 to £40 a night buys a genuinely decent downtown room while the same money would not get you a lounger in the Hotel Zone. The honest trade is that downtown is not beachfront — you take a bus or taxi to the sand — but the R-1 and R-2 buses run the strip constantly and cheaply, so it is far less of a compromise than it sounds."

  - q: "If I route through the US to reach Cancún, do I need an ESTA?"
    a: "Yes, and this catches people out every year. If your flight connects through any airport in the United States — even if you never leave the transit area — you must hold an approved US ESTA (or a US visa) before you travel, because the US has no international transit zone and you legally enter the country to change planes. Apply for the ESTA on the official US government site well before you fly, not through a lookalike agency, and budget for the fee. If you book a genuinely direct UK-to-Cancún flight you do not touch the US at all and no ESTA is needed — which is one more reason to check whether a direct service runs on your dates."
  - q: "Do UK travellers need a visa for Mexico?"
    a: "UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Mexico. Rules and passport-validity requirements change, so confirm the current position on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly, and keep any paperwork you are given until you leave the country."
  - q: "What is the FMM Mexico tourist card?"
    a: "The FMM (Forma Migratoria Múltiple) is Mexico's tourist entry record. For years it was a paper form you filled in on the plane and had to keep until departure; at Cancún and several other airports the process is increasingly digital or handled by an entry stamp, so you may not be handed a paper slip at all. The important points for a UK visitor are simple: you do not pay separately for it as an air passenger because the fee is usually bundled into your ticket, you must not lose any document you are given, and you should check that the number of days granted covers your whole stay before walking away from the immigration desk. If in doubt, ask the officer to confirm your permitted days."

  - q: "What is sargassum seaweed and when is it worst in Cancún?"
    a: "Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts across the Atlantic and washes onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and Cancún's Hotel Zone gets it. It is typically at its worst from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach — one stretch of the strip can be clear while another a couple of kilometres away is buried, and the north-facing beaches near Punta Cancún often fare better than the long east-facing stretch. When it lands in volume it piles up on the sand and smells of sulphur as it rots, which makes swimming there unpleasant. We are not going to soft-pedal this, because it is the single biggest beach disappointment for UK visitors who arrive expecting the postcard: the resorts rake and clear their own sand each morning and some run offshore barriers, but nobody can promise you a clear turquoise sea on a given week."

Nobody can guarantee the sea, so build a little flexibility into how you picture your days."

Most trips in that window pass without a named storm and the more common effect is a heavy afternoon downpour that clears as fast as it arrives, but the risk is real, and it is one of the main reasons late-summer prices are so low. Take travel insurance that specifically covers weather disruption, keep a little flexibility in your dates if you can, and follow the forecasts in the run-up. Cancún is a well-drilled resort city that handles storm season every year, but flights and excursions can be disrupted, so it pays to be prepared rather than surprised."

**Should I stay in the Hotel Zone or downtown Cancún?**

Stay in the Hotel Zone if beachfront and a resort holiday are the whole point and the budget can take it; stay downtown if you want a real room for £19 to £40 and are happy to take a cheap bus to the sand.

**Why are Cancún's cheap hotels downtown and not on the beach?**

Because the beach is a fixed, finite, purpose-built resort strip and downtown is a normal city. The Zona Hotelera was master-planned in the 1970s as a tourism zone: a narrow island of beachfront land carved into large plots and sold to resort operators, so there is very little cheap accommodation on it by design. El Centro grew as the working city that houses the staff who run those resorts, and it has a normal urban hotel market with dozens of independents competing on price. That is why £19 to £40 a night buys a genuinely decent downtown room while the same money would not get you a lounger in the Hotel Zone. The honest trade is that downtown is not beachfront — you take a bus or taxi to the sand — but the R-1 and R-2 buses run the strip constantly and cheaply, so it is far less of a compromise than it sounds.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Cancún?**

Often, yes — but check your specific route rather than assuming. Cancún (CUN) is one of the best-connected leisure airports in the Americas, and there are frequently direct services from the UK, particularly on TUI, British Airways and Virgin Atlantic out of London and some regional airports, mostly as scheduled or charter holiday flights. Frequency and the exact departure airports change by season, so search your own dates: you may find a direct flight, or you may be routed one-stop through a US hub or Mexico City. The direct option is worth paying a little more for on a journey this long, and it also sidesteps the US-transit paperwork trap covered below.

**How long is the flight from the UK to Cancún?**

A direct flight from London to Cancún takes roughly 10 to 11 hours. Add a couple of hours or more if you route one-stop through a US gateway such as Miami, Dallas or Atlanta, or through Mexico City, once you count the connection. Cancún is on Eastern Standard Time and does not observe daylight saving, so the time difference from the UK is around five to six hours behind depending on the season — which, combined with the long westbound flight, means the first evening is usually an early night.

**If I route through the US to reach Cancún, do I need an ESTA?**

Yes, and this catches people out every year. If your flight connects through any airport in the United States — even if you never leave the transit area — you must hold an approved US ESTA (or a US visa) before you travel, because the US has no international transit zone and you legally enter the country to change planes. Apply for the ESTA on the official US government site well before you fly, not through a lookalike agency, and budget for the fee. If you book a genuinely direct UK-to-Cancún flight you do not touch the US at all and no ESTA is needed — which is one more reason to check whether a direct service runs on your dates.

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Mexico?**

UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Mexico. You clear Mexico's immigration on arrival — historically via the FMM tourist card, now handled digitally or by stamp at many airports including Cancún — and the officer records the number of days you are permitted to stay, so check the stamp or slip before you leave the desk. Rules and passport-validity requirements change, so confirm the current position on the UK government travel advice pages before you fly, and keep any paperwork you are given until you leave the country.

**What is the FMM Mexico tourist card?**

The FMM (Forma Migratoria Múltiple) is Mexico's tourist entry record. For years it was a paper form you filled in on the plane and had to keep until departure; at Cancún and several other airports the process is increasingly digital or handled by an entry stamp, so you may not be handed a paper slip at all. The important points for a UK visitor are simple: you do not pay separately for it as an air passenger because the fee is usually bundled into your ticket, you must not lose any document you are given, and you should check that the number of days granted covers your whole stay before walking away from the immigration desk. If in doubt, ask the officer to confirm your permitted days.

**What currency does Cancún use — pesos or dollars?**

The Mexican Peso (MXN) is the currency, and it is what you should try to pay in. Cancún is unusually dollarised because of its US visitor numbers — plenty of resorts, tours and Hotel Zone bars quote and take US dollars — but the exchange rate you get paying in dollars is almost always worse than paying in pesos, and change frequently comes back in pesos at the seller's own rate. Pay in pesos where you can, draw cash from ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than the freestanding machines in the Hotel Zone, carry small notes for buses and tips, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, decline and let your own bank do the conversion.

**Is all-inclusive worth it in Cancún, or should I book room-only?**

It depends entirely on how you plan to spend your days, and the honest answer is that all-inclusive suits some trips and quietly wastes money on others. If you want a resort holiday where you barely leave the property — beach, pools, buffets, bars, kids' club, evening entertainment — then a Hotel Zone all-inclusive can be genuinely good value, because eating and drinking à la carte in the Zona adds up fast. If you intend to be out most days at Chichén Itzá, on Isla Mujeres, at cenotes or exploring downtown and the Riviera Maya, you will be paying for meals you are not eating, and a room-only downtown hotel plus local restaurants works out far cheaper. Do the sums for your actual itinerary rather than assuming all-inclusive is automatically the deal, and always check exactly which meals, drinks, snacks and activities a given all-inclusive rate covers, because they vary widely.

**What is sargassum seaweed and when is it worst in Cancún?**

Sargassum is a brown floating seaweed that drifts across the Atlantic and washes onto Caribbean-facing beaches, and Cancún's Hotel Zone gets it. It is typically at its worst from roughly April to August, but it varies enormously from year to year, week to week and even beach to beach — one stretch of the strip can be clear while another a couple of kilometres away is buried, and the north-facing beaches near Punta Cancún often fare better than the long east-facing stretch. When it lands in volume it piles up on the sand and smells of sulphur as it rots, which makes swimming there unpleasant. We are not going to soft-pedal this, because it is the single biggest beach disappointment for UK visitors who arrive expecting the postcard: the resorts rake and clear their own sand each morning and some run offshore barriers, but nobody can promise you a clear turquoise sea on a given week.

**How can I check the sargassum situation before I travel?**

If clear water is the priority, travel outside roughly April to August, choose a resort with a strong pool complex so a bad seaweed week does not sink the whole trip, and remember that Isla Mujeres, Cozumel's sheltered western shore and the freshwater cenotes are all much less affected than the open Hotel Zone beach. **When is hurricane season in Cancún?**

Atlantic hurricane season runs roughly June to November, with the highest risk from August to October. Most trips in that window pass without a named storm and the more common effect is a heavy afternoon downpour that clears as fast as it arrives, but the risk is real, and it is one of the main reasons late-summer prices are so low. Take travel insurance that specifically covers weather disruption, keep a little flexibility in your dates if you can, and follow the forecasts in the run-up. Cancún is a well-drilled resort city that handles storm season every year, but flights and excursions can be disrupted, so it pays to be prepared rather than surprised.

**Why is late summer so cheap in Cancún?**

Two reasons stack up in August, September and October: it is the peak of hurricane season, and it overlaps with the worst of the sargassum window. Demand drops, so room rates and flight prices fall sharply, and you can find genuine bargains on Hotel Zone resorts that are eye-wateringly expensive at Christmas or Easter. Whether that trade is worth it depends on your appetite for risk and your flexibility: you are gambling on the weather and the seaweed in exchange for a much lower price. **Is the tap water safe to drink in Cancún?**

Downtown El Centro is more Spanish-speaking, but you will still get by, and a few polite words — hola, por favor, gracias, la cuenta — go a long way with taxi drivers, market stalls and family-run places and are always appreciated. A translation app on your phone covers the rest.
