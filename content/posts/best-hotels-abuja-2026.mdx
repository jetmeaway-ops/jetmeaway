## What to See in Abuja

Start with what makes Abuja unlike anywhere else in Nigeria: it is a **purpose-built capital**, planned from the 1980s and moved here from Lagos precisely because the country needed a neutral, central seat of government. The result is a city of wide boulevards, generous green space, low-density districts and a calm that genuinely surprises first-time visitors. If your mental image of Nigeria is Lagos traffic and Lagos noise, Abuja will not match it — and that is a useful distinction to know before you book.

The city is built around **Aso Rock**, a huge granite monolith on its eastern edge. The Presidential Villa, the National Assembly and the Supreme Court all sit at its foot, and the whole government quarter takes its informal name from the rock. You cannot roam the presidential complex, but the rock itself looms over the skyline from almost everywhere. Along **Independence Avenue** stand two of the most photographed buildings in the country, deliberately facing each other: the gold-domed **Nigerian National Mosque** and the soaring **National Christian Centre**. Seeing them across the road from one another tells you something real about how Nigeria understands itself.

For green space and everyday city life, **Millennium Park** is Nigeria's largest public park, a wide, formal expanse in Maitama that fills with families at weekends. **Jabi Lake** and the adjoining Jabi Lake Mall give you a waterside walk, boats and the city's best mall in one stop. The **Arts and Crafts Village** in the Central Business District is the place for Nigerian craft — carvings, textiles, leather and beadwork, with bargaining expected and good-humoured. For the view, drivers take visitors up to the **Katampe Hills** viewpoint, which lays the whole planned grid of the city out below you, and **Usuma Dam** is a pleasant half-day for water and greenery. The big day trip is **Zuma Rock**, an even larger monolith about 45 minutes north on the Kaduna road near Suleja, familiar to every Nigerian from the old 100 naira note.

Beyond sightseeing, Abuja works as **the calm business-and-government base** and as the gateway for onward domestic flights across Nigeria. Before you book any of it, read the current [UK FCDO travel advice for Nigeria](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/nigeria) — it is region-specific and matters here more than in most places — and start your visa application through the official [Nigeria Immigration Service](https://immigration.gov.ng/) well ahead of travel.

## Choosing Where to Stay in Abuja

- **Maitama and Asokoro** — the smartest, greenest and most secure districts, full of embassies and quiet tree-lined streets, with Asokoro closest to the government quarter. The calmest place to stay, though you will use a car for everything.
- **Wuse II** — the best concentration of restaurants, bars, cafés and business offices. The easiest base if you want life in the evening without leaving the smart side of the city.
- **The Central Business District** — where the big-name hotels, conference venues and the Arts and Crafts Village sit. Central and practical for meetings.
- **Garki and Utako** — better value at a plainer standard, a short drive from the centre, and where a lot of the cheapest listings in this guide are.
- **Near Nnamdi Azikiwe (ABV) airport** — the practical choice before a very early flight. Be honest with yourself about the distance: the airport is roughly 40 minutes from the centre, so an airport-side night genuinely saves you something.

## Staying Safe in Abuja — the Honest Version

Abuja is generally considered calmer and safer than Lagos, and its planned layout, low density and heavy official presence are a large part of why. But Nigeria as a whole requires care, and pretending otherwise would not help you.

The practical habits are simple. **Use hotel cars or trusted ride-hailing apps** rather than hailing anything on the street. **Do not travel by road between cities after dark** — fly domestic legs instead, which is what most visitors and residents do. Keep phones, cash and jewellery out of sight, use the hotel safe, draw money inside banks or malls rather than at street ATMs, and ask your hotel's reception which specific areas and routes to avoid at which times; they give straight, current answers.

Most importantly, **read the UK FCDO travel advice for Nigeria before you book**. This is not boilerplate: the guidance is **region-specific**, and parts of Nigeria carry advise-against-all-travel or advise-against-all-but-essential-travel warnings while other parts do not. Travelling against that advice can also invalidate your travel insurance. Check the page before you commit to flights and hotels, and check it again shortly before you fly.

## Abuja Hotels FAQs

**Do UK travellers need a visa for Nigeria?**
Yes. Nigeria is **not visa-free** for UK passport holders, and there is **no simple visa-on-arrival system** for most travellers. You must **apply in advance** through the official Nigerian Immigration Service e-visa and visa process before you fly. Start early — supporting documents and processing time are involved — and check current rules on the official immigration site and the UK FCDO travel advice page before booking.

**What currency is used in Abuja?**
The **Nigerian Naira (NGN/₦)**. Card acceptance is improving in Abuja hotels, malls and larger restaurants, but **cash still matters** for taxis, markets and smaller places. Exchange rates have moved a great deal in recent years, so treat every pound figure here as a rough conversion for guidance only, not an exact quote.

**Are there direct flights from London to Abuja?**
Yes. **British Airways flies direct from London to Nnamdi Azikiwe International Airport (ABV) in roughly 6.5 hours.** There are also one-stop options via the Gulf hubs and European cities, which are often cheaper if you do not mind a connection.

**Is Abuja safe for tourists?**
Abuja is generally considered **calmer and safer than Lagos**, and it is a planned, low-density capital with a heavy official presence. That said, Nigeria as a whole requires care. Use hotel cars or trusted ride apps rather than hailing on the street, avoid road travel between cities after dark, keep valuables out of sight, and use the hotel safe. Read the UK FCDO travel advice for Nigeria before booking.

**Do I need to read the FCDO advice before travelling to Nigeria?**
Yes, and it matters more here than for most destinations. The advice is **region-specific**: some states carry advise-against-travel warnings while others do not, and travel insurance can be invalidated if you go against it. Read it before you book flights or hotels, and again shortly before you fly.

**Do I need a yellow fever certificate for Nigeria?**
⚠️ Yes. A **yellow fever vaccination certificate is required for entry to Nigeria**, and you should carry it with your passport. Arrange the vaccination well ahead of travel through your GP or a travel clinic — it needs time to take effect and appointments can be scarce.

**Is there malaria in Abuja?**
⚠️ **Malaria is a real risk** throughout Nigeria, including Abuja. **See your GP or a travel clinic well before you fly** about antimalarial tablets, and pack repellent, cover up at dusk and use the air conditioning or nets your hotel provides. This is general information, not medical advice.

**When is the best time to visit Abuja?**
The **dry season, roughly November to March**. Within it, the **Harmattan** blows dusty Saharan haze down from around **December to February**, which cools things but dulls the light. The **rainy season runs roughly April to October**, when Abuja is greenest but downpours are heavy.

**What is the Harmattan?**
A dry, dusty wind carrying Saharan sand across West Africa from roughly December to February. In Abuja it means hazy skies, cooler nights and very dry air. Not dangerous for most visitors, but pack accordingly if you have asthma or sensitive sinuses.

**Abuja or Lagos — which should I visit?**
They are genuinely different cities. **Abuja is the purpose-built capital** — greener, calmer, more orderly, far less congested — and suits business, government meetings and a gentler first taste of Nigeria. **Lagos is the commercial and cultural engine**: enormous, loud, creative and exhausting, and the place for music, art and nightlife. Many trips take in both.

**Which area of Abuja should I stay in?**
**Maitama and Asokoro** for the smartest, greenest, most secure diplomatic districts; **Wuse II** for restaurants, bars and business; the **Central Business District** for the big-name hotels and conference venues; **Garki and Utako** for better value; and **near ABV airport** for early departures.

**How far is Abuja airport from the city?**
Roughly **40 minutes** from central Abuja on the airport expressway, longer in traffic or rain. For a very early flight, an airport-side room or a pre-booked hotel car is worth the money. Arrange transfers through your hotel rather than picking up a ride outside the terminal.

**How do I get around Abuja?**
**Hotel cars and trusted ride-hailing apps.** Abuja is a low-density, car-based city with wide roads and long distances between districts, so walking between areas is not realistic. Avoid hailing taxis on the street, and avoid road travel between cities after dark.

**Can I drink the tap water in Abuja?**
No — stick to **bottled or properly filtered water**. Hotels provide bottled water and many have filtered dispensers. Use bottled water for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach, and be careful with ice and raw salads at the cheapest stalls.

**How many days do you need in Abuja?**
**Two to three days** covers the city: Aso Rock and the government quarter, the National Mosque and the National Christian Centre, Millennium Park, Jabi Lake and the Arts and Crafts Village. Add a half day for Zuma Rock, and a fourth if you want to slow down or use Abuja as a base before an onward domestic flight.

**What is Aso Rock?**
A huge **granite monolith** on the city's edge that Abuja was effectively built around. The Presidential Villa, National Assembly and Supreme Court sit at its foot, and the government quarter is known informally as Aso Rock. You cannot wander the presidential complex, but the rock dominates the skyline from all over the city.

**What is Zuma Rock?**
A far larger monolith about **45 minutes north on the Kaduna road**, near Suleja, rising several hundred metres straight out of the plain. It appeared on the old 100 naira note and is one of Nigeria's most recognisable landmarks. Most visitors go with a driver for photographs rather than climbing it.

**What is there to see in Abuja?**
Aso Rock and the government quarter; the **Nigerian National Mosque** and the **National Christian Centre** facing each other across Independence Avenue; **Millennium Park**, Nigeria's largest public park; **Jabi Lake** and Jabi Lake Mall; the **Arts and Crafts Village**; the **Katampe Hills** viewpoint; **Usuma Dam**; and **Zuma Rock** on a half-day trip north.

Yes. Abuja is the calm business-and-government base and a **gateway for onward domestic flights**. Flying rather than driving between cities is the standard advice for visitors. Check the FCDO region-specific guidance before planning any onward legs.
