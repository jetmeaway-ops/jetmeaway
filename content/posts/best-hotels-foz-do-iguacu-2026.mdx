Here is the single most useful thing to understand before you book anything. **There are two sides to the falls, and they are different experiences.** The **Brazilian side** is the panorama: one cliff-side trail looking straight across at the full sweep of the falls, ending on a walkway that puts you in the spray. It is the photograph everyone has seen, and you can do it properly in roughly half a day. The **Argentine side** is bigger — several circuits of walkways at different levels, a small park train, and the catwalk that carries you right out over the **Devil's Throat (Garganta del Diablo)**, the enormous horseshoe where most of the river disappears. That side deserves a full day. Most visitors do both, one day each, and cross the border in between, which is routine — but Argentina is a separate country with its own entry rules, so check them before you go and take your passport.

## What to See and Do in Foz do Iguaçu

Everything starts with the **Iguazu Falls**. This is a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the New 7 Wonders of Nature: **275 individual waterfalls** strung along nearly 3km of the Iguaçu river where it drops into a basalt gorge on the Brazil–Argentina border. It is wider than Victoria Falls and taller than Niagara, and nothing you have seen in photographs prepares you for the noise.

**The Brazilian side** is the panorama. From the visitor centre a park shuttle drops you at the head of a single cliff-side trail that runs for a bit over a kilometre with the entire sweep of the falls in front of you, ending in a walkway that pushes out into the spray below the Salto Floriano and a lift up to a viewing platform. It is the classic wide-angle view, it is the easier of the two sides to walk, and you can do it thoroughly in **roughly half a day** — which is why so many people pair it with Parque das Aves in the same visit.

**The Argentine side** is the immersion. The Parque Nacional Iguazú is considerably larger, with an Upper Circuit and a Lower Circuit of walkways at different heights, a small park train, and a long catwalk across the top of the river that ends directly over the **Devil's Throat (Garganta del Diablo)** — the vast U-shaped chasm where the bulk of the water goes over. Give it a **full day**; people routinely underestimate the walking. Both sides also run boat trips into the spray beneath the falls, the **Macuco Safari** in Brazil and the Gran Aventura in Argentina.

The honest summary is that **most visitors do both, one day each**, and that if you only manage one you should pick Brazil for the view and Argentina for the experience. Crossing the border between the two is routine — the Tancredo Neves bridge, a short drive, immigration on both sides — and tours, transfers and taxis all do the run. Take your passport, and **check Argentina's own entry requirements before you travel**, because it is a separate country from Brazil with separate rules.

Right by the Brazilian park entrance, **Parque das Aves** is a bird park with big walk-through aviaries full of toucans, macaws and other Atlantic Forest species, and it takes an hour and a half to two hours — an easy addition to a Brazilian-side morning. Across town, the **Itaipu Dam** is one of the world's largest hydroelectric dams, built jointly by Brazil and Paraguay on the Paraná river; panoramic and technical tours both run daily and it makes a solid half-day. The **Marco das Três Fronteiras** is the riverside obelisk marking the point where Brazil, Argentina and Paraguay meet, and it is best at sunset. For the aerial version of the falls there are short **helicopter flights** from the Brazilian side. And if you want spectacle of an entirely different kind, day trips run across the Friendship Bridge to **Ciudad del Este** in Paraguay, a huge and chaotic duty-free shopping city — cheap electronics, dense crowds, and a real need to watch your bags.

Before you book, read the UK government's current [foreign travel advice for Brazil](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/brazil), which covers entry requirements, safety and health, and check it again in the week before you fly. For regional information, opening hours and events, Brazil's official tourism site [Visit Brasil](https://www.visitbrasil.com/) is the reliable starting point.

## Where to Stay in Foz do Iguaçu: Choosing Your Base

There are really two decisions here, and the right answer depends on whether you want a pool or a restaurant scene.

**The Rodovia das Cataratas — the falls road** — runs from the city out past the airport to the national park entrance, and it is where the resort hotels sit. Staying here means the shortest run to the park gates, quieter and greener surroundings, bigger grounds and better pools, and an airport that is on the same road. The trade-off is that you will take a taxi every time you want dinner somewhere other than the hotel restaurant.

**The city centre** is where the budget hostels, pousadas and small hotels in this guide mostly are, along with the restaurants, shops, supermarkets and the bus terminal. Taxis and buses are cheap, and the park entrance is roughly 20 to 25 minutes away. For solo travellers, couples on a budget and anyone who likes to walk out to eat, the centre is the better base — and it is where the £10 to £45 rooms live.

**Inside the national park** there is exactly one hotel: **[Hotel das Cataratas, A Belmond Hotel](/hotels/la_lp39093?city=Foz%20do%20Iguacu)**, the famous pink colonial-style property, and the only way to be on the falls trail before the gates open and after they close. It is also the most expensive room in this guide at around £599 a night, so it is a very specific kind of splurge.

A couple of other districts show up in the budget list. **Vila Portes** and the streets near the Friendship Bridge put you minutes from the Paraguay crossing but further from everything else. **Vila Yolanda** and similar residential areas are quiet and cheap and a short ride from the centre. Brazil drives on the right, but you do not need a car here — taxis are inexpensive, the park runs its own internal shuttles, and organised trips cover the Argentine side and Itaipu without any driving on your part.

## Practicalities: What Nobody Tells You Before You Go

**Allow at least two full days.** One day on each side of the falls is the minimum that does the place justice, and a third day gives you room for Itaipu, Parque das Aves or a boat trip without rushing. People who budget a single day almost always regret it, because a good chunk of that day disappears into transfers and queues.

**The falls run all year, but the water volume changes.** Flow peaks roughly **December to March**, which is also the hottest, most humid and busiest stretch — maximum thunder, maximum spray, maximum crowds and the highest hotel prices. **April to June and September to November** are milder and quieter, with the falls still hugely impressive and the walking far more comfortable. In a very dry year flow can drop noticeably; after exceptionally heavy rain, walkways or the Devil's Throat catwalk occasionally close for safety. Neither is common enough to plan around, but both are worth knowing.

**You will get wet.** The lower walkway on the Brazilian side and the Devil's Throat catwalk on the Argentine side both put you in constant drifting spray, and on a windy day it is genuinely like standing in heavy rain. Bring or buy a cheap **poncho**, carry a **dry bag or waterproof pouch** for your phone and camera, wear shoes with grip because the boards get slippery, and pack a spare top. If you are doing a boat trip, assume everything not in a sealed bag will be soaked.

Two last practical notes. Park entry is paid separately on each side and in each country's currency, so a card and a little cash are both useful. And the **coatis** — the ring-tailed animals that patrol the walkways looking friendly — are wild animals that scratch and bite for food; keep snacks sealed and do not feed them.

## Foz do Iguaçu Hotels FAQs

**How much does a hotel in Foz do Iguaçu cost per night?**
On ordinary midweek dates, real bookable rooms in this guide start at around £10 a night for a private room in a shared house in the city centre and run to about £599 a night at Hotel das Cataratas, A Belmond Hotel — the only hotel inside the Brazilian national park and by far the most expensive property here. A solid 3★ or 4★ in the city typically lands between £30 and £115, and the other 5★ hotels sit between £125 and £250. Foz is genuinely cheap for a world-famous attraction.

**Which side of Iguazu Falls is better — Brazil or Argentina?**
They are different, not better or worse, and most people do both. The Brazilian side gives you the panoramic wide-angle view: you walk a single cliff-side trail looking across at the whole 3km of falls, and it can be done comfortably in about half a day. The Argentine side is much larger, with several circuits of walkways, a little park train and the catwalk that takes you out over the Devil's Throat (Garganta del Diablo) — allow a full day there. If you only have time for one, choose Brazil for the photograph and Argentina for the experience. The classic plan is one day each.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Brazil?**
No. UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Brazil and can normally stay up to 90 days. You will need a passport valid for the length of your stay, and border officers may ask for proof of onward travel and accommodation. Entry rules change, so check the current UK government advice for Brazil before you book and again in the week before you fly. If you plan to cross to the Argentine side, check Argentina's own entry rules separately — they are a different country with their own requirements.

**Is it easy to cross the border to the Argentine side?**
It is routine, and thousands of visitors do it every day, but it is a real international border and you must take your passport. You cross the Tancredo Neves bridge from Foz do Iguaçu to Puerto Iguazú in Argentina, and both sets of immigration officials will normally stamp you out and in. Organised day tours, shared transfers and taxis all do the run; public buses exist but can be slower and some do not wait at immigration. Allow extra time at the border on weekends and Brazilian holidays, and check Argentina's entry requirements for UK passport holders before you travel.

**How many days do you need in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Two full days is the realistic minimum if you want to see both sides of the falls properly — one day for the Brazilian panorama and Parque das Aves, one day for the Argentine circuits and the Devil's Throat. Three days is the comfortable version, giving you room for the Itaipu Dam, the Marco das Três Fronteiras or a boat ride under the falls. One day only gets you one side, and you will spend a chunk of it travelling.

**What currency does Foz do Iguaçu use?**
The Brazilian Real, written BRL or R$. **Are there direct flights from the UK to Foz do Iguaçu?**
No. There is no direct UK service to Foz do Iguaçu. The normal routing is a long-haul flight to São Paulo Guarulhos (GRU) or Rio de Janeiro, then a domestic flight of roughly two hours to Foz do Iguaçu International (IGU). Some travellers instead fly to Buenos Aires and take a domestic flight to Puerto Iguazú on the Argentine side. Allow a full travel day in each direction and give yourself a comfortable connection window in São Paulo, where you may need to clear immigration and re-check bags.

**Where is the airport and how far are the hotels?**
Foz do Iguaçu International (IGU) sits on the Rodovia das Cataratas — the falls road — between the city and the national park entrance, which is unusually convenient. The resort hotels along that road are minutes away, and the city centre is roughly 15 to 20 minutes by car. Taxis and app-based rides are cheap and plentiful. Several of the budget properties in this guide are in the centre, so factor in a short ride each way.

**When is the best time to visit Iguazu Falls?**
The falls run all year and there is no bad time, but the character changes. Water volume is at its highest roughly from December to March, which is also the hottest, most humid and busiest period — the falls are at their most thunderous and the spray is heaviest. April to June and September to November are milder, quieter and generally more comfortable for walking the trails, with the falls still very impressive. Occasional low-water spells can happen in a dry year, and after very heavy rain some walkways or the Devil's Throat catwalk may close temporarily.

**Will I get wet at the falls?**
Yes, especially on the Brazilian side's lower walkway and at the Devil's Throat on the Argentine side. Spray drifts constantly and on a windy day it feels like standing in rain. Bring a cheap poncho — they are sold everywhere near the entrances — and a dry bag or waterproof pouch for your phone and camera. Wear shoes with grip, because the walkways get slippery, and pack a spare top if you are going straight on somewhere afterwards.

**Is Parque das Aves worth visiting?**
For most visitors, yes. Parque das Aves is a bird park right by the Brazilian national park entrance, with large walk-through aviaries where toucans, macaws and other Atlantic Forest species fly around you. It takes an hour and a half to two hours, it sits within walking distance of the falls entrance, and it pairs neatly with a half-day on the Brazilian side. Families in particular tend to rate it highly.

**Is the Itaipu Dam worth a visit?**
It is one of the world's largest hydroelectric dams, built jointly by Brazil and Paraguay on the Paraná river, and it is genuinely impressive at the scale of engineering. Tours run daily in several formats — a panoramic bus tour along the crest, and longer technical visits inside the structure — and there is an evening illuminated visit on some dates. Book ahead, and treat it as a half-day. If dams do not interest you at all, spend that time on the Argentine side instead.

**What is the Marco das Três Fronteiras?**
The Landmark of the Three Borders: a riverside viewpoint in Foz do Iguaçu where the Iguaçu river meets the Paraná and Brazil, Argentina and Paraguay all meet at one point. Each country has its own obelisk on its own bank. It is a short visit rather than a half-day, best at sunset, and there is a small complex with food and evening shows. Pure novelty, but a good one.

**Can you go on a boat ride under the falls?**
Yes — the Macuco Safari on the Brazilian side and the Gran Aventura on the Argentine side both run inflatable boats up the river and directly into the spray beneath the falls. You will be thoroughly soaked; that is the point. Waterproof bags are provided for valuables, but leave anything you cannot risk at the hotel. It is the single most popular paid add-on at Iguazu and worth booking ahead in peak season.

**Can you take a helicopter flight over the falls?**
Yes, from the Brazilian side, near the national park entrance. Flights are short — typically around ten minutes — and give the aerial view of the horseshoe that you cannot get from the walkways. They are not cheap and they are weather-dependent. Note that scenic overflights operate on the Brazilian side rather than the Argentine one.

**Which area should I stay in in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Two sensible choices. The Rodovia das Cataratas — the falls road running from the city out to the national park — is where the resort hotels sit, closest to the park entrance and the airport, quieter and greener, but you will drive or taxi for meals. The city centre is where the budget hostels, pousadas and small hotels are, with restaurants, shops and cheap taxis and buses on the doorstep, roughly 20 to 25 minutes from the park entrance. Families and anyone wanting a pool tend to prefer the falls road; solo travellers and budget trips are usually better off in the centre.

**Is there a hotel inside the national park?**
Yes — one. Hotel das Cataratas, A Belmond Hotel is the famous pink colonial-style hotel inside the Brazilian national park, and it is the only accommodation within the gates. Staying there means you can walk the falls trail early in the morning and again in the evening, when day visitors have gone and the park is effectively yours. It is the most expensive property in this guide at around £599 a night, and that access is exactly what you are paying for.

**Is Foz do Iguaçu safe for tourists?**
Foz is a tourism town and the visitor areas are used to foreigners, but it is also a busy border city, so normal Brazilian city precautions apply. Use taxis or app rides after dark rather than walking between districts, keep your phone out of sight on the street, do not carry more cash than you need, and leave valuables in the hotel safe. The national park, Parque das Aves and Itaipu are all straightforward. Take extra care with crowds and pickpockets on a shopping day in Ciudad del Este. Read the UK government's travel advice for Brazil before you go.

**Is the shopping trip to Ciudad del Este worth it?**
It depends what you want. Ciudad del Este, just across the Friendship Bridge in Paraguay, is a huge duty-free shopping city and a genuine spectacle — chaotic, crowded and cheap for electronics and perfume. It is also the most intense experience of the three-borders area: heavy crowds, long bridge queues and a real pickpocket risk. Go with a guide or a tour if you are unsure, take your passport, watch your bags, and be aware of Brazilian customs allowances on the way back.

**Do you need to speak Portuguese in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Brazilians speak Portuguese, not Spanish, and while Foz is more used to visitors than most Brazilian cities, English is far from universal outside the bigger hotels and the park. A few phrases go a long way: bom dia, por favor, obrigado if you are a man or obrigada if you are a woman. On the Argentine side you are in Spanish, and in Paraguay Spanish and Guaraní. An offline translation app earns its keep here more than in most places, because you may use three languages in two days.

**Can I drink the tap water in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Stick to bottled or filtered water. The mains supply is treated but building tanks and older plumbing mean quality varies, and most visitors drink bottled. It is cheap and sold everywhere, and many hotels have filtered dispensers, so carry a refillable bottle. You will drink more than you expect walking the falls trails in the heat.

**Do I need a car in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Not really. Taxis and app rides are cheap, the falls road is a straight run from the city, and the national park runs its own shuttle buses from the visitor centre to the trailheads. Organised day trips cover the Argentine side and Itaipu comfortably. A car only makes sense if you want total independence, and if you hire one, note that Brazil drives on the right and that taking a rental across an international border usually needs prior authorisation from the hire company.

**Are hotel breakfasts included in Foz do Iguaçu?**
Very often, yes. Brazil has a strong hotel breakfast culture, and a café da manhã of tropical fruit, breads, cakes, cheese, eggs and good coffee is included at many mid-range and even budget properties here. It is not universal — self-catering flats and the cheapest shared-house rooms usually exclude it — so check the room page before you book. A big breakfast is worth having on a falls day, because park food is expensive.

**Do Foz do Iguaçu hotels have pools and air conditioning?**
Air conditioning is effectively standard and you will want it, because summers here are hot and humid. Pools are extremely common — even modest 3★ city hotels often have one — and the resort properties along the falls road build their whole offer around them. **Are the coatis in the park dangerous?**
They are not dangerous in the way a big animal is, but they are wild animals and they will scratch or bite for food. Coatis — quatis in Portuguese — patrol the walkways on both sides of the falls in large family groups, and they have learned that visitors carry snacks. Keep food sealed inside your bag, do not hand them anything, and keep small children close when a group approaches. Park signage says the same thing, and it is worth taking seriously.

**Is Foz do Iguaçu good for families?**
Very. The falls themselves work for most ages — the Brazilian side is a straightforward walk with a pushchair-friendly stretch, though the Argentine circuits involve more stairs and distance. Parque das Aves is a reliable hit with children, the boat trips are a highlight for older kids, and almost every hotel here has a pool, which matters after a hot day out. The resort hotels on the falls road are the easiest base for a family.
