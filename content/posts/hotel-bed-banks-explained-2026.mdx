## What a Bed Bank Actually Is

A "bed bank" is a business-to-business (B2B) hotel wholesaler. Picture the hotel supply chain as three layers stacked on top of each other: **hotels** at the bottom, contracting bulk inventory to **bed banks** in the middle at negotiated wholesale rates, who then redistribute that inventory to **thousands of downstream buyers** — travel agencies, tour operators, airlines building flight-plus-hotel packages, and comparison engines like JetMeAway — who in turn sell (or, in JetMeAway's case, compare and pass through) that inventory to the actual traveller.

The critical thing to understand: **a bed bank never sells to you directly.** You will not find LiteAPI.com or the RateHawk consumer site set up to take your booking with a marketing homepage and customer reviews, because that's not their business model. Their customers are travel businesses, not travellers. If you've booked a hotel through almost any comparison site, meta-search engine, airline package deal, or smaller regional travel agency in the last decade, there's a reasonable chance a bed bank supplied some or all of that inventory somewhere in the chain — you just never saw its name, because it was relabelled by whichever retail-facing platform you were using.

This is also why bed banks are sometimes called "wholesale hotel distributors," "hotel API aggregators" or simply "hotel wholesalers" in trade press — different terms for the same layer of the market. In 2026 the dominant technical model is **API-first**: a bed bank exposes its hotel inventory, live pricing and booking flow through a programming interface (an API) that a platform like JetMeAway connects to directly, rather than the older model of manually-negotiated paper contracts and phone-based booking that dominated the pre-2010s wholesale travel industry.

<a id="history"></a>

## A Short History of the Bed Bank

The wholesale hotel model didn't appear overnight — it grew out of the package-holiday industry's own logistics problem, decades before "comparison engine" was a phrase anyone used.

**The pre-internet era.** Long before online travel existed, tour operators building package holidays needed a way to guarantee blocks of hotel rooms across dozens of destinations without personally negotiating with every property. Specialist wholesale desks — often a division inside a large tour operator, or an independent broker — began contracting bulk allocations directly with hotels and reselling them to smaller agencies and operators who couldn't negotiate hotel deals at that scale themselves. This is the direct ancestor of the modern bed bank: a business that exists purely to sit between the hotel and the retail seller.

**The rise of Hotelbeds and the 2000s consolidation.** As package travel scaled through the 1990s and 2000s, a handful of wholesale operators grew to dominate the space — Hotelbeds chief among them, eventually becoming deeply embedded in TUI's own supply chain and much of the UK high-street travel-agency market. This era cemented the basic economics still in place today: wholesale contracts negotiated on volume, resold downstream to a long tail of smaller retail buyers who could never have negotiated equivalent rates on their own.

**The API-first shift (2015 onward).** The biggest structural change in the last decade has been technical, not commercial: bed banks moved from manually-negotiated paper contracts and phone- or fax-based booking toward live, API-driven inventory that any developer can connect to programmatically. This is the shift that made LiteAPI's model possible — instead of a bed bank being a business only a large, established travel company could realistically integrate with, an API-first bed bank can be plugged into a comparison engine, a fintech app or a smaller travel start-up in weeks rather than the months or years a legacy paper-contract relationship required.

**Where it stands in 2026.** The market today is a mix of legacy giants (Hotelbeds), regionally-specialised players (Hotelston), and newer API-native aggregators (LiteAPI, which itself aggregates several underlying wholesale sources into one unified connection) — alongside large, diversified groups like Webbeds and RateHawk's parent ETG that combine broad global coverage with genuine regional depth. The direction of travel is toward more API-first distribution and more comparison engines connecting directly to wholesale layers, which is precisely the trend JetMeAway's own hotel search sits inside.

<a id="why-bed-banks-exist"></a>

## Why Bed Banks Exist

The wholesale layer isn't an accident of the market — it solves a real structural problem for both hotels and downstream travel businesses.

**For hotels, the problem is unsold inventory risk.** A hotel room is a perishable product: if it isn't sold tonight, that revenue is gone forever — there's no way to "restock" an empty room and sell it tomorrow instead. Hotels therefore want as many distribution channels as possible feeding demand into their booking system, so that whichever channel a given traveller happens to search on, the hotel has a chance of capturing that booking. Contracting bulk allocation to a bed bank gives a hotel access to hundreds of downstream retail and B2B buyers through a single commercial relationship, rather than negotiating individually with each one.

**For travel businesses downstream, the problem is scale.** Building direct commercial relationships with individual hotels — negotiating rates, integrating booking systems, handling payment reconciliation — across even a modest 500-city footprint would require thousands of separate contracts. A single bed-bank integration instead gives a comparison engine, airline or travel agency access to hundreds of thousands (or in LiteAPI's case, millions) of hotels through one technical connection. This is precisely what made it commercially realistic for a comparison engine the size of JetMeAway to launch genuine hotel search and booking at all — building that inventory from individual hotel contracts one at a time simply isn't viable for a company our size, but a bed-bank API makes it achievable.

**For the traveller, the upside is price competition further down the chain.** Because bed-bank rates are wholesale, any downstream platform that connects to them has room to compete on price against retail-only competitors who are paying (and passing on) a full retail markup. That price competition is the entire reason the two-tier market described below matters to you as a traveller, even though you'll likely never interact with a bed bank's own name directly.

## How a Bed-Bank Transaction Actually Works

It helps to walk through the mechanics step by step, because the process is more transparent than the "hidden wholesale layer" framing sometimes suggests.

**Step 1 — Hotel contracts allocation to the bed bank.** A hotel's revenue manager agrees a rate and a room allocation (sometimes a fixed number of rooms per night, sometimes a dynamic allocation tied to occupancy forecasts) with a bed bank, at a negotiated wholesale price. This is a formal commercial contract, renewed and renegotiated periodically, usually at least annually.

**Step 2 — The bed bank exposes that inventory via API.** Once contracted, the room allocation, live availability and rate become queryable through the bed bank's API — the technical interface a platform like JetMeAway calls in real time when you search a destination and dates.

**Step 3 — The retail-facing platform queries and displays the rate.** When you search [hotels on JetMeAway](/hotels), our system queries LiteAPI, RateHawk and Webbeds (alongside the retail OTA feeds) simultaneously, compares what comes back, and shows you the live rate returned by each source. This happens in real time, not from a cached price list — wholesale rates move with occupancy and demand just like retail ones do.

**Step 4 — You book, and the payment routes through the source you selected.** If you choose a bed-bank rate, the booking and payment flow through that bed bank's own system (LiteAPI's or Webbeds' merchant-of-record flow, for example), which then confirms the reservation with the hotel and issues your booking voucher. The hotel is paid by the bed bank, not directly by you — but the contractual chain from you, through the bed bank, to the hotel is documented at every step, exactly as it would be through a retail OTA.

**Step 5 — Commission settles after the stay.** The bed bank retains its margin (built into the wholesale rate it negotiated with the hotel) and pays a referral commission to the retail-facing platform that sent the booking — in JetMeAway's case, that's how a bed-bank-sourced booking generates revenue for us without adding any markup to what you pay.

The mechanics are ordinary B2B commerce — a supply contract, an API, a booking confirmation, a commission settlement — dressed up in unfamiliar terminology. Nothing about the process is opaque to the traveller at the point of booking; the unfamiliarity is simply because the terminology (“bed bank”) never needed to reach a consumer audience before.

## The Two-Tier Hotel Market — Retail vs Wholesale

Here's the pattern most UK travellers notice in the wild without necessarily understanding why: the same hotel, same room category, same dates — and the price varies by **10–25%** depending on which site you happen to land on. That gap is structural, not promotional, and it comes from where in the supply chain the price you're seeing was set.

**Layer 1 — Wholesale (bed-bank) rate.** This is the price a hotel contracts to a bed bank like LiteAPI, RateHawk or Webbeds, negotiated on volume and typically without the marketing-value premium a hotel expects to pay for placement on a major consumer brand. It's the lowest layer in the chain before any retail markup is applied.

**Layer 2 — Retail markup.** A retail OTA receiving that bed-bank rate (whether directly or through their own supplier relationships) typically adds:

- **Commission** (commonly 15–25%) — funds marketing spend, customer service infrastructure, and the technology stack behind a large consumer travel brand
- **Loyalty programme cost** — points, cashback and status-tier perks baked into the headline price
- **Currency-conversion margin** — many major sites add roughly 1–3% on foreign-exchange conversion for international bookings

**Layer 3 — What you actually pay.** The final consumer price is Layer 1 plus however much of Layer 2 that particular platform has stacked on top. A retail-only OTA shows you Layer 1 + Layer 2 combined, with no way to see the split. A bed-bank-direct integration like JetMeAway's LiteAPI, RateHawk and Webbeds flows shows you a price much closer to Layer 1, because the retail-marketing layer simply isn't being added on our side of the transaction.

Whether you personally save 6%, 16% or 25% on any given booking depends on the specific hotel, the season, and which OTA you're comparing against — the gap isn't a fixed number, and we won't pretend it is. But the supply-chain structure explains reliably *why* the gap exists at all, which is more useful to understand than any single average would be, because it tells you what to actually check before you book: is this price coming from Layer 1, or Layer 1-plus-Layer-2?

### A quick worked example

Say a genuine 4-star hotel in Lisbon has a published bed-bank rate of £120/night for a standard double in June 2026. A retail OTA sourcing from the same or a comparable wholesale contract, adding a typical 20% commission plus a small FX margin, lists that same room at roughly £146–150/night. Both listings are for the identical room, on the identical dates, with comparable cancellation terms — the £26–30 difference is entirely the retail layer. Multiply that across a 5-night stay and the gap is £130–150 — enough to fund an extra activity, a nicer dinner, or simply a cheaper trip overall.

## Bed Banks by Region — Where Each One Is Strongest

Because every bed bank negotiates its hotel contracts independently, none of them has uniform strength everywhere — and knowing the rough regional pattern helps explain why comparing more than one wholesale source consistently pays off.

**Western Europe (France, Spain, Italy, Portugal, Germany).** All three of JetMeAway's bed-bank partners carry deep inventory here — this is the most competitive, most contested region in the wholesale market, which tends to compress the retail-versus-wholesale gap somewhat because retail OTAs also fight hardest for market share in these destinations. Webbeds has particular strength across mainstream Western European city and beach destinations.

**Eastern Europe, Central Asia and the CIS region.** RateHawk's parent, Emerging Travel Group, built its original business in this region (via Ostrovok), and RateHawk's contract depth here is noticeably stronger than the mainstream Western-focused OTAs — a genuinely useful reason to check RateHawk specifically for a Georgia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan or wider CIS trip.

**The Middle East and North Africa.** Webbeds has a strong historical footprint here, reflecting the group's origins and ongoing investment in Gulf and North African hotel contracts — relevant for UK travellers booking Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Egypt or Morocco stays.

**Turkey and the wider Mediterranean.** Hotelston's specific competitive edge, and part of why our own six-hotel Barcelona/Antalya/Sharm el-Sheikh/Tunisia test (below) found it cheapest across the board — smaller, regionally-focused bed banks can genuinely out-negotiate the bigger generalists on their home turf.

**North America and long-haul global.** LiteAPI's aggregation-of-aggregators model gives it the broadest single-API global reach, useful precisely because no single regional specialist covers North America, Asia-Pacific and Europe equally well — LiteAPI's approach of blending several underlying wholesale sources behind one API is a direct response to that regional-fragmentation problem.

The practical lesson: there is no single "best" bed bank across every destination, which is exactly why a comparison engine plugged into more than one wholesale source has a genuine structural edge over one connected to only a single supplier, regardless of how large that one supplier is.

## Consumer Platforms That Run on Bed-Bank Inventory

You've almost certainly used a bed bank's inventory without knowing it, because a huge share of the consumer travel industry runs on wholesale supply somewhere in its chain. A few categories worth knowing:

**Package-holiday operators.** Traditional UK tour operators building flight-plus-hotel packages have relied on bed-bank inventory (historically Hotelbeds above all) for decades — it's the reason a package holiday can sometimes undercut booking the same flight and hotel separately, since the operator is buying accommodation at wholesale rather than retail rates and blending it into a single package price.

**Airlines selling hotel add-ons.** When an airline's own booking flow offers "add a hotel to your flight" at checkout, that hotel inventory is very rarely the airline's own — it's almost always a white-label bed-bank integration running quietly behind the airline's branding.

**Meta-search and comparison engines.** Sites that compare hotel prices across multiple sources — JetMeAway included — typically blend retail OTA feeds with one or more direct bed-bank integrations, precisely so the comparison includes both layers of the market rather than only the retail one.

**Fintech and super-app travel features.** Banking apps, loyalty-point redemption portals and "book with points" travel features bolted onto non-travel platforms are almost always powered by a bed-bank API under the hood — building a hotel booking feature from scratch, hotel by hotel, isn't realistic for a company whose core business is something else entirely.

**Smaller regional travel agencies.** A high-street or regional online travel agency without the scale to negotiate its own direct hotel contracts across hundreds of destinations will typically plug into a bed bank for the bulk of its accommodation inventory, reserving direct hotel relationships for a small number of local, high-value properties.

The pattern across every one of these: **the bed-bank layer is largely invisible by design.** It's meant to power other people's consumer experiences, not build its own. That's precisely why most UK travellers have never heard the term before, despite almost certainly having booked bed-bank-sourced inventory at some point.

## Is It Safe? Cancellation, Loyalty Points & Consumer Protection

Three questions come up constantly once travellers understand the wholesale layer exists, and they deserve straight answers.

**Is a bed-bank booking as safe as a retail OTA booking?** Yes, provided the integration is legitimate. Safety on a hotel booking comes from the payment processor, the disclosed cancellation terms, and a clear contractual chain back to the hotel — not from how recognisable the consumer-facing brand name is. LiteAPI, RateHawk and Webbeds are all established B2B travel-technology companies with direct commercial hotel contracts, and in many cases retail OTAs are themselves quietly reselling the exact same wholesale inventory one layer downstream from where you're booking it. Paying by UK credit card also gives you Section 75 protection on qualifying purchases regardless of which layer of the supply chain the room came from.

**Does a bed-bank booking earn hotel chain loyalty points?** Not always, and this is the one genuine trade-off worth knowing about. Because a bed-bank booking is technically routed through a B2B intermediary rather than the hotel chain's own retail channel, it may not automatically register for that chain's points programme or elite-status credit the way a booking made directly on the hotel's own website would. If chain loyalty points matter more to you than the price gap for a specific stay, it's worth checking the hotel's own site rate as a third comparison point alongside the retail-OTA and bed-bank prices, and weighing the points value against the cash saved.

**Are cancellation terms different?** Sometimes, and this is worth reading carefully rather than assuming either way. The very cheapest bed-bank rates can carry stricter, non-refundable cancellation windows — exactly the same trade-off that exists on the cheapest advance-purchase rates from any retail OTA. Flexible, fully-refundable bed-bank rates are also available at most hotels, usually at a modest premium over the non-refundable price, mirroring the retail market's own structure. Always check the specific cancellation policy on the rate you're selecting, not the general reputation of the platform.

## How to Spot Which Layer You're Buying From

You don't need insider knowledge to work out whether a price you're looking at is closer to the wholesale layer or the retail-marked-up one. A few practical tells:

**Check the checkout page's merchant name.** A wholesale bed-bank booking will often show the bed bank's own name (or a clearly-labelled partner name) as the merchant of record on your card statement or booking confirmation, rather than the comparison site's own brand — that's a sign you're one layer closer to the source. JetMeAway is explicit about this at checkout on every bed-bank booking.

**Compare the same room across at least three sources.** One retail OTA, one comparison engine with bed-bank integrations, and — where practical — the hotel's own website. If all three cluster within a few percent, you're probably not looking at a meaningful retail markup on that particular date. If the gap is 10%+ between the retail-only sites and the bed-bank-connected one, you've likely found the layer split described in this guide.

**Read the cancellation policy, not just the headline price.** A wholesale rate that's dramatically cheaper but strictly non-refundable is a genuinely different product from a flexible retail rate at a higher price — compare like-for-like cancellation terms before concluding one option is definitively "the deal."

**Look for "member pricing," "mobile-only deals" or opaque blind-booking products with caution.** These retail-side promotional mechanisms sometimes approach wholesale-level pricing, but sometimes they're simply a smaller discount dressed up as an exclusive one — the only way to know is to compare against an actual bed-bank-connected price for the same room and dates.

**Trust the comparison, not the brand.** The single most reliable method remains simply running the comparison itself — which is the entire reason a comparison engine exists in the first place. [Search hotels on JetMeAway](/hotels) for your destination and dates, and you'll see both layers laid out side by side rather than having to reverse-engineer which one you're looking at.

## Common Myths, Corrected

**"Bed-bank hotels are lower quality or somehow 'off-brand.'"** Not true. A bed-bank contract covers the exact same physical room, same star rating, and usually the same or a comparable cancellation policy as the retail listing for that hotel — the wholesale layer changes the price you see, not the room you get.

**"If it's cheaper, it must be a scam."** The price gap has a specific, well-documented structural cause — retail marketing and commission overhead stacked on top of a wholesale rate — not fraud. Legitimate bed-bank integrations issue the same kind of booking confirmation and voucher system any retail OTA would.

**"You can just book directly with a bed bank and cut out the middleman entirely."** Bed banks are B2B-only; they don't sell to consumers directly, so there's no "direct" wholesale booking route available to the public. You always access wholesale rates through a retail-facing platform that holds the commercial integration — the value is in choosing a platform that passes the wholesale rate through rather than adding its own full retail markup on top of it.

**"The cheapest platform is always the bed-bank-direct one."** Not always — retail OTAs run their own promotions, flash sales and loyalty discounts that occasionally undercut even the wholesale rate for a specific hotel and date. That's exactly why JetMeAway compares both layers rather than only showing you our own bed-bank integrations; the honest answer is "check both," not "wholesale always wins."

**"Every hotel is available through every bed bank."** No single bed bank carries every property. Coverage varies by region and by hotel size — small independent boutiques and guesthouses are the category most likely to sit outside wholesale contracts entirely, in which case the hotel's own website may be your only or best option.
