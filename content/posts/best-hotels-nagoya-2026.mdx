Price your actual journeys as individual tickets first, then compare.   - q: "What is Nagoya meshi?"
    a: "It is the collective name for Nagoya's own food culture, and it is genuinely distinct from the rest of Japan. The staples are miso katsu, a breaded pork cutlet under a dark red-miso sauce; hitsumabushi, grilled eel over rice served with instructions for eating it three different ways; tebasaki, peppery fried chicken wings; and kishimen, flat wide noodles served hot or cold. Add miso nikomi udon and, for breakfast, ogura toast with sweet red-bean paste. Osu and Sakae are the easiest places to work through the list."

Many phones can now hold a digital IC card, which avoids the queues. IC cards do not cover shinkansen reserved travel, which you buy separately."

Winter is cold but dry and quiet, and it is the cheapest time to book. Avoid Golden Week in late April and early May and Obon in mid-August, when domestic travel fills everything."

Service is included, standards are high without it, and no one expects anything extra in restaurants, taxis or hotels. The one exception is a formal ryokan or a private guide, where a gratuity is occasionally presented in an envelope, and even that is optional."

## What to See in Nagoya

Start with **Nagoya Castle**. Its most famous feature is the pair of **golden shachihoko** on the roof — mythical creatures with a tiger's head and a carp's body, cast in gold leaf and so closely identified with the city that they appear on everything from manhole covers to biscuit tins. Beside the keep stands the **Hommaru Palace**, a painstaking reconstruction of the original shogunal residence completed using traditional techniques and historic drawings, with cypress interiors and reproduction screen paintings that are genuinely worth the entrance fee. Note that access arrangements at the castle have changed in recent years while structural work on the main keep is considered, so check what is open before you go.

**Atsuta Shrine** is the other essential, and a much quieter experience. It is one of the most sacred sites in Shinto — traditionally said to enshrine the Kusanagi sword, one of the Three Sacred Treasures of the imperial regalia — and it sits in a large wooded precinct of ancient camphor trees in the south of the city, a world away from the traffic outside. Entry is free, it takes an hour, and the shops by the gate sell some of the best kishimen noodles in Nagoya.

Then come the museums, and this is where Nagoya quietly beats cities twice its size. The **Toyota Commemorative Museum of Industry and Technology** occupies the original red-brick Toyoda works and traces the company's path from automatic looms to cars, with working machinery demonstrated by staff rather than displayed behind glass — even people with no interest in vehicles tend to come out impressed. The separate **Toyota Automobile Museum**, out east of the city and reached on the Linimo maglev line, is a full-scale history of the motor car worldwide rather than a corporate showroom. Nagoya is the heart of Japan's car industry and makes no secret of it; for many visitors this pair is the highlight of the trip.

The **SCMaglev and Railway Park** is JR Central's own museum on the waterfront, where you can walk around real shinkansen generations, a steam locomotive and the record-breaking maglev test vehicles. The **Nagoya City Science Museum** in Shirakawa Park houses a planetarium under one of the largest domes in the world — the shows are in Japanese, but the sky is universal and the building itself, a vast silver sphere wedged into a glass frame, is a sight on its own.

For wandering, **Osu** is the answer: a grid of covered arcades around the **Osu Kannon** temple, mixing second-hand clothing, electronics stalls, anime shops, old-fashioned tea sellers and an enormous amount of street food. **Sakae** is the modern counterpart, with the department stores, the underground shopping streets, the **Oasis 21** glass-roofed structure — a shallow pool of water suspended above the bus terminal that you can walk beneath — and the television tower in **Hisaya-odori Park**. Out at the port, **Legoland Japan** sits alongside an aquarium and the railway park, which makes the waterfront a straightforward full day with children.

And then there is the food, which is the thing residents will insist you get right. Nagoya has its own culinary tradition, collectively called **Nagoya meshi**: **miso katsu**, a pork cutlet under thick dark red-miso sauce; **hitsumabushi**, grilled eel over rice served with instructions to eat it three different ways; **tebasaki**, peppery fried chicken wings; and **kishimen**, flat wide noodles that are the local answer to udon. Add miso nikomi udon simmered in an iron pot and, for breakfast, ogura toast spread with sweet red-bean paste. None of it is expensive, and Osu and Sakae are the easiest places to work through the list.

## Why Nagoya Is Worth Basing In — The Honest Case

Nagoya's real value is its position. It sits on the **Tokaido Shinkansen**, the busiest high-speed line in the world, roughly halfway between the two ends of Japan's main tourist axis: about **100 minutes to Tokyo**, about **35 minutes to Kyoto** and about **50 minutes to Shin-Osaka**, with trains every few minutes. Hotels here are **consistently cheaper than in Tokyo, Kyoto or Osaka**, and availability holds up in the weeks when Kyoto sells out entirely. Base here and day-trip out, and you can see Kyoto on a room rate that Kyoto itself will not give you.

It is also the gateway to a set of places the big three cannot reach easily. The JR Hida limited express runs north to **Takayama** in about two and a half hours, through river gorges and mountain villages; from there, buses reach **Shirakawa-go**, the UNESCO-listed valley of steep thatched farmhouses. The Chuo line runs east to the **Kiso Valley** and the preserved post towns of the old **Nakasendo** trail, where you can walk the two-hour stretch between Magome and Tsumago in an afternoon. And the Kintetsu line runs south-west to **Ise**, home of the **Ise Grand Shrine**, the most important shrine in Shinto and one that most foreign visitors never see.

The honest counterweight: Nagoya is often skipped, and not entirely without reason. It has nothing like Kyoto's density of temples or Tokyo's endless variety, much of the centre was rebuilt after wartime bombing, and it reads as a prosperous working city rather than a sightseeing showpiece. Two days is enough for the city itself. But that under-visiting is exactly what keeps the rooms cheap, the restaurants un-queued and the castle grounds calm on a Saturday — and it is why, for a second or third trip to Japan, this is one of the smartest bases in the country.

Before you book, read the UK government's current [foreign travel advice for Japan](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/japan), which covers entry requirements, safety, health and money, and check it again in the week before you fly — Japan has been introducing a pre-travel electronic authorisation system for visa-free visitors, so the paperwork position is worth re-verifying rather than assuming. For attractions, seasonal events, opening hours and rail planning, [Japan's official tourism site for UK visitors](https://www.japan.travel/en/uk/) is the reliable starting point. Two practical notes while you are packing: tipping is not customary anywhere in Japan and will only cause confusion, and the tap water is safe to drink everywhere, so bring a refillable bottle.

## Where to Stay in Nagoya: Choosing Your Base

**Nagoya Station — Meieki** is the default and rarely the wrong call. The shinkansen platforms, three subway lines, the Meitetsu airport train and the JR lines out to Takayama and the Kiso Valley all converge here, and the city's tallest towers stand directly above the concourse with hotels inside several of them. Everything below the station is connected by an enormous underground shopping network, which matters more than it sounds in a rainy week. The trade-off is that Meieki is a business district: it is efficient rather than charming, and the evening life is thinner than in Sakae.

**Sakae** is the opposite trade. Two subway stops east, this is where the department stores, the underground arcades, the bars of Nishiki and the restaurants are, along with Oasis 21 and Hisaya-odori Park. It is the better base if you want to walk out to dinner and walk home again, and the budget hotels here cost only a few pounds more than the ones by the station. If you are staying three nights or more, this is usually the more enjoyable choice.

**Fushimi** sits between the two, one stop from each, and is the compromise that often wins on price. You get subway access in both directions, the Science Museum and Shirakawa Park on the doorstep, a business-district calm in the evenings, and rates that tend to undercut both neighbours. **Marunouchi**, just north of Fushimi, is where the cheapest rooms in this guide cluster.

**Kanayama**, one hub south, is worth knowing about for one reason: the direct airport train. If you have an early flight from Centrair or a late arrival, a room here saves a change and a walk. **Chubu Centrair (NGO)** itself sits on a man-made island in Ise Bay about **30 minutes from the city** by Meitetsu limited express, and there are airport hotels on the island if your schedule really demands it — but for almost everyone, the city is close enough that there is no reason to sleep out there.

**Are hotels in Nagoya cheaper than Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka?**
Yes, and that is the honest reason to consider it. Nagoya is Japan's fourth-largest city and a major business centre, but it draws a fraction of the international tourism that Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka do — so room rates are lower and availability is better, particularly in cherry-blossom and autumn-colour season when Kyoto in particular becomes very expensive. You will typically pay noticeably less here for the same standard of business hotel, and the shinkansen puts Kyoto about 35 minutes away.

**Where should I stay in Nagoya?**
Three areas cover almost everyone. Nagoya Station, known locally as Meieki, is the obvious first choice: the shinkansen, the subway lines, the Meitetsu airport train and the city's tallest towers are all there, so it suits day-trippers and short stays. Sakae is the nightlife, shopping and restaurant district about two subway stops east, better if you want to walk out to dinner and bars. Fushimi sits between the two and is usually a little cheaper than either while staying walkable to both. Kanayama, one hub south, is handy for the airport line and often cheaper again.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Nagoya?**
The cheapest rooms cluster around Marunouchi and the Sakura-dori side of Nagoya Station, plus Kanayama to the south — this is where the LiVEMAX, KOKO HOTEL and Toyoko Inn style properties sit at £36 to £45 a night. All of them are on the subway, so cheap does not mean remote here: Marunouchi is one stop from both Nagoya Station and Fushimi, and Kanayama is on the direct line to the airport. Sakae has a few properties in the same price band too.

**Is Nagoya worth visiting?**
Honest answer: it depends on what you want. Nagoya is not a sightseeing showpiece in the way Kyoto is, and plenty of first-time visitors to Japan skip it entirely. It is a large, prosperous, liveable working city — the heart of Japan's car industry — with a strong museum line-up, a genuinely distinctive food culture and a castle. If you have two weeks and it is your first trip, Kyoto and Tokyo will win. If you have been to Japan before, if you like industrial and transport museums, or if you want a cheaper and less crowded base with brilliant rail access to the mountains and to the big three cities, Nagoya is an excellent choice and a much better one than its reputation suggests.

**How many days do you need in Nagoya?**
Two full days covers the city itself comfortably: one for Nagoya Castle, Atsuta Shrine and Osu, and one for the museums — the Toyota Commemorative Museum of Industry and Technology, the SCMaglev and Railway Park and the Nagoya City Science Museum planetarium. If you are using Nagoya as a base, add a day each for Takayama, the Kiso Valley or Ise, and treat Kyoto as a very easy day trip at about 35 minutes each way by shinkansen.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Japan?**
UK passport holders can normally enter Japan visa-free for short tourist stays of up to 90 days. That said, Japan has been introducing a pre-travel electronic authorisation system for visa-free visitors, so the paperwork position can change — always check the current entry requirements on the UK government's Japan travel advice page and Japan's own official immigration guidance before you book flights, and again in the week before you fly.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Nagoya?**
No. There is no direct UK service to Nagoya. The two practical routes are to fly direct to Tokyo (Haneda or Narita) or Osaka and take the shinkansen on — about 100 minutes from Tokyo, about 50 minutes from Shin-Osaka — or to connect through a Gulf or Asian hub into Chubu Centrair International Airport (NGO), which sits on a man-made island in Ise Bay about 30 minutes from the city by train. For most UK travellers the Tokyo-plus-shinkansen option is cheaper and the train ride is part of the trip.

**How do I get from Chubu Centrair Airport into Nagoya?**
Take the Meitetsu train from the airport station directly into Meitetsu Nagoya, which is right beside Nagoya Station. The fastest limited express service takes around 30 minutes; ordinary services take a little longer and cost less. The airport is built on a man-made island in Ise Bay, so the train ride out over the water is genuinely pleasant. Kanayama, one hub south of Nagoya Station, is also a direct stop on the same line, which is why hotels there suit early flights.

**How long does the shinkansen take from Nagoya to Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka?**
Nagoya sits on the Tokaido Shinkansen, the busiest bullet-train line in the world, roughly halfway between Tokyo and Osaka. The fastest Nozomi services reach Tokyo in about 100 minutes, Kyoto in about 35 minutes and Shin-Osaka in about 50 minutes. Departures run every few minutes for most of the day, so there is no real need to plan around a timetable. This is the single strongest argument for basing yourself here.

**Is the Japan Rail Pass worth it?**
It depends entirely on your route, and it is no longer the automatic yes it once was. The nationwide pass rose sharply in price in 2023 and now needs a genuinely long itinerary to pay for itself — a single Nagoya to Tokyo return, for example, costs far less than a week of pass. Price your actual journeys as individual tickets first, then compare. If you are staying in central Japan, JR Central's regional passes covering Takayama, the Hokuriku coast or the Ise-Kumano area are often much better value than the full national pass, and for city travel you want an IC card, not a rail pass.

**Why stay in Nagoya rather than Kyoto or Osaka?**
Money and position. Room rates in Nagoya are consistently lower than in Kyoto, Osaka or Tokyo for the same standard of hotel, and availability holds up in peak weeks when Kyoto sells out. From Nagoya you are about 35 minutes from Kyoto, 50 from Shin-Osaka and 100 from Tokyo by shinkansen, plus you are the gateway to Takayama, Shirakawa-go, the Kiso Valley and Ise, which none of the big three can claim. The trade-off is honest: you sleep somewhere less atmospheric and spend more time on trains.

**Can I day-trip to Takayama and Shirakawa-go from Nagoya?**
Yes, and this is one of Nagoya's very best arguments. The JR Hida limited express runs north from Nagoya up the Hida river gorge to Takayama in roughly two and a half hours, through some of the prettiest scenery on the JR network. Shirakawa-go, the UNESCO-listed village of steep thatched gassho-zukuri farmhouses, is reached by bus — usually from Takayama, though direct coaches from Nagoya also run. Both are doable in a day if you start early, but an overnight in Takayama is far more pleasant and lets you see the old town before the coaches arrive.

**What is there to see in Nagoya?**
Nagoya Castle with its golden shachihoko roof ornaments and the meticulously reconstructed Hommaru Palace; Atsuta Shrine, one of the most sacred sites in Shinto; the Toyota Commemorative Museum of Industry and Technology and the Toyota Automobile Museum; the SCMaglev and Railway Park, JR Central's train museum on the waterfront; the Nagoya City Science Museum, whose planetarium dome is one of the largest in the world; the Osu shopping arcades and Osu Kannon temple; Oasis 21 and the Sakae district; and Legoland Japan out at the port. Two days covers it.

**What is Nagoya meshi?**
It is the collective name for Nagoya's own food culture, and it is genuinely distinct from the rest of Japan. The staples are miso katsu, a breaded pork cutlet under a dark red-miso sauce; hitsumabushi, grilled eel over rice served with instructions for eating it three different ways; tebasaki, peppery fried chicken wings; and kishimen, flat wide noodles served hot or cold. Add miso nikomi udon and, for breakfast, ogura toast with sweet red-bean paste. Osu and Sakae are the easiest places to work through the list.

**Which IC card do I need for trains and buses in Nagoya?**
Nagoya's own card is manaca, sold at subway and Meitetsu stations, and it works on the subway, city buses and local trains. You do not need a specific one, though — Japan's IC cards are mutually compatible on almost all city networks, so a Suica from Tokyo or an ICOCA from Osaka will tap through the gates in Nagoya perfectly well, and vice versa. Many phones can now hold a digital IC card, which avoids the queues. IC cards do not cover shinkansen reserved travel, which you buy separately.

**When is the best time to visit Nagoya?**
Spring and autumn. Late March into early April brings cherry blossom, with Nagoya Castle and the city parks putting on a good show, and November brings the autumn colours. Summers in Nagoya are notably hot and humid even by Japanese standards — the city sits in a basin and holds the heat — so July and August are hard work outdoors. Winter is cold but dry and quiet, and it is the cheapest time to book. Avoid Golden Week in late April and early May and Obon in mid-August, when domestic travel fills everything.

**Do you tip in Japan?**
No. Tipping is not customary anywhere in Japan and attempting it usually causes polite confusion — staff may chase you down the street to return money you left on the table. Service is included, standards are high without it, and no one expects anything extra in restaurants, taxis or hotels. The one exception is a formal ryokan or a private guide, where a gratuity is occasionally presented in an envelope, and even that is optional.

**Can you drink the tap water in Nagoya?**
Yes. Tap water across Japan, Nagoya included, is safe to drink and held to a high standard — restaurants serve it free as a matter of course and locals drink it without a second thought. Bring a refillable bottle: you will save money and a good deal of plastic, and public water fountains are common in parks and stations.

**What currency is used in Japan, and do I need cash?**
The currency is the Japanese Yen (JPY). All pound figures in this guide are rough conversions of live rates rather than fixed quotes, so they move with the exchange rate and your dates. Cards are now widely accepted in cities — hotels, chains, department stores and most restaurants — but Japan remains more cash-friendly than most of Europe, and small shops, some temples and shrines, older restaurants, coin lockers and some bus fares still want cash. Carry a reasonable amount of yen; convenience-store ATMs, especially at 7-Eleven, reliably accept foreign cards.

**Is Nagoya good for families?**
Better than most people expect. Legoland Japan sits on the waterfront in the port area with the Sea Life aquarium and the Port of Nagoya Public Aquarium nearby, the SCMaglev and Railway Park lets children climb into real bullet trains, the Nagoya City Science Museum planetarium is one of the largest in the world, and Higashiyama Zoo and Botanical Gardens is a full day on its own. Add the fact that rooms cost less than in Tokyo or Osaka and family-sized twin or triple rooms are easier to find, and it adds up well.

**Will hotel staff in Nagoya speak English?**
At the international and four-star hotels, yes. At the budget chains it varies — expect some English signage, translation apps at reception and, increasingly, self check-in kiosks with an English menu. Nagoya sees fewer foreign visitors than Tokyo or Kyoto, so English is a little thinner on the ground, but station signage, ticket machines and subway announcements are bilingual and a translation app on your phone covers the rest without difficulty.
