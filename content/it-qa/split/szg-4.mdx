## Salzburg Hotels FAQs

**Why do the Salzburg hotel prices in this guide look so high?**
Because they were sampled for late August, which is close to the most expensive stretch of the Salzburg year. Late summer is peak tourist season and it overlaps the Salzburg Festival, the huge classical music and opera festival that runs from late July through August and fills the city's hotels for five straight weeks. The 49 hotels here run from about £99 at the airport Leonardo to £1,421 a night at the Hotel Sacher, the middle of this sample sits around £160, and the median across the wider Salzburg summer search we pulled for the same dates was closer to £187. Search the same hotels for a week in March, May, late October or November and you will very likely see materially lower numbers. Nothing in this guide should be read as saying Salzburg is a cheap city to sleep in.

**Which months are cheapest for Salzburg hotels?**
Roughly November before the Christmas markets open, then January, February and March once the New Year peak has passed, and again in late April and early May. Those are the windows when the festival crowds are gone, the day-trippers are elsewhere and hotels discount to fill rooms. Avoid late July and August for the Salzburg Festival, the four weeks of Advent when the Christkindlmarkt runs, the days around New Year, and Easter. Late spring and early autumn are the best all-round compromise: mild weather, everything open and rates well below the summer peak.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Austria?**
No. Austria is in the Schengen Area and UK passport holders can visit for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa for tourism. Two things are changing at the border, though, and you should check the current position before you travel rather than trust a guide written months earlier: the EU's Entry/Exit System (EES), which registers non-EU visitors with fingerprints and a photo instead of a passport stamp, and ETIAS, a paid online travel authorisation that will eventually be required in advance. Both have had their start dates moved more than once. Check the official UK government travel advice for Austria for the live rules before you book flights.

**What currency is used in Salzburg?**
The euro (EUR). Every price in this guide is a rough sterling conversion because that is what UK readers compare against, and the exact figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards and contactless are accepted almost everywhere a visitor goes, though Austria is more cash-friendly than the UK — some smaller guesthouses, bakeries, market stalls and mountain huts still prefer cash, so carry a little. Prices on the shelf include VAT, so the number you see is broadly the number you pay, unlike North America.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Salzburg?**
Sometimes, and mostly in winter. Salzburg Airport (SZG) has a strong seasonal ski-season schedule with direct flights from several UK airports between roughly December and March — this is a major gateway for the Austrian Alps — plus a thinner summer schedule. Outside those windows the direct options can dry up, and the usual answer is to fly to Munich, which is only about 1 hour 30 minutes from Salzburg by train, car or direct airport bus, or to Vienna and take the fast railjet train, about 2 hours 20 minutes. Check live routes for your dates rather than assuming a direct flight exists.

**How much does a budget hotel cost per night in Salzburg?**
Between about £124 and £189 a night for the 35 properties in the most affordable tier of this guide, on late-August dates, with a median around £157. That is not cheap by any normal definition, and it is important to be straight about it: these are the least expensive rooms in an expensive, festival-week sample, not bargains. Not a single hotel in the budget tier comes in below £120 on these dates. On spring, autumn or winter dates the same rooms are frequently a good deal less, so put your own dates into any hotel link before you judge.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Salzburg?**
The Altstadt, the old town on the left bank of the Salzach, or the Neustadt directly across the river. The Altstadt is the UNESCO-listed core — the cathedral, the fortress above, Getreidegasse, Mozart's birthplace — and staying inside it means you walk out of your hotel into the postcard. It is also the priciest and the most limited on hotel stock, and cars are largely kept out. The Neustadt on the right bank holds Mirabell Palace and Gardens, the Mozart Residence and a lot of the mid-range hotels, and it is a five to ten minute walk across a footbridge to the Altstadt. For a first visit, base yourself on either side of the river within the ring and you can leave the map in your pocket.

**Is it worth staying in the Altstadt (old town)?**
If atmosphere is what you are buying, yes. The Altstadt is one of the best-preserved baroque city centres north of the Alps, and being inside it early in the morning before the coach parties arrive, or late in the evening after they leave, is the version of Salzburg people fall in love with. The trade-offs are real: hotel stock is small and heavily boutique so it prices at the very top and sells out first, most of the district is pedestrianised so you cannot drive to the door, and in festival season the whole quarter is at capacity. If your budget is tight, sleep across the river in the Neustadt and walk in.

**What is the Neustadt (right bank) like as a base?**
Practical, central and generally better value than the Altstadt. The Neustadt is the right-bank half of the historic centre, built around Mirabell Palace and its gardens — the ones every Sound of Music tour photographs — with the Mozart Residence, the Kapuzinerberg hill and a good spread of restaurants. It holds a lot of the city's mid-range and four-star hotels, several of them in this guide, and it is only a short walk across the Salzach on the Makartsteg footbridge to the old town. For most visitors who want to be central without paying Altstadt prices, this is the sweet spot.

**Where are the cheaper hotels in Salzburg?**
Away from the two historic banks. The most affordable rooms cluster around the main railway station (Hauptbahnhof) in the north, out along the arterial roads to the north and south of the centre, and in the airport belt to the west. The station area is the most useful of these for a visitor without a car, because it is a 15 to 20 minute walk or a couple of stops on the frequent buses into the old town, and it is where the trains from Munich and Vienna arrive. The airport and outer-road hotels are cheaper again but assume you will bus or drive in. There is no genuinely cheap corner inside the historic centre itself.

**Is the Salzburg Festival why the hotels are so expensive?**
In late July and August, largely yes. The Salzburger Festspiele is one of the most important classical music and opera festivals in the world, it runs for around five weeks from late July into the end of August, and it brings a wealthy international audience that books the best hotels months ahead and holds rates at their annual peak. Our sample falls squarely in that window, which is a big part of why the numbers look the way they do. If you are not coming for the festival, the single most effective thing you can do to cut your hotel bill is to avoid those five weeks — a week in June or September is a different city at a different price.

**When are the Christmas markets, and do they push prices up?**
The Salzburg Christkindlmarkt runs through Advent, roughly from mid or late November until around Christmas, with the main market on Domplatz and Residenzplatz in front of the cathedral and several smaller ones across the city. It is genuinely lovely and it is the second big demand spike of the year after the summer festival, so weekend hotel rates in December climb and the best-placed hotels sell out. The days around New Year are dear too. If you want the markets without the peak, come on a weekday in the first half of December rather than a weekend close to Christmas.

**Is the Sound of Music tour worth doing?**
It depends entirely on who you are. The 1965 film was shot in and around Salzburg, and the bus tours that visit Mirabell Gardens, Leopoldskron and Hellbrunn palaces, the gazebo and Nonnberg Abbey are enormously popular with international visitors — for a lot of people it is the reason they came. It is worth being honest about the local side of it, though: the film is barely known among Salzburgers, many have never seen it, and the whole phenomenon is a visitor thing rather than a local one. If you love the film, do the tour and enjoy it. If you do not, you lose nothing by skipping it and seeing the same places on foot.

**Do I need a car in Salzburg?**
No, and it is a mild nuisance to have one. The historic centre is small, flat along the river and made for walking, the bus network covers everywhere else a visitor goes, and much of the old town is pedestrianised or restricted, with parking pushed into paid garages on the edge. Keep a car only if you are touring the lakes and mountains around the city — the Salzkammergut, Hallstatt, Berchtesgaden — and even then consider picking it up on the day you leave town rather than parking it unused for your city days. Austria drives on the right and motorways need a toll vignette.

**How do I get from Salzburg Airport into the city?**
Salzburg Airport (SZG) is only about 4 kilometres west of the centre, so it is a quick trip. City buses — the 2 and the 10 — run from directly outside the terminal to the main station and the edge of the old town in around 20 minutes for the price of a normal ticket, which is much cheaper than a taxi. Taxis take 10 to 15 minutes and are the easy option late at night or with luggage and children. If you have flown into Munich instead, the direct airport-to-Salzburg buses and the train via Munich Hauptbahnhof both take around 1 hour 30 to 2 hours.

**Is Salzburg walkable?**
Very. It is one of the most walkable small cities in Europe: the UNESCO old town, the fortress hill, Mirabell Gardens and most of the sights sit within a compact core either side of the Salzach, and you can cross the whole historic centre on foot in 20 to 25 minutes. The one climb is up to the Hohensalzburg Fortress, and even that has a funicular if you would rather not walk it. Good shoes matter more than any transport pass — most visitors barely use the buses once they are based centrally.

**How many days do I need in Salzburg?**
Two full days covers the city itself comfortably. Day one for the Altstadt — the cathedral, Residenzplatz, Getreidegasse and Mozart's birthplace, then the funicular up to the Hohensalzburg Fortress for the view. Day two for the right bank — Mirabell Palace and Gardens, the Mozart Residence, a walk up the Kapuzinerberg — plus Hellbrunn Palace and its trick fountains on the southern edge. A third and fourth day are easily filled with day trips: the lakes of the Salzkammergut, Hallstatt, the Untersberg cable car or the salt mines. Many people visit Salzburg as a single day trip from Munich, which sees the highlights but misses the early-morning and evening old town.

**Can I do a day trip to Hallstatt or the lakes from Salzburg?**
Yes, and it is one of the best reasons to base yourself here. The Salzkammergut lake district begins just east of the city: the Wolfgangsee, Mondsee (whose church features in the Sound of Music wedding scene) and Fuschlsee are all within about 30 to 50 minutes by car or bus. Hallstatt, the much-photographed lakeside village, is farther — around 1 hour 15 to 1 hour 30 each way by car, or a longer bus-and-boat or train-and-ferry combination — so it makes a full day rather than a half. Go early, because Hallstatt in particular is small and gets extremely crowded by mid-morning in summer.

**Can I visit the salt mines or the Untersberg?**
Both, and both are excellent half-days. Salzburg means 'salt castle' and salt is why the city is rich — the working show mines at Hallein (Salzwelten), about 20 minutes south, take you underground on miners' slides and across an underground lake, and are a reliable hit with children. The Untersberg, the great massif that looms over the city to the south, has a cable car from St. Leonhard that lifts you to around 1,800 metres for an enormous view over the city and into Bavaria; take warm layers because the summit is much colder than the valley. Berchtesgaden and the Eagle's Nest, just over the German border, are a longer but popular day out.

**Is Munich a good way to reach Salzburg?**
It is often the best way. Munich Airport has far more UK connections than Salzburg, especially outside the winter ski season, and Salzburg is only about 1 hour 30 minutes away — there are direct trains from Munich Hauptbahnhof (around 1 hour 30 to 1 hour 50), direct express buses, and an easy drive on the A8 autobahn. Plenty of UK visitors fly into Munich, spend a night or two there, then continue to Salzburg by train, and the two cities pair naturally on one trip. If you go by car, remember Austria requires a motorway vignette and Germany does not.

**Do I need to speak German in Salzburg?**
No. German is the official language and the local dialect is Austrian rather than the textbook German you may have learned, but Salzburg is a major international tourist city and English is widely spoken in hotels, restaurants, shops and at the sights. A few words go a long way socially — Grüß Gott is the standard Austrian hello, danke is thank you — and using them is appreciated, but you will never be stuck. Menus and signs in the tourist core are very often bilingual.

**Is Salzburg safe for tourists?**
Yes. Salzburg is a small, prosperous and very safe city by any international standard, and the overwhelming majority of visitors have an entirely uneventful trip. The realistic risks are the ordinary ones of any busy tourist destination: pickpocketing in the most crowded parts of the old town and at the station in high season, and slips on wet cobbles or winter ice. Use normal city sense with bags and phones in crowds, take care on the steep paths up to the fortress and the Kapuzinerberg in the wet, and check the current UK Foreign Office travel advice for Austria before you go.

**How much should I tip in Salzburg?**
Modestly, and less than in North America. In restaurants and cafés the convention is to round up the bill or add roughly 5 to 10 per cent for good service, told to the server as you pay rather than left on the table — you state the total you want to pay when you hand over the money or card. Round up a taxi fare, leave a euro or two for a hotel porter, and a couple of euros a day for housekeeping if you wish. Service is not run on tips the way it is in the United States, so there is no need to over-think it.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Salzburg?**
Yes — it is excellent, and you should drink it. Salzburg's tap water comes largely from protected alpine and groundwater sources and is high quality straight from the tap, so there is no need to buy bottled water for your room. Public drinking fountains around the city run clean mountain water too. Carry a refillable bottle and you will save money and plastic; asking for tap water — Leitungswasser — in a restaurant is fine, though a small charge is not unheard of.

**Do hotels in Salzburg add taxes and fees on top of the rate?**
A little. Austrian VAT is already included in the quoted room rate, so unlike North America the headline price is close to the real price. On top of it, the city levies a small local tourist or accommodation tax per person per night — a couple of euros a night — which some hotels include in the rate and others add at checkout. A few properties charge separately for parking or breakfast. It is minor compared with the taxes stacked onto a North American hotel bill, but check the total on the live page rather than the headline rate before you compare two hotels.

**Is Salzburg good for families?**
It is a manageable and rewarding family city. The centre is compact and walkable, the fortress with its funicular and museums is a reliable hit, the Hellbrunn trick fountains are built to soak children on a hot day, the Haus der Natur museum is genuinely good in any weather, and the salt mines and Untersberg cable car sit a short trip away. The one honest caveat is hotel stock: family rooms in the historic centre are limited and pricey in summer, so families often do best in the mid-range and budget hotels near the station or the outer roads, where rooms are larger and cheaper and the bus in is short.

**What should I eat in Salzburg?**
A few local things are worth seeking out. Salzburger Nockerl is the city's signature dessert — a huge, sweet baked soufflé in three peaks meant to echo the hills around the city, big enough to share. Wiener Schnitzel, Tafelspitz (boiled beef) and Kasnocken (Austrian cheese dumplings) anchor the savoury side, and the Augustiner Bräustübl, a vast monastery-run beer hall, is an experience in itself. On the sweet side the Mozartkugel — a pistachio-marzipan and nougat chocolate ball — is everywhere; the original hand-made version comes from Café Fürst in the old town, while the foil-wrapped ones in every shop are the mass-market spin-off. Café Sacher does the famous Sachertorte.

**Are the star ratings in this guide official?**
Treat them as supplier-declared rather than a formal government grading. Austria does operate a hotel classification scheme, but the star figures shown here come from our accommodation data and are a rough guide, not an audited grade — which matters most at the bottom of the list. Several strong entries in the affordable tier carry no star rating at all because they are apartment, pension or boutique stays that have not sought a classification, and a couple of the best-reviewed budget hotels are unrated for exactly that reason. Review counts are a far better guide to reliability than stars, which is why every entry here shows both.
