## Graz Hotels FAQs

**How much do hotels cost in Graz?**
Less than you might fear for a UNESCO-listed European city. Across the 49 hotels in this guide the rates run from about £40 a night at the a&o by the main station to £254 at the Schlossberghotel, the single five-star in the city and the one genuine splurge in the post. The great bulk of Graz sits in between, in a broad and honest mid-range: most of the four-star hotels here fall between about £55 and £110, and the most affordable tier runs from roughly £40 to £115. Graz is Austria's second city and a student town, not a peak-season resort, so it prices calmly and fairly all year — noticeably below Vienna and Salzburg. These were sampled for a typical shoulder-season stay; put your own dates into any hotel link for the live figure.

**What is the most expensive hotel in this guide?**
The Schlossberghotel, at about £254 a night — and it is the only hotel in the post that goes anywhere near that number. It is the one property in Graz that clears the five-star bar: a boutique art hotel tucked right under the Schlossberg in the old town, with a small pool, a rooftop terrace and a serious private art collection on the walls. After it, the price ladder drops away sharply — the next hotels down are four-stars in the £90 to £110 range. There is no cluster of £400 grande dames here the way there is in Salzburg or Vienna; Graz has one standout and then a deep, sensible middle.

**What is the cheapest hotel in this guide?**
The a&o Graz Hauptbahnhof at about £40 a night, a large budget hostel-hotel beside the main railway station with over nine thousand reviews behind it — rooms are basic and functional, but the price and the location for anyone arriving by train are hard to argue with. Close behind it come the harry's home Hart bei Graz at about £52 and the ibis Styles Graz Messe at about £53. Below about £60 you are mostly looking at chain budget rooms and apartments a tram ride out from the old town, which for calm, cheap Graz is a perfectly reasonable trade.

**Why does Graz have only one five-star hotel?**
Because that is simply the shape of the city, and this guide is not going to pad the top of the list to hide it. Graz is Austria's second city, a relaxed university town rather than an international luxury destination, so it has never developed the row of grand five-star hotels you find in Vienna or Salzburg. What it has instead is one genuine standout — the Schlossberghotel — sitting above an unusually strong and deep field of four-star and characterful three-star hotels. For most visitors that is good news: you get real quality in the £55 to £110 band without paying resort prices, and the money you save on the room goes further in Graz than almost anywhere else in Austria.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Austria?**
No. Austria is in the Schengen Area and UK passport holders can visit for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa for tourism. Two things are changing at the EU border, though, and you should check the current position before you travel rather than trust a guide written months earlier: the Entry/Exit System (EES), which registers non-EU visitors with fingerprints and a photo instead of a passport stamp, and ETIAS, a paid online travel authorisation that will eventually be required in advance. Both have had their start dates moved more than once. Check the official UK government travel advice for Austria for the live rules before you book flights.

**What currency is used in Graz?**
The euro (EUR). Every price in this guide is a rough sterling conversion because that is what UK readers compare against, and the exact figure moves with the exchange rate. Cards and contactless are accepted almost everywhere a visitor goes, though Austria is more cash-friendly than the UK — some smaller guesthouses, bakeries, market stalls and Buschenschank wine taverns still prefer cash, so carry a little. Prices on the shelf include VAT, so the number you see is broadly the number you pay, unlike North America.

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Graz?**
Rarely, and you should not count on one. Graz Airport (GRZ) is a small regional airport with only a thin scattering of direct UK links, mostly seasonal, so the reliable route for most UK travellers is to fly to Vienna and take the train. Vienna to Graz is about 2 hours 30 minutes on a direct railjet service through the Semmering pass, one of the prettiest rail journeys in the country. Some visitors also fly into Ljubljana in Slovenia, about two hours south by road, or connect through Frankfurt, Munich or Vienna on a through-ticket. Check live routes for your dates rather than assuming a direct flight exists.

**How do I get from Vienna to Graz?**
By train, and it is easy. Direct railjet services run from Vienna Hauptbahnhof to Graz Hauptbahnhof roughly hourly and take about 2 hours 30 minutes, climbing over the historic Semmering railway — a UNESCO-listed mountain line that is worth the window seat on its own. Book ahead on the ÖBB (Austrian Railways) site for the cheapest Sparschiene fares. Driving takes a similar time on the A2 autobahn, but remember Austrian motorways require a toll vignette, and you will not need a car once you are in walkable Graz, so the train is usually the better call.

**Which area should I stay in for a first visit to Graz?**
The Altstadt, the UNESCO-listed old town, or its immediate edges. This is the compact historic core around Hauptplatz and Herrengasse, under the wooded Schlossberg hill with its famous clock tower, and staying inside it means you walk out of your hotel straight into the best of the city. Graz is small and its centre is wonderfully walkable, so being central matters more than in a bigger city. The Schlossberghotel, the Palais-Hotel Erzherzog Johann, Hotel Weitzer, Boutique Hotel Dom and the landmark Grand Hôtel Wiesler all sit in or beside the old town. For a first visit, base yourself here and you can leave the tram map in your pocket.

**Is it worth staying in the Altstadt (old town)?**
For most visitors, yes. The Graz Altstadt is one of the best-preserved historic city centres in Central Europe and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a dense, red-roofed tangle of Renaissance courtyards, baroque façades and arcaded passages under the Schlossberg — and unlike Salzburg or Venice it is a real, lived-in city rather than a museum piece. Being able to walk out into it in the early morning or late evening, when the students are out and the squares are lively, is the version of Graz people fall for. Central hotel stock is broader and better value here than in Austria's more touristed cities, so you do not pay a heavy premium for the location.

**What is it like staying near the university?**
Quiet, green and pleasantly local. The main Karl-Franzens-University sits in the leafy Geidorf district, north-east of the centre — a calm residential quarter of nineteenth-century apartment houses, student cafés, bakeries and small parks, about a 15-minute walk or a couple of tram stops from Hauptplatz. It is a lovely base if you want to feel like a resident rather than a tourist, and the apartment-style stays around here (such as the Appartement in Graz-Geidorf) are good value for families and longer visits. You trade the on-the-doorstep old town for a bit of a stroll in, but in a city this walkable that is a small price.

**Where are the cheaper hotels in Graz?**
Around the main railway station (Hauptbahnhof) west of the centre, and out in the surrounding districts along the tram lines. The station area is the most useful of these for a visitor without a car: it is where the Vienna trains arrive, it has the highest concentration of budget and mid-range chain hotels in the city, and it is a 15 to 20 minute walk or a few tram stops from the old town. Further out — towards the Messe exhibition grounds in the south-east, the suburb of Hart bei Graz to the east, and the outer ring generally — rooms are cheaper again but assume you will ride the tram or drive in. In calm, tram-friendly Graz none of these feel remote.

**Is the area around Graz Hauptbahnhof a good place to stay?**
Yes, more so than the equivalent district in many cities. Graz Hauptbahnhof was handsomely renovated and the streets around it are ordinary and safe rather than seedy, with a strong cluster of dependable hotels — the a&o, the Austria Trend Hotel Europa, the B&B Hotel Graz-Hbf, the design-led Hotel Daniel and the IntercityHotel all sit here. The old town is a flat 15 to 20 minute walk or a short hop on the frequent trams down Annenstraße, and the station itself puts the Vienna railjet and the airport bus on your doorstep. For a budget-minded or rail-arriving visitor, it is the practical base.

**How much does a budget hotel cost per night in Graz?**
Between about £40 and £115 a night for the 38 properties in the most affordable tier of this guide, with the middle of the tier sitting around £75 to £80. That is genuinely affordable for a UNESCO city and a world away from Salzburg or Vienna, where the cheapest rooms start far higher. The floor is set by the a&o by the station at about £40; from there the tier climbs steadily through chain budget hotels, apartments and characterful family-run houses. Not everything here is dirt-cheap — Graz is moderate rather than bargain-basement — but the value on offer is among the best in Austria.

**Is Graz cheaper than Vienna or Salzburg?**
Noticeably, yes. Graz is Austria's second city and a student town rather than a tourist-magnet capital or a festival city, so its hotels price calmly all year and rarely spike. Where Salzburg in festival week and Vienna in its peaks push their cheapest rooms well above £100 a night, Graz starts at about £40 and keeps a deep, honest field of four-star hotels in the £55 to £110 band. Food, coffee and wine are gentler on the wallet here too. For a UK visitor watching the budget, Graz is one of the best-value city breaks in the whole country.

**Is Graz walkable?**
Very. Graz is a compact city and its UNESCO old town is small, flat and made for walking — you can cross the historic centre on foot in 15 to 20 minutes, and most of the sights (Hauptplatz, Herrengasse, the cathedral, the Schlossberg, the Kunsthaus and the Murinsel) sit within that core. The one climb is up the Schlossberg itself, and even that has a funicular and a glass lift if you would rather not take the stairs. Once you are based centrally you will barely need transport at all; good shoes matter more than any ticket.

**Do I need a car in Graz?**
No. The centre is walkable, the tram and bus network covers everywhere else a visitor goes, and much of the old town is pedestrianised, so a car is more nuisance than help for a city stay. Keep one only if you are touring the Southern Styrian wine road, Riegersburg or the wider Styrian countryside, where public transport thins out — and even then consider picking it up on the day you leave the city. Austria drives on the right, and its motorways require a toll vignette you buy at the border or online.

**How do the trams work in Graz?**
Simply and cheaply. Graz has an extensive tram (Straßenbahn) network that fans out from Jakominiplatz, the central interchange a couple of minutes from Hauptplatz, and it is the backbone of city transport alongside the buses. Buy a single or 24-hour ticket from the machine at the stop or the Holding Graz app and validate it; a 24-hour ticket pays for itself quickly if you are staying out of the centre and riding in. The old town is small enough that many visitors barely use the trams, but they are the easy way to reach Eggenberg Palace, the station and the outer hotels.

**How many days do I need in Graz?**
Two full days is a comfortable fit for the city itself. Day one for the Altstadt — Hauptplatz, Herrengasse and the Landhaus courtyard, the cathedral and mausoleum, the Styrian Armoury, then up the Schlossberg for the clock tower and the view. Day two for the modern and riverside side — the Kunsthaus, the Murinsel, the Lend district's bars and market, and a tram out to Eggenberg Palace and its park. A third day opens up the best day trips: the Southern Styrian wine road or the clifftop fortress at Riegersburg. Many people pair Graz with Vienna on a wider Austria trip, the two being about 2 hours 30 apart by train.

**What is there to see in Graz?**
More than its size suggests. The signature sight is the Schlossberg, the wooded hill in the middle of the city crowned by the Uhrturm, the 16th-century clock tower that is Graz's emblem, reached by funicular, glass lift or a zig-zag of steps. The UNESCO old town below holds Hauptplatz and its town hall, the arcaded Renaissance Landhaus, the cathedral and the imperial mausoleum, and the Styrian Armoury (Landeszeughaus) — the largest historic armoury in the world, with some 32,000 pieces. On the river sit two icons of Graz's 2003 Capital of Culture year: the biomorphic Kunsthaus art museum, nicknamed the Friendly Alien, and the floating Murinsel island. On the western edge, baroque Eggenberg Palace and its peacock park are UNESCO-listed too.

**What is the Kunsthaus Friendly Alien?**
It is Graz's contemporary art museum, and one of the most striking modern buildings in Austria. Built for the city's 2003 turn as European Capital of Culture, the Kunsthaus is a great bulbous, blue, biomorphic blob of a building — locals affectionately call it the friendly alien — that squats on the west bank of the river Mur in the Lend district, its skin studded with lights that can display low-resolution animations at night. Inside it hosts changing exhibitions of modern and contemporary art. Even if you do not go in, it is worth walking over to see, and it pairs naturally with the nearby Murinsel and the bars of Lend and Mariahilf.

**Can I do a day trip to the Southern Styrian wine road?**
Yes, and it is one of the best reasons to base yourself in Graz. The Südsteirische Weinstraße (Southern Styrian Wine Road) begins about 45 minutes to an hour south of the city, a landscape of steep vine-covered hills so lush it is often compared to Tuscany, dotted with Buschenschank taverns serving the growers' own wine, cold platters and Styrian pumpkin seed oil. The region is famous for crisp whites — Sauvignon Blanc, Welschriesling, Morillon — and for the klapotetz, the wooden wind-rattles that scare birds from the grapes. It is really a driving or organised-tour trip rather than a public-transport one; hire a car for the day or join a tour from the city.

**Can I visit Riegersburg from Graz?**
Yes. Riegersburg is one of the most dramatic castles in Austria — a vast medieval fortress perched on a sheer volcanic crag about an hour east of Graz, towering over the rolling farmland of eastern Styria. It is a genuine cliff-top stronghold you climb up to, with museums on witches and weaponry inside and birds-of-prey displays in season. The same corner of Styria is home to the Zotter chocolate factory, a cult artisan chocolate-maker with a tasting tour, and the Vulkanland thermal spas, which makes for an easy full day out by car. Public transport is possible but slow, so a car or a tour is the practical way to go.

**What food is Graz and Styria known for?**
Styria calls itself the green heart of Austria, and its food is fresher and more vegetable-forward than the schnitzel-and-dumplings cliché. The signature product is Steirisches Kürbiskernöl — Styrian pumpkin seed oil, a dark, almost black-green, intensely nutty oil drizzled over salads, soups, cheeses and even vanilla ice cream, and protected like a fine wine. Add to that the region's crisp white wines, its apples, its Vulcano ham, its pumpkin dishes and its Backhendl (fried chicken), and you have one of Austria's best food regions. Graz itself is a designated UNESCO City of Gastronomy, with a lively café scene and excellent farmers' markets at Kaiser-Josef-Platz and Lendplatz.

**When is the best time to visit Graz?**
Late spring and early autumn are the sweet spot — May, June and September deliver warm, settled weather, open terraces and the wine country at its greenest or its harvest-busiest, without any real crowd pressure, because Graz never gets as busy as Austria's headline cities. Summer is warm and lively with the students away and plenty of outdoor events, though July and August can be hot. Autumn is wine and pumpkin-harvest season in Styria and arguably the tastiest time to come. Winter is quiet and cold with an atmospheric Advent market season; hotel prices stay gentle year-round, so the choice is really about weather rather than cost.

**Is Graz good for families?**
It is an easy, manageable family city. The centre is compact and walkable, the Schlossberg is an adventure in itself — the glass lift, the funicular, the tunnels dug into the hill and the slide inside it are all a hit with children — and the floating Murinsel, the open squares and the parks give little legs room to roam. Museums like the Zeughaus armoury and the interactive exhibits at the Universalmuseum Joanneum keep older kids engaged. On the practical side, the apartment stays near the university and the family rooms in the station-area hotels give you space and self-catering at a gentle price, which is exactly what a family budget wants.

**Is Graz safe for tourists?**
Yes. Graz is a prosperous, relaxed university city and one of the safer places you can visit in Europe, and the overwhelming majority of trips pass without any trouble at all. The realistic risks are the ordinary ones of any city: occasional pickpocketing in the busiest spots and around the station, and slips on the Schlossberg steps or on winter ice. Use normal sense with bags and phones in crowds, take care on the steep paths and stairs up the hill in the wet, and check the current UK Foreign Office travel advice for Austria before you go.

**Do I need to speak German in Graz?**
No. German is the official language and the local Styrian accent is broad, but Graz is a university city with tens of thousands of students and English is widely spoken in hotels, restaurants, shops and at the sights. A few words go a long way socially — Grüß Gott is the standard Austrian hello, danke is thank you — and using them is appreciated, but you will never be stuck. Menus and signs in the tourist core are very often bilingual, and younger Grazers in particular speak confident English.

**How much should I tip in Graz?**
Modestly, and far less than in North America. In restaurants and cafés the convention is to round up the bill or add roughly 5 to 10 per cent for good service, told to the server as you pay rather than left on the table — you state the total you want as you hand over the money or card. Round up a taxi fare, leave a euro or two for a hotel porter, and a couple of euros a day for housekeeping if you wish. Service here is not run on tips the way it is in the United States, so there is no need to over-think it.

**Is the tap water safe to drink in Graz?**
Yes — it is excellent, and you should drink it. Graz's tap water comes largely from protected alpine and groundwater sources in the surrounding hills and is high quality straight from the tap, so there is no need to buy bottled water for your room. Public drinking fountains around the city run clean water too in the warmer months. Carry a refillable bottle to save money and plastic; asking for tap water in a restaurant is fine, though it is more usual here to order a drink with a meal than in the UK.

**Do hotels in Graz add taxes and fees on top of the rate?**
Only a little. Austrian VAT is already included in the quoted room rate, so unlike North America the headline price is close to the real price. On top of it the city levies a small local tourist or accommodation tax per person per night — around a euro or two — which some hotels fold into the rate and others add at checkout. A few properties charge separately for parking or breakfast. It is minor compared with the taxes stacked onto a North American hotel bill, but check the total on the live page rather than the headline rate before you compare two hotels.
