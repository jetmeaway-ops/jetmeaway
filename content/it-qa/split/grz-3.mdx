## Where to Stay in Graz: Choosing Your Base

Graz makes this decision easy, because it is small and walkable and the choice really comes down to three areas. Here is the honest breakdown.

**The Altstadt (the UNESCO old town)** is where most first-time visitors should aim, and unlike Salzburg or Vienna you do not pay a punishing premium to be here. This is the compact historic core around **Hauptplatz** and **Herrengasse**, under the wooded **Schlossberg**, a dense and beautiful tangle of Renaissance courtyards, arcaded passages and red-tiled roofs — and, crucially, a real, lived-in student city rather than a preserved museum. Staying inside it means you walk out of your door into the best of Graz, early and late, when it is at its most atmospheric. The **Schlossberghotel** (the city's one five-star), the historic **Palais-Hotel Erzherzog Johann**, the boutique **Hotel Dom** by the cathedral, the central-edge **Parkhotel** and **Hotel Gollner**, and the riverside **Hotel Weitzer** and landmark **Grand Hôtel Wiesler** just across the Mur all sit in or beside this core.

**The university district (Geidorf)**, just north-east of the centre, is the choice for a quieter, more local stay. This is a leafy, residential quarter of nineteenth-century apartment houses, student cafés and small parks around the main **Karl-Franzens-University**, about a 15-minute walk or a couple of tram stops from Hauptplatz. It is calm and green and full of everyday Graz life, and the apartment-style stays here — such as the **Appartement in Graz-Geidorf** — are good value for families and longer visits who want space, a kitchen and a neighbourhood feel over an on-the-doorstep old town.

**The station district (Hauptbahnhof)** to the west is where the affordable, practical hotels cluster, and in Graz it is a genuinely good budget base rather than a compromise. The station was handsomely renovated, the surrounding streets are ordinary and safe, and the old town is a flat 15 to 20 minute walk or a short tram down **Annenstraße**. This is where you find the cheapest room in the guide — the **a&o** — alongside the **Austria Trend Hotel Europa**, the **B&B Hotel Graz-Hbf**, the design-led **Hotel Daniel**, the **IntercityHotel** and several more. For a visitor arriving on the Vienna railjet or watching the budget, it is the sensible place to sleep.

**The outer districts** — towards the **Messe** exhibition grounds in the south-east, **Hart bei Graz** to the east, and the northern and southern fringes — are cheaper again and built around drivers. Rooms and apartments are larger, parking is easy, and the rates are the lowest in the guide, but you trade a walkable centre for a tram ride or a short drive in. The **ibis Styles Graz Messe**, the two **harry's home** properties, the **JUFA** hotels, the quirky **Novapark** aeroplane hotel and the traditional country-style Gasthöfe live out here. In tram-friendly Graz the commute is a small chore rather than a real one, but check the line and the journey on the live page before you book.

The short version: **for a first trip, base yourself in the Altstadt and walk everywhere** — the premium for it here is small. For a quieter, more local and often cheaper stay, take Geidorf near the university. For the lowest prices and an easy rail arrival, the station district is the practical answer. Only head to the outer districts if you are driving or chasing the very cheapest room, and check the tram time on the live page before you commit.

## Beyond the Hotel: What to See in Graz

Start with the hill. **The Schlossberg** is the wooded outcrop that rises straight out of the middle of the city, and climbing it — or riding up — is the first thing to do in Graz. At the top stands the **Uhrturm**, the 16th-century **clock tower** that is the city's emblem, its giant clock faces famously oversized because they were designed to be read from far below (the large hand originally showed the hours). The fortress that once crowned the hill was demolished by Napoleon's troops, but the tower and the bell tower survived because the citizens bought them back, and the views over the red-roofed old town to the Styrian hills are the best in the city. Get up there by the **Schlossbergbahn funicular**, the glass **Schlossberg lift** driven up through the rock, or a zig-zag of stone steps — and inside the hill are wartime tunnels and, for children, a slide that runs down through the rock.

Below the hill, **the Altstadt** is one of the best-preserved historic centres in Central Europe and a **UNESCO World Heritage Site**. Its heart is **Hauptplatz**, the main square with the grand **Rathaus** (town hall) and the Erzherzog-Johann fountain, from which **Herrengasse** — the arcaded main street — runs down to Jakominiplatz past the **Landhaus**, a superb arcaded Renaissance courtyard that is one of the finest north of Italy. Attached to it is the **Landeszeughaus (Styrian Armoury)**, the **largest historic armoury in the world**, its four floors still stacked with some 32,000 pieces of armour and weaponry exactly as they were left — an extraordinary, atmospheric sight. Nearby stand the **Cathedral (Dom)** and, beside it, the flamboyant **Mausoleum** of Emperor Ferdinand II, one of the most important Mannerist buildings in Austria.

Then cross the river for the modern Graz. On the west bank sits the **Kunsthaus**, the contemporary art museum built for the city's 2003 stint as **European Capital of Culture** — a great bulbous, biomorphic, blue blob of a building that locals affectionately call the **"Friendly Alien"**, its skin studded with lights that animate at night. In the river beside it floats the **Murinsel (Mur Island)**, an artificial shell-shaped island-platform of glass and steel, designed by Vito Acconci for the same year, holding a café and a small amphitheatre and linked to both banks by walkways. Around them, the **Lend** and **Mariahilf** districts are the arty, bar-and-café heart of young Graz, with the lively **Lendplatz** market — the best area in the city for an evening out.

On the western edge, **Eggenberg Palace (Schloss Eggenberg)** is Graz's grandest building and part of the same UNESCO listing as the old town. It is a magnificent Baroque palace built as a model of the cosmos — 365 windows for the days of the year, 24 state rooms for the hours, four towers for the seasons — with lavish painted halls and a large park where **peacocks** roam freely. It is a short tram ride out from the centre and well worth a half-day; the park alone is a lovely escape on a warm afternoon.

And then there is **Styria beyond the city**, which is a big part of why Graz rewards more than a flying visit. **Styria calls itself the green heart of Austria**, and the countryside around Graz is glorious. To the south, the **Südsteirische Weinstraße (Southern Styrian Wine Road)** begins under an hour away — a landscape of steep, vine-covered hills so lush it is regularly compared to Tuscany, dotted with **Buschenschank** taverns where growers serve their own crisp white wines, cold platters and the region's famous **pumpkin seed oil**. To the east, about an hour out, the medieval fortress of **Riegersburg** sits dramatically on a sheer volcanic crag, near the cult **Zotter** chocolate factory and the thermal spas of the Vulkanland. Both make superb day trips, and both are really car-or-tour outings rather than public-transport ones.

**The food is a genuine pleasure here, and distinctly Styrian.** Graz is a designated **UNESCO City of Gastronomy**, and the local table is fresher and greener than the schnitzel cliché. The signature is **Steirisches Kürbiskernöl** — Styrian pumpkin seed oil, a dark, almost black-green, intensely nutty oil drizzled over salads, soups, cheeses and even vanilla ice cream, and protected like a fine wine. Add the region's crisp whites, its **Backhendl** (fried chicken), its Vulcano ham, its apples and its pumpkin dishes, and eat your way round the wonderful **farmers' markets** on **Kaiser-Josef-Platz** and **Lendplatz**, and you have one of the best food regions in the country — at gentle Graz prices.

## Getting Around Graz

**Walking is the answer for the centre.** Graz is a compact city and its old town is small and flat: you can cross the historic core on foot in 15 to 20 minutes, and Hauptplatz, Herrengasse, the cathedral, the Schlossberg, the Kunsthaus and the Murinsel all sit within it. Once you are based centrally you will barely use transport at all — good shoes and a light waterproof matter more than any ticket, and the only real climb is the Schlossberg, which has a funicular and a lift if you would rather not take the steps.

**The trams (Straßenbahn)** are the backbone of city transport, and they are what you use to reach anywhere the walk does not. The network fans out from **Jakominiplatz**, the central interchange a couple of minutes' walk from Hauptplatz, where nearly every line and many buses meet. Buy a single or 24-hour ticket from the machine at the stop or the **Holding Graz** app and validate it; a 24-hour ticket quickly pays for itself if you are staying out of the centre and riding in. The trams are the easy way to reach **Eggenberg Palace**, the **Hauptbahnhof** and the outer hotels.

**Arriving and leaving**, most UK visitors come via **Vienna**: direct **railjet** trains run from Vienna Hauptbahnhof to Graz roughly hourly, taking about **2 hours 30 minutes** over the scenic, UNESCO-listed **Semmering** mountain line — a journey worth the window seat. **Graz Airport (GRZ)**, about 10 kilometres south of the centre, has only a thin, mostly seasonal scattering of direct UK links, but when it is used it connects to the centre by the **S-Bahn train** (to the Hauptbahnhof in around 15 minutes) and by city bus. Some visitors also fly into **Ljubljana** in Slovenia, about two hours south by road, or connect through Frankfurt, Munich or Vienna.

**Do not hire a car for the city itself** — the centre is walkable, much of the old town is pedestrianised, and parking is pushed into paid garages. Hire a car only for the Styrian countryside — the wine road, Riegersburg, the wider region — remember that Austrian motorways require a **toll vignette** (buy it at the border or online), and consider picking the car up on the day you leave the city rather than paying to park it unused during your city days. Austria drives on the right.

**Two money habits worth building.** First, unlike North America, **VAT is already in the price** on menus and shelves, so the number you see is broadly the number you pay — the only extra on a hotel bill is a small per-person **city tourist tax** of a euro or two a night, which some hotels fold into the rate and others add at checkout. Second, **tipping is modest**: round up the bill or add 5 to 10 per cent for good service in restaurants, told to the server as you pay rather than left on the table, round up a taxi fare, and leave a euro or two for a porter. Nobody here works for tips the way they do in the United States.
