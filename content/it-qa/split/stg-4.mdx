## Strasbourg Hotels FAQs

**Are there direct flights from the UK to Strasbourg?**
Rarely, and not reliably. Strasbourg Entzheim (SXB) is a small regional airport, and UK direct service has come and gone as seasonal or short-lived routes rather than a year-round fixture, so check whether anything is actually flying on your dates before you build a trip around it. The dependable options are all indirect: fly or take Eurostar to Paris and finish on the TGV in about 1h46, or fly to Basel-Mulhouse, Karlsruhe/Baden-Baden or Frankfurt and come in overland. Most UK visitors arrive by train, and that is usually the right call.

**How do I get from Paris to Strasbourg?**
TGV from Paris Gare de l'Est, roughly 1h46 non-stop on the fastest services, arriving at Strasbourg's central station a ten-minute walk from the cathedral. Book well ahead for the cheapest advance fares, because TGV pricing rises steeply as the train fills. If you are coming from London, Eurostar lands at Gare du Nord and Gare de l'Est is one Metro stop or a ten-minute walk away, which makes London to Strasbourg a comfortable single-day journey of roughly five to six hours door to door.

**Which airport is best for Strasbourg if I cannot get a direct flight?**
Three realistic alternatives. Basel-Mulhouse (EuroAirport) is the busiest UK-served option and sits about two hours south by road or rail. Karlsruhe/Baden-Baden (FKB) across the German border is roughly an hour away and often has cheap Stansted fares. Frankfurt (FRA) is about two hours by direct high-speed train and has the widest UK choice of all. Paris Charles de Gaulle also works, because the TGV from Paris is fast and frequent. Weigh the transfer time and cost against the fare before assuming the cheapest flight is the cheapest trip.

**Do UK passport holders need a visa for Strasbourg?**
No visa for tourism. UK passport holders can visit France and the rest of the Schengen area visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. Your passport needs to have been issued within the previous ten years and to be valid for at least three months after the day you plan to leave. Border rules for UK travellers have been changing, so read the UK Foreign Office travel advice for France before you book rather than relying on what was true last time you went.

**What are EES and ETIAS, and do they affect my trip?**
EES is the EU Entry/Exit System, a biometric border check that records a facial image and fingerprints instead of stamping your passport, and it has been rolling out in stages across Schengen external borders — expect a slightly slower first crossing while you are registered. ETIAS is a separate pre-travel authorisation that UK visitors will need to apply for online for a fee once it starts, and its launch date has been pushed back more than once. Because both timelines keep moving, check the UK government France travel advice page for the position on your own travel dates rather than trusting an older article.

**What currency does Strasbourg use?**
The euro. Alsace is French, so it is euros everywhere, and the German border town of Kehl across the Rhine uses euros too. Cards are accepted almost universally in hotels, restaurants, shops and at the tram ticket machines, and contactless is normal. Carry a small amount of cash for Christmas market huts, some winstubs and market stalls. Sterling is not accepted anywhere.

**Can you drink the tap water in Strasbourg?**
Yes. Tap water across France is treated to strict standards and Strasbourg is no exception, so fill a bottle at the hotel rather than buying plastic. Restaurants will bring a free carafe d'eau if you ask for it, which is a legal expectation rather than a favour, and there are public drinking fountains around the Grande Île. Ordering bottled water is a choice, not a necessity.

**Do I need to tip in Strasbourg?**
Not really. Service is included in French menu prices by law, so a tip is a bonus rather than an obligation. Rounding up the bill or leaving a euro or two per person for good service in a winstub is normal and appreciated, and five to ten per cent is generous for a long, attentive meal. Taxi drivers do not expect more than a rounded-up fare, and nobody tips for a coffee or a beer at a bar.

**Why does Strasbourg have so few five-star hotels?**
Because the city is smaller than its reputation and its visitor mix is unusual. Strasbourg has roughly 290,000 residents, a compact UNESCO island at its centre with tight planning constraints on new build, and demand driven by European institutions, conferences and short leisure breaks rather than long luxury stays. Our verified list found only three five-star properties with enough review depth to rank honestly. What Strasbourg has instead is an unusually strong four-star tier — ten of them here, several with review counts in the thousands — and that is where the real quality of this city sits.

**When is the Strasbourg Christmas market?**
The Christkindelsmärik runs from late November until Christmas, spread across Place Broglie, Place Kléber, Place de la Cathédrale and several smaller squares on and around the Grande Île. It dates back to 1570, which makes it one of the oldest in Europe, and Strasbourg brands itself the Capital of Christmas on the strength of it. Exact opening and closing dates shift a little each year, so confirm them on the official tourism site before you lock in non-refundable travel.

**How far ahead should I book a Strasbourg hotel for the Christmas market?**
Months, not weeks. This is the single biggest demand event of the Strasbourg year and the city simply does not have the room stock to absorb it — hotels across every tier sell out, and what remains is priced at a large multiple of the normal rate. Aim to book by the summer for a December weekend, and treat September as late. If you are deciding in November, expect either nothing at all or eye-watering prices, and look seriously at staying outside the city and commuting in by tram or train.

**How much do Strasbourg hotel prices rise during the Christmas market?**
Enough to change what kind of trip you are taking. December weekends near the market routinely price at a large multiple of the same room in February, and the cheapest end of the market disappears first because those rooms sell out rather than reprice. The practical consequence is that the from-prices in this guide, pulled on ordinary flexible dates, are not the prices you will see for a December weekend. Book many months early, or accept a room on the outskirts and a tram ride in.

**What are European Parliament session weeks and why do they matter for hotels?**
Strasbourg is the official seat of the European Parliament, which holds around a dozen plenary sessions a year, typically Monday to Thursday. During those weeks several thousand MEPs, staff, journalists, interpreters and lobbyists arrive at once, and a city of Strasbourg's size feels it immediately: availability tightens across the board and rates rise, including in the budget tier. It is the least-known booking trap in the city. If your dates are flexible, look at the Parliament's published plenary calendar first and pick a week outside it.

**How do I check whether my dates fall in a Parliament session week?**
The European Parliament publishes its plenary calendar for the year on its own website well in advance, and the Strasbourg tourist office is well aware of the effect. If you cannot find the calendar easily, a good proxy is to run the same hotel search on two neighbouring weeks: if one week is dramatically more expensive and thinner on availability than the weeks either side, and it is not December, you have almost certainly found a plenary.

**What is the best area to stay in Strasbourg for first-time visitors?**
The Grande Île — the UNESCO-listed island at the heart of the old town, ringed by the river Ill. Staying on or beside it puts the cathedral, Petite France, the covered bridges, the main squares and the best restaurants within a flat ten-minute walk, and it is the single most atmospheric place to sleep in the city. It is also where most of the four-star tier sits. The trade is price and, in high season, some evening noise near the busiest squares; light sleepers should ask for a courtyard-facing room.

**What is the cheapest area to stay in Strasbourg?**
The western and outer districts — around the Zénith arena, Lingolsheim, the port and the Rhine crossing towards Kehl — where the budget chains and aparthotels cluster and rates start around £35 to £55. You trade a central address for a tram or short drive into town, but Strasbourg's tram network is fast and cheap, so this is a genuinely workable saving rather than a compromise. The station quarter is the sweet spot: cheaper than the island, still walkable to it.

**Is Petite France a good place to stay?**
It is the prettiest corner of the city and a lovely place to be based if you can get a room there — half-timbered houses leaning over the canals, the covered bridges and the Barrage Vauban all on your doorstep. The catch is that Petite France is small, protected and short on hotels, so options are limited and they price accordingly. If you cannot find one, anywhere on the Grande Île is a few minutes' walk away, which is almost as good.

**Where should I stay near Strasbourg station?**
The area immediately around Gare de Strasbourg is the practical base for anyone arriving by TGV, and it is only a ten-minute walk or one tram stop from the Grande Île. It carries several of this guide's better-value hotels and aparthotels, tends to be cheaper than the island itself, and puts you a step from the trains for day trips to Colmar and the wine route. It is a working city quarter rather than a postcard, but it is central, safe enough with normal city awareness, and very convenient.

**Should I stay in the European district near the Parliament?**
Only if your trip is built around the institutions. The European quarter — the Parliament, the Council of Europe and the Court of Human Rights — sits north-east of the centre around the Orangerie park, and it is a pleasant, green, quiet area, but it is a tram ride from the cathedral and the restaurants, and it has relatively few hotels. For a leisure break, base yourself on or near the Grande Île and visit the institutions as a half-day; for official business at the Parliament, a hotel out here can make sense.

**What is the best area to stay in Strasbourg with a family?**
For families, the aparthotels around the station and the Wilson/Contades area are the practical pick — a kitchenette, separate sleeping space and a lower per-night rate matter more with children than a postcard address, and you are still a short walk or tram ride from the island. If you want to be central and do not need self-catering, the quieter edges of the Grande Île work well. The Orangerie park in the European district, with its little zoo and boating lake, is the city's best free afternoon out with kids.

**How do I get around Strasbourg?**
On foot and by tram. The Grande Île is compact, flat and largely pedestrianised, so you will walk most of a city break. For everything beyond it — the station, the European district, the outer hotels — the CTS tram network is fast, frequent, cheap and clean, with several lines crossing the centre, and one line (D) that runs across the Rhine into Kehl in Germany. Buy a single or a day ticket from the machines at any stop; validate it as you board.

**Is Strasbourg a good city for cycling?**
It is one of the best in France for it. Strasbourg has an exceptionally dense cycle-lane network, flat terrain and a bike-share scheme (Vélhop) with hire points across the city, and locals treat the bike as the default way to get around. For a visitor it is a genuinely good way to reach Petite France, the European district and the parks without waiting for a tram, and the riverside paths are a pleasure in their own right. Just watch the pedestrianised island, where bikes are restricted in the busiest zones.

**Can I do a day trip to Colmar and the Alsace wine route from Strasbourg?**
Easily, and you should. Colmar is about 30 minutes south by frequent TER train and is the storybook heart of Alsace — canals, half-timbered houses and the Unterlinden Museum. The Alsace Wine Route runs through the villages between and around the two cities — Riquewihr, Eguisheim, Ribeauvillé — and is best explored by car or on an organised half-day tour, since the prettiest villages are not all on the railway. Strasbourg makes a natural base for both.

**Is Strasbourg or Colmar the better base?**
Strasbourg for a city break, Colmar for a wine-country base. Strasbourg is bigger, has the cathedral, the European institutions, a far wider choice of hotels and restaurants, and better transport links to the UK and to Paris. Colmar is smaller, prettier in a concentrated way, and sits deeper in the vineyards, which makes it the better home for a wine-route touring holiday. Many visitors do both — a couple of nights in each — and the 30-minute train between them makes that painless.

**What is the best time of year to visit Strasbourg?**
Late spring and early autumn — May, June, September — give you warm weather, long days, the terraces open and none of the Christmas-market crush. December is magical but expensive and crowded, and you must book far ahead. July and August are pleasant but can be humid, and some family-run winstubs close for summer holidays. Winter outside the market weeks is quiet and cheap but cold. For the best balance of weather, price and atmosphere, aim for the shoulder months.

**How many days do I need in Strasbourg?**
Two full days covers the city comfortably — one for the Grande Île, the cathedral and Petite France, and one for the European district, the parks and the museums, with time for the food. Add a third and a fourth day if you want to fold in Colmar and the wine route, which most people should, because the villages are a highlight of the region. A single day is enough to see the cathedral and Petite France but too rushed to feel the city.

**Is Strasbourg safe for tourists?**
Yes, it is a comfortable, walkable city for visitors, with the usual big-city caveats. Keep an eye on your belongings in the busy market squares and on packed trams, particularly during the Christmas market when the crowds are dense, and use normal care around the station area at night. Violent crime against tourists is rare. As always, read the current UK Foreign Office advice for France before you travel for any specific security guidance.

**What food is Strasbourg known for?**
Alsatian cooking, which is French technique with a strong German accent. The signatures are tarte flambée (flammekueche) — a wafer-thin flatbread with crème fraîche, onions and lardons, cooked in a wood oven; choucroute garnie — fermented cabbage with sausages and pork; baeckeoffe, a slow-cooked meat and potato casserole; and pretzels, kougelhopf cake and Munster cheese. Eat it in a winstub, the traditional Alsatian wine tavern, and drink the local Riesling, Pinot Gris or Gewürztraminer, or an Alsace beer, alongside.

**What is a winstub?**
A winstub — literally 'wine room' in Alsatian dialect — is the traditional cosy Alsatian tavern, wood-panelled and lace-curtained, serving regional dishes and local wine in a deliberately homely setting. It is the authentic place to eat choucroute, baeckeoffe and tarte flambée, and the atmosphere is a large part of the point. The best ones on and around the Grande Île fill up in the evening, especially at weekends and in December, so book a table rather than turning up and hoping.

**Why does Strasbourg feel both French and German?**
Because it has been both. Alsace sits on the Rhine border and changed hands between France and Germany repeatedly between 1871 and 1945, and the layers are still visible: French to the west of the station in the medieval old town, grand Wilhelmine German architecture in the Neustadt district built under the German Empire, and a local Alsatian dialect and cuisine that belong to neither exactly. That blend — the cathedral and the winstubs, the tarte flambée and the Riesling, the tram that runs into Germany — is a large part of what makes the city distinctive.

**Do hotels in Strasbourg include breakfast?**
It varies by tier. The four- and five-star hotels usually offer an Alsatian breakfast, sometimes included in the rate and sometimes charged separately at €15 to €25 a head, and it is generally good — local breads, viennoiserie, cheese and charcuterie. Budget hotels and aparthotels often price breakfast as an extra or leave you to self-cater, which is easy given how good the city's bakeries are. **Can I walk everywhere in central Strasbourg?**
Yes — the Grande Île and its immediate surroundings are compact, flat and largely car-free, and the cathedral, Petite France, the main squares, the museums and dozens of restaurants are all within a ten to fifteen minute walk of each other. You only need the tram for the station (a ten-minute walk anyway), the European district and the outer hotels. It is one of the most pleasant French cities to explore entirely on foot.

**Are the budget hotels near Strasbourg station a good choice?**
Yes, for most visitors they are the smart pick. The station quarter and the streets just west of the Grande Île carry several of this guide's best-value hotels and aparthotels, they are a ten-minute walk or one tram stop from the cathedral, and they put you next to the trains for Colmar, the wine route and Paris. You save meaningfully against an island address for a walk you would happily do anyway. It is a working city quarter, not a postcard, but it is central and convenient.
