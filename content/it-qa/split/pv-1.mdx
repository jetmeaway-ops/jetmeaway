  - q: "Do UK travellers need a visa for Mexico?"
    a: "UK passport holders do not need a visa for short tourist stays in Mexico. You clear immigration on arrival and the officer grants you a specific number of days — check what they have written or stamped before you leave the desk, because it is not automatically the maximum.   - q: "What is the FMM tourist card and do I still need one?"
    a: "The FMM, or Forma Migratoria Multiple, is Mexico's tourist card — historically a paper form you filled in on the plane, handed half of to immigration on arrival and kept the other half to surrender on departure. Mexico has been moving to a digital and stamp-based system at many airports, including the busier ones, so you may not receive a paper slip at all. If you are given one, keep it somewhere safe with your passport for the whole trip, because losing it used to mean a fee and a queue on the way home. Either way, check the number of days written or stamped in your passport before you walk away from the desk."
  - q: "Where should I stay in Puerto Vallarta — which area?"
    a: "There are four practical choices and they suit very different trips. The Zona Romantica, also called Old Town or Emiliano Zapata, sits south of the Rio Cuale around Playa Los Muertos and is the most walkable, most characterful and most restaurant-dense part of town — cobbled streets, small hotels, the pier, and the heart of Puerto Vallarta's LGBTQ+ scene. Centro is the old centre around the Malecon and the church, walkable and central with cheap rooms. The Hotel Zone, or Zona Hotelera, is the strip of larger beachfront resorts north of the centre, where most of the all-inclusives sit. And Marina Vallarta, further north near the airport, is the planned yacht-harbour district with golf, calmer streets and a slightly corporate feel. For a first visit, the Zona Romantica is almost always the right answer."
  - q: "What is the Zona Romantica and who should stay there?"
    a: "The Zona Romantica is the southern old-town quarter, running from the Rio Cuale down to Playa Los Muertos and up the hill behind it. It is the part of Puerto Vallarta that people fall in love with: narrow cobbled streets, terracotta roofs, hundreds of restaurants and bars within a few blocks, beach clubs on Los Muertos, the sculptural Los Muertos pier, and a long-established, genuinely welcoming LGBTQ+ scene concentrated around Olas Altas and Lazaro Cardenas. It suits couples, solo travellers, foodies and anyone who wants to walk everywhere. It suits you less if you want a big resort pool complex and a swim-up bar, because those are mostly further north."
  - q: "Should I stay in the Hotel Zone or the Marina instead?"
    a: "Stay in the Hotel Zone if the point of your trip is a large beachfront resort with pools, buffets and a lounger — it is a strip of big properties on Playa Las Glorias and the beaches either side, about 10 to 20 minutes by bus or taxi from the old town, and it is where most of the all-inclusives in this guide sit. Stay in Marina Vallarta if you want calm, golf, a yacht harbour to walk around in the evening and a very short airport run, and you do not mind that the restaurant scene is thinner and the beach is not the town's best. Neither is walkable to the Malecon, so factor transport into both."
  - q: "What is the Malecon in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "The Malecon is the seafront promenade running along the centre of town, roughly a mile of paved walkway between the sea and the old centre, lined with bronze sculptures, bars, restaurants, street performers and, at the southern end, the Los Arcos amphitheatre. Walking it at sunset is the single most reliably enjoyable free thing to do in Puerto Vallarta, and it is the axis the whole town organises itself around. Several of the cheapest hotels in this guide are a block or two behind it."

  - q: "When is the best time to visit Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "November to April is the dry season and it is close to perfect — warm days, low humidity, almost no rain, and the sea calm enough for boat trips. It is also the busy, expensive stretch, with Christmas, New Year and Easter as the hard peaks. December to March adds humpback whales in the bay, which is a genuine reason to travel then.   - q: "When is the rainy season in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Roughly June to October, peaking in August and September. It does not usually rain all day — the classic pattern is hot, bright, humid mornings with heavy tropical downpours and thunderstorms in the late afternoon or overnight, which is why plenty of experienced visitors happily travel then. The bay and the hills behind it are spectacularly green in that window, prices drop hard, and restaurants that need booking weeks ahead in February will seat you straight away. The trade is humidity, mosquitoes, rougher seas on some days and the small but real chance of a tropical storm."
  - q: "Is hurricane season a real risk in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "The Pacific hurricane season runs roughly mid-May to the end of November, with the highest risk in August, September and October. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than heavy afternoon rain, and Puerto Vallarta sits in a large, sheltered bay which offers some protection, but the risk is genuine and has produced serious storms in the past. If you travel then, take insurance that explicitly covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility in your plans, and follow local advice if a storm is forecast."
  - q: "Is it worth going to Puerto Vallarta in the rainy season to save money?"
    a: "For a lot of UK travellers, yes — and this is where the real bargains in this guide live. Room rates in the June-to-October window are frequently a third to a half below the February peak, the town is quiet, the landscape is at its most dramatic, and the mornings are usually fine. You are trading dry certainty for cost. If you are travelling with small children, if you cannot afford to lose a day to weather, or if the whole point is a beach lounger, pay the extra and go in the dry season instead."
  - q: "When can I see whales in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Humpback whales migrate into Banderas Bay to calve and breed roughly from December to March, and the season is usually described locally as running from mid-December to late March. It is one of the best whale-watching bays in Mexico and sightings in the heart of the season are common rather than lucky — you can sometimes see spouts and breaches from the Malecon. Go with a licensed operator that follows the approach-distance rules, book a morning trip when the sea is calmest, and understand that nothing about wild animals is guaranteed."
  - q: "How do I get to Yelapa from Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Yelapa is a small village on the southern shore of Banderas Bay with no road connection, so you reach it by boat. Water taxis run from the Los Muertos pier in the Zona Romantica and from Boca de Tomatlan, the little cove at the southern end of the bay bus route, with the Boca crossing being shorter and cheaper. Expect a rough guide of 45 minutes or so from Los Muertos and around half an hour from Boca, weather depending. Check the last return departure carefully before you go, take cash, and consider staying a night if the place gets under your skin — plenty of people do."
  - q: "What are the Marietas Islands and can I see the Hidden Beach?"
    a: "The Marietas are a protected group of uninhabited islands at the mouth of Banderas Bay, a national park and an important seabird colony, best known for Playa del Amor — the Hidden Beach, a sand beach inside a collapsed crater open to the sky. Access is strictly limited to protect the site: visitor numbers are capped daily, the beach is closed on some days, only licensed operators with permits can take you, and reaching the beach itself involves a swim through a low tunnel with a life jacket. Book with a permitted operator well ahead and treat the Hidden Beach as a bonus rather than a guarantee — the snorkelling, the birdlife and the boat ride are worth the day on their own."
  - q: "What are the timeshare touts at Puerto Vallarta airport?"
    a: "This is the single most consistent complaint from first-time visitors, so know about it before you land. The arrivals area at PVR contains booths and staff who present themselves as tourist information, transport desks or hotel check-in assistance, and who will ask which hotel you are staying at and offer you a free or heavily discounted transfer. The transfer is real; the price is a 90-minute or longer timeshare presentation the next morning. The rule is simple: do not stop, do not answer questions about your hotel, do not accept a free ride or a free breakfast, and keep walking until you are outside the terminal building where the official taxi and Uber pickup areas are. Being politely firm once at the door saves an entire morning of your holiday."
  - q: "Is the tap water safe to drink in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Stick to bottled or filtered water. Puerto Vallarta's municipal water has a better local reputation than most of Mexico's and many residents drink it, but as a visitor you have no immunity to the local microbiology and it is not worth the risk on a short holiday. Hotels almost always provide purified drinking water, either bottles in the room or a refill dispenser in the lobby, restaurants use purified water and ice as standard, and brushing your teeth with tap water is generally fine. Bring a reusable bottle and use the refill stations."
  - q: "Is Puerto Vallarta safe for tourists?"
    a: "Puerto Vallarta is one of Mexico's most established and most visited resort towns and the overwhelming majority of trips are completely trouble free, with the usual tourist-town issues — opportunistic theft, drink pricing, taxi overcharging — rather than anything more dramatic. Sensible precautions still apply: use official taxis or Uber rather than unmarked cars, agree taxi fares before setting off, use ATMs inside bank branches, do not carry more cash than you need, watch your drink, and take care swimming where there is surf, because some beaches south of town have a real undertow.   - q: "Is Puerto Vallarta LGBTQ+ friendly?"

The atmosphere in that part of town is relaxed and mainstream rather than niche. As anywhere, attitudes are more conservative outside the tourist areas and in smaller towns inland, so read the room when you travel further afield."
  - q: "How do I get around Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "The old town — Centro and the Zona Romantica — is comfortably walkable, and that is the main reason to stay there. For longer distances the local buses are cheap, frequent and genuinely useful: routes run north along the Hotel Zone to the Marina and the airport, and south along the coast road to Mismaloya and Boca de Tomatlan for the Yelapa boats. Taxis are widely available and are not metered, so agree the fare before you get in; Uber also operates here and removes the negotiation. Water taxis from Los Muertos pier reach the southern beaches with no road access. Note that the old town is steep and cobbled in places, which matters if you have mobility issues or a pushchair."
  - q: "Do I need a hire car in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Not for a normal beach and town holiday. Everything in the old town is walkable, the buses cover the Hotel Zone, the Marina, the airport and the southern coast road cheaply, and Uber fills the gaps. A car earns its keep if you want to explore the Riviera Nayarit north of the bay — Sayulita, San Pancho, Punta Mita — on your own schedule, or drive inland to the mountain towns.   - q: "What currency should I use in Puerto Vallarta — pesos or dollars?"
    a: "The Mexican Peso (MXN) is the currency and it is what you should use. US dollars are widely accepted in Puerto Vallarta because of the volume of American and Canadian visitors, but the exchange rate you are given is almost always poor and change frequently comes back in pesos at the seller's own rate. Pay in pesos, withdraw from ATMs inside proper bank branches rather than freestanding machines in shops, carry small notes for taxis and tips, and if a card machine offers to charge you in pounds, always decline and let your own bank do the conversion."
  - q: "How much should I tip in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Tipping is customary and staff rely on it. Around 15 percent is the norm in restaurants, and you should check the bill first because some places add a service charge — propina — and you do not want to pay twice. Bar staff, hotel housekeeping, tour guides, boat crew, airport porters and the people who bag your shopping at the supermarket are all tipped. Tip in pesos rather than dollars where you can, and carry small notes specifically for it, because nobody will have change for a large one."
  - q: "Do I need to speak Spanish in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "No. Spanish is the language, but Puerto Vallarta has been an international resort town for over half a century and English is spoken almost everywhere a visitor goes — hotels, restaurants, tour offices, shops and taxis. A few polite words are still worth having and are always appreciated: hola, por favor, gracias, la cuenta por favor. Away from the tourist areas and in the villages inland, English drops off quickly."
  - q: "Is Puerto Vallarta good for families?"
    a: "Yes, and it is a noticeably easier family destination than some of its rivals. The bay is sheltered so the sea is calmer than the open Pacific, the Hotel Zone all-inclusives are set up for children with pools and kids clubs, boat trips and whale watching work well with older children, and the airport is close enough that the transfer does not destroy the first day. The complications are that a meaningful share of Puerto Vallarta's best-known hotels are adults-only, that the old town's cobbles and steep streets are hard work with a pushchair, and that some southern beaches have a strong undertow. Families usually do best in the Hotel Zone or the Marina, or in a family-suite hotel in Centro."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Given the length of the journey from the UK, seven nights is the sensible minimum and ten to fourteen is better. Four or five nights works only if Puerto Vallarta is one stop on a wider Mexico route, and even then the flying-to-holiday ratio is poor."
  - q: "Is there sargassum seaweed in Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "No — and this is one of the strongest arguments for the Pacific coast over the Caribbean one. Sargassum is an Atlantic and Caribbean phenomenon, so the beaches around Puerto Vallarta are not affected by it at all, unlike Cancun, Playa del Carmen and Tulum which can be badly hit between roughly April and August. Puerto Vallarta's beaches are golden-grey volcanic-influenced sand rather than the powdery white of the Caribbean, and the water is a deeper blue rather than turquoise, but you will not be looking at a wall of rotting weed."
  - q: "What is Sayulita and can I visit from Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Sayulita is a small surf town about an hour to an hour and a half north of Puerto Vallarta in the neighbouring state of Nayarit, known for beginner-friendly waves, colourful streets and a bohemian, backpacker-meets-boutique atmosphere. It is an easy day trip by bus from the north bus station or by car, and it makes a good contrast with Puerto Vallarta's larger, more polished resort scene. Note that Nayarit has historically observed a different time zone from Jalisco at some points, so check local times before you plan a return bus."
  - q: "Can I visit Mismaloya and Los Arcos from Puerto Vallarta?"
    a: "Yes, both are easy. Mismaloya is a cove about 20 to 30 minutes south of town on the coast road, reachable by the cheap local bus, and it is where The Night of the Iguana was filmed in the 1960s — the film that turned Puerto Vallarta into a resort town in the first place. Los Arcos is the cluster of rock arches and islets just offshore nearby, a protected marine area and the town's best snorkelling and diving site, visited by boat trip or by kayak from the shore. Both make comfortable half-days."

### The Best Mid-Range Hotels in Puerto Vallarta

This is the tier that solves Puerto Vallarta for most people, and it splits neatly in two. On one side are four-star **room-only hotels** at £61 to £84 a night — international business brands, a beachfront boutique on the Malecón, a riverside boutique in the old town — which are the sensible choice if you intend to eat out and explore. On the other are four-star **all-inclusive resorts** at £169 to £249, which are the sensible choice if you intend to stay put. Nothing here is a bad hotel. They are answers to two completely different questions.

![Holiday Inn Express Puerto Vallarta by IHG — Puerto Vallarta, Mexico](https://static.cupid.travel/hotels/230305066.jpg)

**11. [Holiday Inn Express Puerto Vallarta by IHG](/hotels/la_lp6de25?city=Puerto%20Vallarta)** — Puerto Vallarta · 4★ · 2,198 reviews · from ~£71/night. The second most-reviewed hotel in the guide with **2,198 reviews**, and an international IHG brand at around £71 a night. What you get is exactly what the badge promises: air conditioning that works, reliable wifi, a proper breakfast, a known cancellation policy and no surprises. That is worth a lot on a long-haul trip where the first night matters, and it is the obvious answer for anyone nervous about booking an unfamiliar independent. Check the location relative to the old town before booking, because the walkability varies.

![The Paramar Beachfront Boutique Hotel With Breakfast Included - Downtown Malecon — Puerto Vallarta, Mexico](https://static.cupid.travel/hotels/361159707.jpg)

**12. [The Paramar Beachfront Boutique Hotel With Breakfast Included - Downtown Malecon](/hotels/la_lp219232?city=Puerto%20Vallarta)** — Puerto Vallarta · 4★ · 1,631 reviews · from ~£84/night. Beachfront, boutique, breakfast included, and **on the downtown Malecón** — the listing name is an unusually accurate summary. At around £84 a night with 1,631 reviews this is arguably the best-positioned hotel in the whole guide: you walk out of the door onto the promenade, with the old centre behind you and the Zona Romántica a short stroll south across the river. If your priority is being in the middle of Puerto Vallarta rather than beside a big pool, this is the one to beat.

![Krystal Vallarta — Puerto Vallarta, Mexico](https://static.cupid.travel/hotels/9244999.jpg)

**13. [Krystal Vallarta](/hotels/la_lp1c15b?city=Puerto%20Vallarta)** — Puerto Vallarta · 4★ · 1,013 reviews · from ~£75/night. One of the longest-established resorts in the **Hotel Zone** and a genuinely distinctive one: low-rise villa-style buildings, cobbled paths and gardens rather than a single tower, which makes it feel more like a village than a hotel. At around £75 a night with 1,013 reviews it is one of the best-value beachfront addresses on this page. It is the older sibling of the Krystal Grand at number 10 — a different property with a different character, so compare them directly rather than assuming.

![Plaza Pelicanos Grand Beach Resort All Inclusive — Puerto Vallarta, Mexico](https://static.cupid.travel/hotels/82111713.jpg)

**14. [Plaza Pelicanos Grand Beach Resort All Inclusive](/hotels/la_lp59082?city=Puerto%20Vallarta)** — Puerto Vallarta · 4★ · 795 reviews · from ~£172/night. A beachfront all-inclusive on the **Hotel Zone** sands at around £172 a night with 795 reviews. This is the classic Puerto Vallarta package holiday: pool, beach, buffet, bar, entertainment, and a bus into town when you fancy it. Sensible for families and for anyone who wants the holiday costed up front. Poor value if you plan to be out at Yelapa, the Marietas and the old town's restaurants most days — you will be paying for meals you never eat.

![Costa Sur All Inclusive Resort — Puerto Vallarta, Mexico](https://static.cupid.travel/hotels/605515205.jpg)

**15. [Costa Sur All Inclusive Resort](/hotels/la_lp1c833?city=Puerto%20Vallarta)** — Puerto Vallarta · 4★ · 738 reviews · from ~£169/night. An all-inclusive on the **southern coast** towards Conchas Chinas and Mismaloya, where the shoreline turns rocky and dramatic and the hills come right down to the water. At around £169 a night it is the cheapest of the four-star all-inclusives here. The south coast is the prettiest stretch of the bay and the swimming is different — coves and rocks rather than a long flat beach — so check the photographs and decide whether that is what you want.

The Spanish Barceló group's Puerto Vallarta property, on the **southern coast road** out of town, at around £190 a night with 619 reviews. A large, full-facility all-inclusive with the reliability that comes from an international operator, set on one of the bay's better southern beaches. The trade-off is distance: you are a proper taxi or bus ride from the Malecón, so this works best as a stay-put resort week rather than a base for exploring.
