## What to Do in Punta Cana

- **Bávaro Beach and the coconut coast** — the reason almost everyone comes: kilometres of white sand and leaning coconut palms along a warm, shallow Caribbean. The gorgeous stretches are mostly resort-fronted, but the public beach at **El Cortecito** lets you enjoy the same coast even if you are staying room-only — bring a towel and eat at the beach shacks.
- **Saona Island** — the classic full-day catamaran trip to a protected palm-fringed island in the Parque Nacional del Este, with a natural sandbar swimming pool out at sea, snorkelling, a beach lunch and rum on the boat. Touristy but genuinely stunning, and the best-known day out from Punta Cana.
- **Scape Park and Hoyo Azul** — the adventure park in Cap Cana built around a jaw-dropping turquoise freshwater cenote, with zip lines, cave swims and jungle trails. One of the few real sights within Punta Cana itself, and unaffected by sargassum, so a good rainy-or-seaweed-day plan.
- **Catalina Island** — further west near La Romana, famous for shallow-reef snorkelling and a wall dive. A longer, earlier day than Saona, best if the underwater world rather than the beach is your priority.
- **Santo Domingo** — a long day trip west to the Dominican capital, whose **Zona Colonial** is the oldest European city in the Americas: cobbled streets, the first cathedral in the New World, forts and colonial squares, all UNESCO-listed. The real history counterpoint to the resort strip.
- **A catamaran party or snorkel cruise** — the shorter half-day boat trips along the Bávaro coast, mixing snorkelling on the natural reef and pool, a floating bar and music. Easy, fun and bookable through any hotel.
- **Golf** — Punta Cana and Cap Cana are among the Caribbean's top golf destinations, with several signature championship courses, most famously the cliff-and-ocean holes around Cap Cana and Punta Cana Resort. Worth a round if the game is your thing.

## Where to Base Yourself in Punta Cana — Bávaro, Cap Cana or Uvero Alto?

Punta Cana is a spread-out resort region, not a city, so where you base yourself matters — and unlike a city break, the three main zones feel quite different.

**Bávaro.** The heart of Punta Cana and the coconut coast: the longest run of resorts, the widest choice at every price, the shortest transfers from the airport at roughly 15 to 25 minutes, and — crucially — the only zone with a genuine walkable town. **El Cortecito** and neighbouring **Los Corales** give you a public beach, a strip of open-air seafood restaurants, dive shops and small stores, and it is where every budget and room-only hotel in this guide sits. For a first visit, for families, and for anyone watching the budget, Bávaro is the natural default.

**Cap Cana.** The upmarket, master-planned enclave just to the south, built around a marina and the exclusive **Juanillo beach**, with the glossiest resorts, championship golf and Scape Park on the doorstep. It is quieter, more polished and often escapes the worst of the sargassum, but it is pricier and there is little to do outside the resort gates without a taxi. The choice for a splurge or an upscale, do-nothing week.

**Uvero Alto.** The quieter stretch further north, around 40 minutes to an hour from the airport, with a run of large resorts on a wilder, emptier beach. Lovely for switching off completely, but genuinely remote — you will rely on your resort's facilities and organised excursions, and trips into Bávaro or beyond are a real drive. Best for a pure fly-and-flop, all-inclusive holiday where you have no intention of leaving the resort.

**Be honest with yourself about the trade.** If you want a full-board beach resort with everything on tap and no plans to leave it, any of the three works and Cap Cana or Uvero Alto add calm. If you want value, local restaurants, a walkable town and the freedom to explore, Bávaro — and especially the El Cortecito pocket — is the answer, and it is the only zone where the £22-to-£60 room-only market really exists.

## Honest Practicalities for Punta Cana

**Getting there, and the ESTA trap.** Punta Cana (PUJ) is one of the Caribbean's busiest airports, with its distinctive open-air thatched terminals, and there are regular **direct flights from the UK** — most reliably TUI and British Airways from London Gatwick, taking roughly **9 to 10 hours** — though your exact route may be direct or one-stop, so search your own dates. If you route one-stop through a US hub such as New York or Miami, you must hold an approved US **ESTA** even just to change planes, because there is no international transit zone in the US; a direct flight, or a one-stop via Madrid on Iberia or Air Europa, avoids that entirely. From the airport the Bávaro resorts are around 15 to 25 minutes away, Cap Cana similar, and Uvero Alto up to an hour, by pre-booked transfer or fixed-fare taxi.

**No visa, and the tourist card is already paid.** UK passport holders do not need a visa for tourist stays of up to 30 days. The old US$10 tourist card is now bundled into most airfares, so you usually have nothing to buy at immigration — check your booking confirmation, keep your passport valid for the whole trip, and have an onward or return ticket.

**The all-inclusive-versus-room-only decision is the big one.** More than the choice of hotel, this shapes your holiday and your budget. Punta Cana is built for all-inclusive: if you plan to stay put on the beach and by the pool, one price for a resort is genuinely good value and the least hassle. If you plan to be out on island trips and day trips and are happy to eat in local restaurants, a room-only base in El Cortecito is far cheaper and frees you from paying for meals you will not eat. Do the sums for your actual plans, and always check exactly what an all-inclusive rate includes, because they range from buffet-only to premium.

**Sargassum seaweed is real, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.** The brown Atlantic seaweed washes onto the east-facing coconut coast and Bávaro can get it, typically at its most likely from **roughly April to August**, though it varies enormously by year, week and beach and plenty of trips see clear water. When it lands in volume it piles up and smells of sulphur as it rots. The big resorts rake their sand each morning and some run offshore barriers, but nobody can promise a flawless sea on a given week — check recent dated photos and the daily beach updates before you travel, consider Cap Cana or a sheltered spot, and remember the pools, cenotes and island trips are unaffected.

**Hurricane season runs roughly June to November**, with peak risk from August to October — which, with the sargassum window, is exactly why late summer is so cheap. Most trips in that window see nothing worse than a short, heavy afternoon downpour, but the risk is real: take insurance that specifically covers weather disruption, keep some flexibility, and follow the forecasts in the run-up.

**Everyday sense goes a long way.** Use official taxis or pre-booked transfers rather than unmarked cars, agree fares before you set off because taxis are not metered, draw cash from ATMs inside bank branches, keep valuables in the room safe, and drink bottled or filtered water rather than tap. Pay in **Dominican Pesos (DOP)** where you can — US dollars are widely accepted but the rate is usually worse — and remember restaurants add a 10 percent service charge plus 18 percent ITBIS tax, so check the bill before tipping on top. Before you book anything, read the current [UK government travel advice for the Dominican Republic](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/dominican-republic), which carries practical safety and entry guidance that is updated over time, and for trip ideas and official destination information the national tourist board site [Go Dominican Republic](https://www.godominicanrepublic.com/) is a reasonable starting point. Spanish is the language, though English is widely spoken in the resorts, and Santo Domingo — the colonial capital — is the historic day trip when you want more than the beach.
