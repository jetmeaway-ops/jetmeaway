## What to See in Buenos Aires

Start with the **Recoleta Cemetery**, a walled city of marble mausoleums and cypress alleys in the middle of the smartest barrio, where **Eva Perón's grave** draws a steady queue and the rest of Argentine history is filed away in the surrounding streets of tombs. It is the single most atmospheric hour in the city, and the neighbourhood around it — cafés, galleries, the weekend craft fair on the green — is worth the rest of the afternoon.

**La Boca** and the painted **Caminito** street are the postcard image of Buenos Aires: corrugated-iron houses in primary colours, tango dancers performing for tips, and **La Bombonera**, Boca Juniors' famously steep stadium, a few blocks away. Be honest with yourself about it: go in daylight, stay on the tourist streets around Caminito and the ground, and take a taxi or app ride in and out rather than walking in from surrounding blocks. Done that way it is a good, cheerful half-day.

**San Telmo** is the oldest barrio and the best one to wander: cobbled streets, antique shops, faded grand doorways, and on Sundays a long **antiques market** down Defensa street with tango danced in Plaza Dorrego. Just north, the **Plaza de Mayo** is the political heart of the country, with the pink **Casa Rosada** on one side, the colonial Cabildo on another, and the Metropolitan Cathedral on a third.

The **Teatro Colón** is the cultural set piece — one of the world's great auditoriums, ranked with Vienna and Milan for its acoustics, open for daytime guided tours as well as a full opera, ballet and concert season. Nearby on Avenida Santa Fe, the **Ateneo Grand Splendid** bookshop occupies a converted 1919 theatre, with shelves in the boxes and a café on the stage; it regularly appears on lists of the most beautiful bookshops in the world, and it is free to walk in.

**Puerto Madero**'s waterfront is the modern face of the city: converted brick warehouses, glass towers, the white **Puente de la Mujer** footbridge and the Costanera Sur ecological reserve at the far end, which is the best place in the city for a long flat walk. Out in Palermo, the **MALBA** museum holds the strongest collection of twentieth-century Latin American art in the country, and the **Bosques de Palermo** parks — lakes, a rose garden, a Japanese garden — are where Buenos Aires goes to run, cycle and drink mate on a Sunday.

Then there is football, which is closer to religion here: **La Bombonera** in La Boca and the **Monumental** in Núñez are two of the great grounds of the world game, both offering stadium tours between matches. And above all there is **tango** — polished dinner shows for visitors, and milongas, the social dance halls where locals actually go, several of which run beginner classes before the dancing starts. Pair it with the other national obsession, the **steak-and-Malbec** culture of the parrilla, and you have the two things people fly 7,000 miles for.

## Where to Stay in Buenos Aires: Choosing Your Base

**Palermo** — specifically Palermo Soho and Palermo Hollywood — is the usual first choice and rarely the wrong one. Low-rise, tree-lined and dense with restaurants, wine bars, boutiques and late-night places, with the big parks and MALBA on the doorstep. It is the most fun place to sleep in the city; the trade-off is that the historic centre is a Subte ride away.

**Recoleta** is the elegant option: wide avenues, French-influenced apartment blocks, embassies, the cemetery, and streets that feel comfortable to walk in the evening. It sits neatly between Palermo and the centre, and it has a surprising number of small budget guesthouses hidden between the grand blocks. Quieter than Palermo at night, which suits some people exactly.

**Puerto Madero** is where the modern waterfront hotels are — glass towers, river views, safe and calm, and pleasant for an evening walk along the docks. The honest catch is that it feels a little corporate and has less street life than the older barrios, so you will travel for dinner rather than step out into it.

**San Telmo** gives you the most character per pound: cobbles, antiques, colonial buildings, tango in the squares, the Sunday market, and prices well below Palermo. It is a lived-in, working neighbourhood rather than a polished one, so apply the usual city precautions and take a ride rather than walking long distances late.

**The Microcentro** is the cheapest and best-connected base — the biggest cluster of budget hotels, every Subte line, and Plaza de Mayo, the Obelisco and the Teatro Colón within walking distance. The honest downside is that it is an office district: lively by day, and noticeably quiet and less pleasant at night once the offices empty. Perfect for a short, sightseeing-heavy trip on a tight budget; less good for a week.

## Money in Argentina — What UK Visitors Actually Need to Know

This is the one section of this guide we would ask you not to skim, because Argentina does not behave like most destinations.

Argentina has lived through **years of high inflation**, and at various points it has operated **more than one exchange rate at the same time**. The practical consequence for a visitor is straightforward: peso prices move fast, and the relationship between the pound and the peso can shift meaningfully over the course of a few months. A restaurant price, a taxi fare or a hotel rate quoted in an article — including the pound figures in this guide, which are conversions of live rates pulled while writing — should be read as **indicative only**, not as a quote you can hold anyone to.

What that means in practice, without any of it being financial advice:

- **Check the current rate shortly before you travel**, not months in advance, and check it again once you are there.
- **Check the current guidance on the best way to pay** — the rules around foreign cards, currency and how tourists are charged in Argentina have changed more than once in recent years, so look for up-to-date information rather than relying on a blog post or a friend's trip from a couple of years ago.
- **Carry some cash as well as cards.** Small cafés, kiosks, markets, taxis and neighbourhood shops may be cash-only or prefer it, and a card that works fine in a Palermo restaurant may not work at a San Telmo market stall.
- **Do not budget off an old number.** If a figure you have seen is more than a few months old, assume it is out of date.

## Staying Safe in Buenos Aires — Honest, Practical Advice

Buenos Aires deserves a straight answer rather than either scaremongering or a shrug. Millions of people visit every year without trouble, and by the standards of large Latin American cities it is a comfortable place to walk around. It does, however, have a real petty-crime problem, and visitors who look wealthy and distracted are the obvious targets.

The precautions are the ordinary big-city ones and they work. Use **app-based rides or radio taxis at night** rather than walking unfamiliar streets, especially between neighbourhoods. Keep your **phone out of sight** instead of navigating with it in your hand at a junction — phone snatching is the most common problem tourists report. Carry the card and the cash you need for the day and leave the rest, plus your passport, in the hotel safe. Do not leave a bag on the back of a chair or on the floor of a café. Take particular care around **Retiro** station and the streets behind it, and in **La Boca**, where the standing advice is to visit in daylight and stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito and La Bombonera.

Before you book, read the UK government's current [foreign travel advice for Argentina](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/argentina), which covers safety, entry requirements, health and money, and check it again in the week before you fly. For attractions, festivals, opening hours and travel around the rest of the country, Argentina's official tourism site [Visit Argentina](https://www.argentina.travel/) is the reliable starting point.
