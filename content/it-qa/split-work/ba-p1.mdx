  - q: "When is the best time to visit Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Argentina is in the southern hemisphere, so the seasons are reversed. Summer runs December to February and is hot and humid, with the city emptying out in January as locals head for the coast. Winter, June to August, is cool and grey but rarely severe. The loveliest windows are spring, October and November, when the jacaranda trees flower across Recoleta and Palermo, and autumn, March to May, when the weather is mild and the city is fully back at work. Those two shoulder seasons also tend to be the best value."
  - q: "Is Buenos Aires safe for tourists?"

Keep your phone out of sight rather than navigating with it in your hand, carry only the card and cash you need, and use app-based rides or radio taxis after dark instead of walking unfamiliar streets. Be particularly alert around Retiro station and its surroundings, and in La Boca, where the advice is to visit in daylight and stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito. Check the UK government's current travel advice for Argentina before you go."
  - q: "Which neighbourhood should I stay in in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "For a first visit, Palermo or Recoleta. Palermo — especially Palermo Soho and Palermo Hollywood — has the restaurants, the boutiques, the parks and the nightlife, and is the usual first choice. Recoleta is more elegant and residential, with leafy streets, the famous cemetery and an easy walk to the centre. Puerto Madero suits travellers who want modern waterfront hotels and quiet evenings, San Telmo suits those who want bohemian character and lower prices, and the Microcentro is the cheapest and best connected but noticeably quiet and less pleasant after office hours."
  - q: "Is Palermo a good place to stay?"
    a: "For most visitors, yes — it is the barrio people mean when they say Buenos Aires feels like a European city with better weather. Palermo Soho is low-rise, tree-lined and packed with restaurants, wine bars and independent shops around Plaza Serrano; Palermo Hollywood next door is where the production companies and the late-night restaurants are. The huge Bosques de Palermo parks, the rose garden and the MALBA art museum are all within walking or short-ride distance. The trade-off is that you are a Subte ride or a longer walk from the historic centre and Plaza de Mayo."
  - q: "Is Recoleta a good base?"
    a: "Recoleta is the most classically elegant part of the city: wide avenues, French-influenced apartment blocks, embassies, cafés and the Recoleta Cemetery with Eva Perón's grave. It is generally considered one of the safest and most pleasant areas to walk in the evening, it is well placed between Palermo and the centre, and it has a good spread of hotels from grand 5★ names down to small budget guesthouses. It is quieter than Palermo at night, which is a plus or a minus depending on what you came for."
  - q: "Is San Telmo a good place to stay and is it safe?"
    a: "San Telmo is the oldest and most atmospheric barrio — cobbled streets, colonial buildings, antique shops, tango in the squares and the big Sunday market along Defensa. It is also the best value for character, with guesthouses and small hotels well below Palermo prices. It is a working, lived-in neighbourhood rather than a polished one, so apply normal city-centre precautions, keep valuables out of sight and take a ride rather than walking long distances late at night. Most visitors have a very good time there."
  - q: "Is Puerto Madero worth staying in?"
    a: "Puerto Madero is the redeveloped dockland strip along the river, and it is where most of the city's modern glass-tower hotels are. It is safe, quiet, spacious and pleasant for an evening walk along the water past the Puente de la Mujer footbridge, with the Costanera Sur nature reserve at the far end. The honest catch is that it is a bit corporate and has less street life than the older barrios — good for a comfortable, low-hassle stay, less good if you want to step out of the door into a neighbourhood."
  - q: "Is the Microcentro a good place to stay?"
    a: "It depends what you want. The Microcentro — the grid around Florida street, Avenida Corrientes and Plaza de Mayo — has the densest cluster of cheap hotels in the city, the best Subte connections, and everything historic within walking distance. The catch is that it is an office district: it is busy and noisy by day and empties out in the evening, which makes some streets feel bleak after dark. If your priority is the lowest price and the shortest commute to the sights, it works. If you want to eat and drink where you sleep, stay in Palermo or San Telmo."
  - q: "Where should I stay in Buenos Aires on a budget?"
    a: "The Microcentro, Congreso, Monserrat and San Telmo give the best combination of low prices and walkable access to the sights — this is where £21 to £45 a night buys a real hotel room rather than a compromise. Recoleta also has a surprising number of small budget guesthouses tucked between the grand blocks, which is unusual for a neighbourhood that expensive. Palermo is the priciest of the popular areas, though even there mid-range rates are low by western European standards."
  - q: "How do I see real tango in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "There are two versions and they are not the same thing. Dinner-and-show tango houses are polished, professional and aimed at visitors — they are genuinely good entertainment and worth doing once. Milongas are the social dance halls where locals actually go: you pay a small entry, sit at a table, and watch or dance in a room with its own etiquette and codes. Several run beginner classes before the dancing starts, which is the friendliest way in. You will also see street tango in Plaza Dorrego in San Telmo on a Sunday and around Caminito in La Boca — that is performance for tips, but it is free and it is fun."
  - q: "What time do people eat dinner in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Late. Argentines commonly sit down to dinner at 9pm or 10pm and later still at weekends, and many kitchens do not get busy until well after that. Restaurants that open at 8pm will be almost empty. Plan for a big late lunch, a coffee and a medialuna in the afternoon, and dinner at a properly Argentine hour — or book the earliest slot at a restaurant used to visitors. Sunday lunch, by contrast, is a long and important meal."
  - q: "What should I eat in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Beef, first of all. A parrilla is a grill restaurant, and the ritual is a shared cut cooked over wood or charcoal, chimichurri on the side, and a bottle of Malbec from Mendoza. Beyond steak, look for empanadas, milanesa, provoleta, and the Italian-Argentine cooking that came with a century of immigration — the pizza and pasta here are their own tradition, not an imitation. For breakfast, medialunas with coffee. For anything sweet, dulce de leche, in and on everything. Vegetarians are far better served than the city's reputation suggests, especially in Palermo."
  - q: "Do I need to speak Spanish in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "It helps a lot. English is spoken at the bigger hotels and in tourist-facing restaurants, but far less widely than in western Europe and hardly at all with taxi drivers, in neighbourhood cafés or at ticket windows. Argentine Spanish also sounds different from the Spanish taught in UK schools — vos instead of tú, and a distinctive sh sound where you expect a y. Basic courtesy phrases and an offline translation app will carry you through comfortably, and any attempt at Spanish is received warmly."
  - q: "Is the Subte metro useful, and what about taxis?"
    a: "Yes. The Subte is cheap, fast and covers the routes visitors actually use, running out from the centre along several lines — Line A, opened in 1913, was the first underground railway in Latin America and is worth riding for its own sake. You need a rechargeable travel card, sold and topped up at stations, and the same card works on the buses. It stops running late in the evening, so for nights out most people switch to a radio taxi or an app-based ride, which is also the safer default after dark."
  - q: "Can I drink the tap water in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Tap water in the city is generally treated and considered drinkable, and many residents drink it straight from the tap. Bottled water is nonetheless very common and cheap, and plenty of visitors stick to it out of habit or because they prefer the taste. If you have a sensitive stomach or you are travelling outside the city into rural Argentina, bottled or filtered is the cautious choice."
  - q: "How many days do you need in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Three to four full days covers the city properly: one for the historic centre, Plaza de Mayo, the Casa Rosada and the Ateneo Grand Splendid, one for Recoleta and its cemetery plus the MALBA and the Palermo parks, one for San Telmo and La Boca, and a fourth for tango, a football match or simply eating. Add a fifth if you want a day trip to the Tigre delta or across the river to Colonia in Uruguay. Buenos Aires also rewards doing less — long lunches and café afternoons are the point, not a failure of planning."
  - q: "Is La Boca worth visiting and is it safe?"
    a: "Caminito, the short painted street of corrugated-iron houses in La Boca, is genuinely worth seeing, and La Bombonera stadium is a few blocks away. The honest advice, which locals give as readily as guidebooks do, is to go in daylight, stay on the main tourist streets around Caminito and the stadium, and take a taxi or app ride in and out rather than wandering in on foot from surrounding blocks. Treated that way it is a straightforward and enjoyable half-day."
  - q: "Can I do a day trip from Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Two obvious ones. Tigre, on the Paraná delta about an hour north by train, is a maze of river channels, stilt houses and boat services, and makes an easy half or full day out of the city. Colonia del Sacramento, across the Río de la Plata in Uruguay, is a UNESCO-listed colonial old town reachable by ferry in around an hour on the fast services — remember it is a different country, so take your passport and check entry requirements before you go."
  - q: "Can I visit the Teatro Colón?"
    a: "Yes, and you should. The Teatro Colón is widely rated among the finest opera houses in the world for its acoustics, and it runs guided tours through the horseshoe auditorium, the foyers and the Golden Hall during the day as well as a full opera, ballet and concert programme. Tours are popular and slots are timed, so book ahead rather than turning up. If your dates allow, seeing an actual performance — even from a cheap upper seat — is one of the best-value cultural nights in the city."
  - q: "Can I see a football match in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Buenos Aires is one of the great football cities, and La Bombonera in La Boca and the Monumental in Núñez are its two most famous grounds. Tickets for ordinary league fixtures can be hard for visitors to buy directly because of membership systems, so many people go through an organised hospitality or tour operator, which also handles transport to and from the ground. Stadium tours are available at both when there is no match on and are a good fallback."
  - q: "Are hotel breakfasts included in Buenos Aires?"
    a: "Often, but not always. Argentine hotel breakfast is usually a modest continental spread — coffee, medialunas, bread, jam, fruit and sometimes eggs — rather than a full buffet, and it is included at many mid-range and even budget properties. At the cheapest central hotels and some apartment-style stays it is either extra or absent. Check what is included on the room page before you book; with cafés on almost every corner, skipping it and going out for coffee and medialunas is no hardship."
